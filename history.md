# Research Foundation for a Multi-Year Self-Study Curriculum in Global Human History

## TL;DR
- A connective, seven-era global backbone is fully supported by current resources: a strong free spine (OpenStax *World History* Vols. 1–2, 2023, CC-BY) plus a "2-in-1" narrative-and-sources anchor (Strayer & Nelson, *Ways of the World*), with era-specific scholarly surveys (Van De Mieroop, Pomeroy, Boatwright, Ebrey, Hodgson, Hansen, Bayly/Osterhammel) layered on top; primary sources are largely covered by open repositories (Perseus/Scaife, Fordham Internet History Sourcebooks) supplemented by a few in-copyright critical translations (Andrew George's Gilgamesh; the Landmark Herodotus/Thucydides).
- The historiography strand has a clear standard (Tosh, *The Pursuit of History*, 7th ed. 2022; Carr; Bloch), and a genuine, mature Python-friendly digital-history path exists via *The Programming Historian* (NetworkX, spaCy, stylometry) — realistic for hands-on projects, though large clean historical datasets are the binding constraint.
- On the calibration questions: the **Great Divergence** is CONTESTED but converging on an 18th-century divergence date (with Pomeranz himself having retreated from full-parity-to-1800); the **Axial Age** is HYPE-WATCH/CONTESTED (widely used heuristic, disputed as a causal phenomenon, now with an empirical rebuttal using Seshat data); "1492 as the single biggest discontinuity" is CONTESTED; periodization boundaries are OPEN by construction.

## Key Findings

### The overall architecture is well-supported by current resources
A connective (not region-by-region) world history is exactly what the current generation of survey textbooks is built to deliver. Robert Strayer & Eric Nelson's *Ways of the World* is explicitly organized around "context, change, comparison, and connection," and its "with Sources" versions bundle primary-source dossiers after each chapter — ideal for an autodidact who wants narrative plus documents in one place. For a fully free spine, OpenStax's two-volume *World History* (2023, CC-BY) is peer-reviewed, genuinely global, and downloadable as PDF/web.

### Historiography & method — the standard is settled
- **John Tosh, *The Pursuit of History*** — the standard "doing history" text. Current edition is the **7th (Routledge, 2022)**; the widely circulated 6th ed. is 2015. VERIFIED-CURRENT. PAYWALLED.
- **E. H. Carr, *What Is History?*** (1961; 2nd ed. 1987; Penguin reissue with Richard J. Evans introduction, 2018). A foundational classic — CONTESTED in its relativism (Geoffrey Elton's *The Practice of History*, 1967, is the classic rebuttal). PAYWALLED (cheap).
- **Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft*** (Fr. *Apologie pour l'histoire ou Métier d'historien*, 1949; English 1953, trans. Peter Putnam, Knopf). Classic on source criticism. PAYWALLED.
- Modern equivalents to pair: **Richard J. Evans, *In Defence of History*** (1997); **John Lewis Gaddis, *The Landscape of History*** (2002); **Ernst Breisach, *Historiography: Ancient, Medieval, and Modern*** (3rd ed. 2007) for the long view.

### Open primary-source repositories (all FREE/OPEN)
- **Internet History Sourcebooks Project (Fordham University)**, ed. Paul Halsall — Ancient/Medieval/Modern plus African, East Asian, Indian, Islamic, Jewish, Science, Women's sourcebooks. Status: online and ongoing. Caveat: many texts are copyright-expired 19th/early-20th-century translations, so quality varies and they are not always the best modern translation — good for extracts, not always for close reading.
- **Perseus Digital Library (Tufts)** — open-access Greek/Latin texts with translations. The newer **Scaife Viewer** hosts **2,699 works in 3,860 editions and translations (1,951 in Greek, 668 in Latin), ~85.9 million words**, per the Scaife Viewer homepage. Caveat: English translations are often old (public-domain Loeb/others).
- **Project Gutenberg**, **MIT Classics (Internet Classics Archive)**, and **OpenStax/LibreTexts** round out the free layer.

### The calibration questions
- **Great Divergence — CONTESTED, converging.** The "California School" (Pomeranz, Wong, Frank, Goldstone) argued Europe and the most advanced parts of Asia were roughly comparable until ~1800, with divergence late and contingent (coal + colonies, per Pomeranz specifically). Stephen Broadberry's "Accounting for the Great Divergence" (CEPR, 2021) states that "new historical national accounting data suggest that the Great Divergence dates from the 18th rather than the 19th century, a view that has recently been endorsed by Pomeranz (2011, 2017)"; his companion paper is titled "Dating the Great Divergence: The Eighteenth Century as an Emerging Consensus." Critically, Broadberry notes that "Pomeranz (2011) now accepts that his earlier claim of China on a par with Europe as late as 1800 was exaggerated, and he now settles for the earlier date of 1700" — with NW Europe pulling ahead of the Yangzi Delta after 1700, rooted in trends beginning as early as the 14th century. State of play: the late-divergence timing is largely accepted; strict full parity to 1800 and the "coal-and-colonies" mechanism are not.
- **Axial Age — HYPE-WATCH / CONTESTED.** Jaspers's 1949 thesis (parallel breakthroughs ~800–200 BCE in China, India, Persia, Israel, Greece) remains an influential heuristic embraced by sociologists (Eisenstadt, Bellah) and in Big History. But historians widely doubt it as a coherent causal phenomenon: critics note the lack of a common denominator across regions, no clean discontinuity with "pre-axial" periods, and arbitrary exclusions (Jesus, Muhammad, Akhenaten). The skeptical historiography is John D. Boy & John Torpey, "Inventing the Axial Age: The Origins and Uses of a Historical Concept," *Theory and Society* 42, no. 3 (2013): 241–259; the empirical rebuttal is Daniel Mullins, Daniel Hoyer, Christina Collins, et al., "A Systematic Assessment of 'Axial Age' Proposals Using Global Comparative Historical Evidence," *American Sociological Review* (2018), using Seshat data. Present it as a useful lens, flagged as disputed.
- **1492 / Columbian Exchange as "single biggest discontinuity" — CONTESTED.** Alfred Crosby's *The Columbian Exchange* (1972) and *Ecological Imperialism* (1986) founded environmental history and are genuinely seminal. Mackenzie Cooley et al., "A Patchwork Columbian Exchange: Ecological Imperialism, Animals, and the Relaciones Geográficas of New Spain," *Renaissance Quarterly* 78, no. 4 (Winter 2025), "reframes ecological imperialism as contingent and uneven, foregrounding variation, resistance, and the dynamism of early modern nature" — a "patchwork" rather than a uniform biological juggernaut. "Biggest discontinuity" is a defensible but contested ranking (rivals: Neolithic Revolution, Industrial Revolution).
- **Periodization boundaries — OPEN by construction.** All seven era-boundaries are heuristics; the learner should treat them as scaffolding, not natural kinds.

### Quantitative / digital history — mature enough for real projects
- ***The Programming Historian*** (peer-reviewed, free) is the single best on-ramp: lessons in **NetworkX** (network analysis), **spaCy** (NER), stylometry with R, corpus analysis, and text similarity (city-block/Euclidean/cosine via SciPy). Realistic for a strong-Python learner.
- Tools: **Gephi** (network viz), **Voyant** (browser text analysis), **MALLET** (topic modeling), **Palladio** (mapping/networks).
- Datasets/corpora: **Perseus/Scaife** (classical corpus, ~85.9M words — 40.9M Greek, 16.4M Latin, with the remainder English/other translations); **Seshat: Global History Databank** (est. 2011), which has coded 414 societies across 30 world regions on roughly 1,500 variables spanning ~10,000 years (Turchin et al., *PNAS* 2018, doi:10.1073/pnas.1708800115); **Clio-Infra** and the **Maddison Project Database** (historical GDP/wages for cliometrics); **Pleiades** (ancient places, for GIS mapping).
- Honest assessment: the tooling is mature and well-documented; the binding constraint is clean, well-structured historical datasets. Text mining of the classical corpus, network analysis of correspondence/trade, cliometric interrogation of Maddison/Clio-Infra, and mapping are the most tractable hands-on projects.

### Free courseware (assess honestly)
- **Open Yale Courses** — full video + transcripts, CC-licensed, but the project is largely inactive (content frozen ~2011; since 2022 some professors publish updated Yale courses on other platforms such as Coursetexts). Directly relevant: Freedman, *The Early Middle Ages, 284–1000* (HIST 210); Snowden, *Epidemics and Western Society Since 1600* (HIST 234); Merriman, *European Civilization 1648–1945*. Excellent and free; not updated.
- **MIT OpenCourseWare** — syllabi, readings, some lecture notes (fewer full video lecture series in history than Yale).
- **OER Project** (Big History Project / World History Project) — free, registration-based, strong for connective world-history framing (aimed at secondary/AP but usable). Rankin & Weise, *World History Since 1500: An Open and Free Textbook* (2022, CC-BY) is a downloadable OER companion.

## Details — Era by Era

### Era 1 — Origins & the Neolithic Revolution (deep past–~3000 BCE)
- **Standard survey / textbook:** Chris Scarre, ed., ***The Human Past: World Prehistory and the Development of Human Societies*** (Thames & Hudson) — the standard prehistory survey used in universities (a later edition exists; confirm newest printing). For human evolution specifically, pair with a current paleoanthropology text. Free alternative: OpenStax *World History* Vol. 1, Ch. 1–2; the OER "World History to 1500" Pressbooks chapters.
- **Primary sources:** By definition pre-textual; "sources" are archaeological/material. Use site reports and syntheses (Çatalhöyük, Göbekli Tepe) rather than written primaries.
- **Seminal secondary works:**
  - James C. Scott, ***Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States*** (Yale, 2017) — revisionist, argues early states/grain agriculture were not simple "progress." CONTESTED, influential.
  - Steven Mithen, ***After the Ice: A Global Human History 20,000–5,000 BC*** (2003).
  - David Graeber & David Wengrow, ***The Dawn of Everything*** (2021) — high-profile challenge to linear narratives; HYPE-WATCH (praised and heavily criticized by specialists). List as provocative, not settled.
  - Jared Diamond, ***Guns, Germs, and Steel*** (1997) — POPULAR, geographic-determinist; useful as a foil, widely critiqued by academic historians. Label clearly as pop-history.
  - Robert C. Allen, "The Neolithic Revolution in the Middle East" (*Economic History Review*, 2024) — recent economic-history synthesis, good for the STEM learner.

### Era 2 — First civilizations / Bronze Age (~3000–1200 BCE)
- **Standard survey:** **Marc Van De Mieroop, *A History of the Ancient Near East, ca. 3000–323 BC*** — current edition is the **4th (Wiley-Blackwell, 2023)**; the 3rd (2015) remains common. VERIFIED-CURRENT. PAYWALLED. For Egypt: Van De Mieroop, ***A History of Ancient Egypt*** (Wiley-Blackwell, 2nd ed. 2021). Free alternative: OpenStax Vol. 1; Fordham Ancient Sourcebook.
- **Primary sources & best translations:**
  - ***The Epic of Gilgamesh*** — standard scholarly translation: **Andrew George, Penguin Classics** (1999; revised 2nd ed. 2020). George's two-volume *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic* (Oxford, 2003) is the definitive critical edition. FREE alternative: Maureen Gallery Kovacs (Stanford, 1989), electronic edition free at ancienttexts.org (omits Tablet XII); public-domain R. Campbell Thompson (1928).
  - **Code of Hammurabi**, Egyptian **Pyramid Texts** / *Book of the Dead*, and Mesopotamian letters — extracts free via Fordham; Martha Roth, *Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor* (SBL, 2nd ed. 1997) is the scholarly standard for legal texts (PAYWALLED).
- **Seminal secondary works:** Amanda Podany, ***Brotherhood of Kings*** (Oxford, 2010) on Bronze Age diplomacy — excellent for the connective theme; Eric Cline, ***1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed*** (Princeton, rev. ed. 2021) on the Bronze Age collapse (accessible, well-regarded).

### Era 3 — Classical / Axial Age (~800 BCE–500 CE)
- **Standard surveys:**
  - Greece: **Pomeroy, Burstein, Donlan, Roberts, Tandy & Tsouvala, *Ancient Greece: A Political, Social, and Cultural History*** — current edition **4th (Oxford, 2017)**. Scholarly-standard undergraduate survey. PAYWALLED.
  - Rome: **Boatwright, Gargola, Lenski & Talbert, *The Romans: From Village to Empire*** — **2nd ed. (Oxford, 2012)**; the scholarly-standard textbook. PAYWALLED. Popular-but-authoritative complement: **Mary Beard, *SPQR*** (Profile/Liveright, 2015) — POPULAR, ends at 212 CE.
  - China (Han): use Ebrey (below) plus Mark Edward Lewis, *The Early Chinese Empires: Qin and Han* (Harvard, 2007).
  - India (Mauryan/Gupta): Romila Thapar, *Early India: From the Origins to AD 1300* (rev. ed., Penguin/California, 2004) — standard.
  - Persia (Achaemenid): Pierre Briant, *From Cyrus to Alexander* (Eisenbrauns, 2002) — the definitive scholarly account.
- **Primary sources & best editions:**
  - **Herodotus, *The Histories*** and **Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*** — best reading editions: the **Landmark** series ed. Robert B. Strassler (*Landmark Thucydides*, Free Press 1996, Richard Crawley trans., intro. Victor Davis Hanson; *Landmark Herodotus*, Pantheon/Anchor 2007, Andrea L. Purvis trans., intro. Rosalind Thomas), prized for maps, marginal notes, and appendices by leading classicists. Base texts FREE on Perseus (public-domain translations, without Landmark apparatus).
  - Plato, Aristotle, the Confucian *Analects*, *Bhagavad Gita*, *Dao De Jing*, New Testament — free in translation via Perseus / MIT Classics / Fordham; for scholarly translations use Oxford World's Classics / Penguin Classics.
- **Seminal secondary works:** Peter Brown, ***The World of Late Antiquity*** (1971) — field-defining; Robert Bellah, *Religion in Human Evolution* (Harvard, 2011) for the Axial Age (sympathetic); Boy & Torpey (2013) and Mullins et al. (2018) for the skeptical/empirical historiography.

### Era 4 — Postclassical world (~500–1450)
- **Standard surveys:**
  - Islamic world: **Marshall Hodgson, *The Venture of Islam*** (3 vols., Chicago, 1974) — the magisterial standard, though dense and dated in places; pair with **Hugh Kennedy, *The Prophet and the Age of the Caliphates*** (3rd ed., Routledge, 2016; confirm newest printing) as a more current survey, and Chase Robinson / Michael Cook for current scholarship.
  - China (Tang/Song): **Patricia Buckley Ebrey, *The Cambridge Illustrated History of China*** — current **3rd ed. (Cambridge, 2023)**, with fuller environmental/global-context coverage. VERIFIED-CURRENT. PAYWALLED. Pair with Dieter Kuhn, *The Age of Confucian Rule: The Song Transformation* (Harvard, 2009).
  - Mongols/steppe: see deep-dive below.
- **Primary sources:** Fordham Medieval + Islamic + East Asian Sourcebooks (FREE); Ibn Battuta, *The Travels* (trans. H.A.R. Gibb, Hakluyt Society; abridged Tim Mackintosh-Smith, Picador); Marco Polo, *The Travels* (Penguin, trans. Ronald Latham). The ***Secret History of the Mongols*** — scholarly translation by Igor de Rachewiltz (Brill, 2004; an open-access edition of the abridged version was released via Western Washington University's *Studies in Central & East Asian Religions* in 2015).
- **Seminal secondary works:** **Janet Abu-Lughod, *Before European Hegemony: The World System A.D. 1250–1350*** (Oxford, 1989) — field-defining for the connective/trade theme; **Valerie Hansen, *The Silk Road: A New History*** (Oxford, 2012; "with Documents" ed. 2016/2017, adds primary sources + a Mongol-era chapter). For Indian Ocean trade: **Michael N. Pearson, *The Indian Ocean*** (Routledge, 2003) — the standard single-volume survey; classic alternative K. N. Chaudhuri, *Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean* (Cambridge, 1985).

### Era 5 — Early modern / first globalization (~1450–1750)
- **Standard survey:** OpenStax Vol. 2 (free) for the spine; John Darwin, *After Tamerlane: The Global History of Empire since 1405* (Bloomsbury, 2007) for connective framing. Gunpowder empires: Douglas Streusand, *Islamic Gunpowder Empires* (Westview, 2011).
- **Primary sources:** Fordham Modern/Early Modern Sourcebook (FREE); Columbus's letters, Bernal Díaz, *The Conquest of New Spain* (Penguin); Matteo Ricci journals; Scientific Revolution texts (Galileo, Newton) via public-domain editions.
- **Seminal secondary works:** **Alfred Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange*** (1972; 30th-anniv. ed. 2003) and ***Ecological Imperialism*** (Cambridge, 1986); **Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*** (Princeton, 2000) — the pivot of the divergence debate; Charles Mann, *1493* (2011) — POPULAR synthesis of Crosby's ideas.
- **Scientific Revolution:** **Steven Shapin, *The Scientific Revolution*** (Chicago, 1996) — standard interpretive short book; **David Wootton, *The Invention of Science*** (2015) — major recent synthesis (CONTESTED).

### Era 6 — Modern revolutions / long 19th century (~1750–1914)
- **Standard surveys (both scholarly-standard global histories):** **C. A. Bayly, *The Birth of the Modern World, 1780–1914*** (Blackwell, 2004) and **Jürgen Osterhammel, *The Transformation of the World: A Global History of the Nineteenth Century*** (Princeton, 2014; trans. Patrick Camiller; German orig. 2009). Both are connective/global. PAYWALLED.
- **Primary sources:** Fordham Modern Sourcebook (FREE) — Declarations (American, French, Haitian), the *Communist Manifesto*, imperial-era documents.
- **Seminal secondary works:** Eric Hobsbawm's trilogy — ***The Age of Revolution 1789–1848***, ***The Age of Capital 1848–1875***, ***The Age of Empire 1875–1914*** (Marxist, classic); on the Industrial Revolution and the divergence, Robert C. Allen, *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (Cambridge, 2009) — strong for the STEM/economic-history learner.

### Era 7 — Contemporary (1914–present)
- **Standard surveys:** **Eric Hobsbawm, *The Age of Extremes: The Short Twentieth Century, 1914–1991*** (1994; Vintage US paperback 1996) — classic synthesis (Marxist perspective); **Odd Arne Westad, *The Cold War: A World History*** (Basic Books/Allen Lane, 2017) — leading recent global synthesis by a Bancroft Prize–winning Cold War scholar. For decolonization: Westad, *The Global Cold War* (Cambridge, 2005). Free spine: OpenStax Vol. 2.
- **Primary sources:** Fordham Modern Sourcebook; UN/League of Nations archives; the Cold War International History Project (Wilson Center) digital archive (FREE).
- **Seminal secondary works:** Tony Judt, *Postwar: A History of Europe Since 1945* (2005) — standard for Europe; on the digital age, treat carefully (few settled syntheses; HYPE-WATCH for "revolution" framing).

- **(a) Mesopotamia & Egypt:** Van De Mieroop, *A History of the Ancient Near East* (4th ed., 2023) + *A History of Ancient Egypt* (2nd ed., 2021); Podany, *Brotherhood of Kings*.
- **(b) Greco-Roman Mediterranean:** Pomeroy et al. (4th ed., 2017); Boatwright et al. (2nd ed., 2012); Beard, *SPQR* (popular). Primaries via Landmark + Perseus.
- **(c) China:** Ebrey, *Cambridge Illustrated History of China* (3rd ed., 2023); Lewis's Harvard "History of Imperial China" series; primaries via Ebrey's *Chinese Civilization: A Sourcebook*.
- **(d) Islamic world:** Hodgson, *The Venture of Islam* (3 vols., 1974); Kennedy, *The Prophet and the Age of the Caliphates* (3rd ed., 2016) as a more current survey.
- **(e) Central Asian steppe / Mongols:** **Jack Weatherford, *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World*** (2004) — POPULAR/bestseller, engaging but revisionist and criticized for overstatement; balance with **Timothy May, *The Mongol Empire*** (Edinburgh, 2018) and **Paul Ratchnevsky, *Genghis Khan: His Life and Legacy*** (scholarly). Primary source: de Rachewiltz's *Secret History of the Mongols*.
- **(f) Atlantic world / early-modern globalization:** Crosby (both); Pomeranz; John Thornton, *Africa and Africans in the Making of the Atlantic World, 1400–1800* (Cambridge, 2nd ed. 1998).

## Thematic Lenses
- **(i) Economic/material history:** Robert C. Allen, *Global Economic History: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford, 2011) — excellent compact entry for the STEM learner; Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*.
- **(ii) History of ideas & religion:** Peter Brown, *The World of Late Antiquity*; Charles Taylor, *A Secular Age* (2007, advanced); Robert Bellah, *Religion in Human Evolution*.
- **(iii) State formation & political history:** Francis Fukuyama, *The Origins of Political Order* (2011) — synthetic, POPULAR-scholarly; James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998) and *Against the Grain* (2017); Charles Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European States* (1990).
- **(iv) Environmental/disease history:** Crosby (both); William McNeill, *Plagues and Peoples* (1976) — classic; J. R. McNeill, *Something New Under the Sun* (2000) for the 20th century.
- **(v) History of science & technology (learner's priority):** **David Lindberg, *The Beginnings of Western Science*** (Chicago, 2nd ed. 2007) — standard for ancient/medieval; **Steven Shapin, *The Scientific Revolution*** (1996); the **Cambridge History of Science** (8 vols., 2003–2020, Lindberg & Numbers eds.) as reference; on technology, Joel Mokyr, *The Lever of Riches* (1990) and *The Gifts of Athena* (2002) — excellent for a STEM reader.

## Recommendations
1. **Start with the method + spine (Phase 0, ~4–6 weeks).** Read Tosh (7th ed., 2022) and Carr; skim Bloch. Set up the free spine (OpenStax + Fordham + Perseus/Scaife). Benchmark to advance: you can write a 1,000-word source-criticism note on a primary document, correctly distinguishing a fact from an interpretation.
2. **Run the 7 eras connectively, one era per phase.** For each era use: free spine (OpenStax) → scholarly survey (era-specific, above) → 2–3 primary sources (open where possible) → 1 seminal monograph. MVP fast-path: *Ways of the World* (connective narrative + sources) + one primary source + one monograph per era, skipping the era-specific surveys.
3. **Attach one Python/data project every 2 eras**, escalating: (a) text-frequency/stylometry on the Perseus/Scaife classical corpus; (b) network analysis (NetworkX/Gephi) of a trade or correspondence network; (c) cliometric analysis of the Maddison Project / Clio-Infra GDP-and-wage data to interrogate the Great Divergence yourself against Broadberry vs. Pomeranz; (d) GIS mapping with Pleiades; optionally (e) query Seshat for a cross-cultural social-complexity project. Use *The Programming Historian* lessons as scaffolds.
4. **Calibrate as you go.** For each era, write a short "state of the debate" note using the SETTLED/CONTESTED/OPEN/HYPE-WATCH tags. Threshold to change approach: if you find yourself accepting a single grand narrative (Diamond, Weatherford, strong Axial Age) uncritically, deliberately read its best academic critique (e.g., Mullins et al. 2018 for the Axial Age; Broadberry 2021 for the divergence).
5. **Edition discipline.** Always prefer the verified-current editions flagged above (Tosh 7th/2022; Van De Mieroop ANE 4th/2023; Pomeroy 4th/2017; Boatwright 2nd/2012; Ebrey 3rd/2023; George's Gilgamesh rev. 2020). Where you use a free Fordham/Perseus text, note that the translation may be a century old and cross-check against a modern critical edition for anything you cite.

## Caveats
- **Free ≠ current.** Perseus/Scaife and Fordham translations are frequently public-domain and old; excellent for coverage and extracts, weaker for close reading or citation. Modern critical translations (George, Landmark, Roth) are paywalled and, for the Landmark editions, the value-add (maps, notes, appendices) is precisely what the free base texts lack.
- **Popular vs. scholarly.** Diamond (*Guns, Germs, and Steel*), Weatherford (*Genghis Khan*), Graeber & Wengrow (*Dawn of Everything*), and Beard (*SPQR*) are engaging and worth reading, but are popular/trade or contested syntheses — do not mistake them for the scholarly standard.
- **Open Yale Courses is frozen** (~2011) — high quality but not maintained; verify nothing has been superseded.
- **Digital-history reality check:** tooling is mature; the bottleneck is clean historical data. Budget project time for data cleaning, not just analysis. Seshat's coding methodology has itself been debated in the literature — use it critically, not as ground truth.
- **Editions I could not independently verify within budget:** the latest printing of Scarre's *The Human Past* and Kennedy's *The Prophet and the Age of the Caliphates* (3rd ed. reported as 2016); confirm the newest edition before purchase.
