# A Theory-Centric Sociology Curriculum & Reading List (Generalist, ~1–2 Years)
### Consolidated edition — editions, access, and calibration verified September 2026

## TL;DR
- **A theory-first sociology reading program** in 16 sequential modules: orientation → early social thought → the four classical founders (Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel) plus Du Bois → the classical synthesis → contemporary theory (functionalism, symbolic interactionism, Critical Theory/Frankfurt School, Bourdieu, Foucault, structuration/figurational, contemporary critiques) → a compact methods survey → a compact substantive-subfields survey.
- **Calibrated to "broad but shallow."** Educated-generalist, not research-mastery. Primary sources paired with the best modern editions and readers; almost everything read as **selections**, not cover-to-cover. Full arc ≈ 1–2 years part-time; an **MVP fast-path** gets a solid command of the theory spine in a few months.
- **Classical/canonical lens with honest calibration.** Every major school and thesis carries a **SETTLED / CONTESTED / OPEN / HYPE-WATCH** tag. Several "textbook classics" are contested empirically (Weber's Protestant-ethic thesis, the Davis-Moore stratification thesis, Durkheim's original *Suicide* analysis, the strong secularization thesis) and are flagged as such.
- **Frankfurt is your home-turf advantage.** The Institut für Sozialforschung — home of the Frankfurt School — is in your city; Module 9 treats it as a "visit the source" anchor.
- **No forced ML.** Computational touches appear only where sociologically intrinsic (a light social-network exercise in the networks thread) and are clearly optional.
- **All editions and free-access hosts below were verified in September 2026.** The one live watch-item is a new *Capital* translation (Reitter/North, Princeton, 2024) — noted at Module 2.

## How to use this
Each module gives: (1) goals; (2) prerequisites; (3) **PRIMARY** readings with precise citations and a recommended edition/translation, marking full vs. selections; (4) **SUPPLEMENTARY** companions/critiques; (5) an optional **light exercise** (reading/writing, occasionally a small network exploration); (6) time estimate. **Access tags:** FREE (public-domain / open-access) vs. PAYWALLED. **Calibration tags:** SETTLED (enduring canon / broadly accepted) · CONTESTED (actively debated or critiqued) · OPEN (genuinely unresolved) · HYPE-WATCH (culturally influential but empirically shaky / superseded within the discipline).

**A note on "free."** Where a classic is free online, check *which translation*: for Durkheim, Simmel, Mead, Tocqueville, and Weber the free version is an **older public-domain translation**, not the recommended modern one. This is flagged per module and summarized in the Verified Editions & Access table near the end.

**Free-resource backbone (used throughout):**
- **Marxists Internet Archive** — marxists.org. Full texts of Marx & Engels (copyleft) plus a Reference Archive with Simmel, Mead-adjacent material, Gramsci, and the Frankfurt School (Marcuse in full; Adorno/Horkheimer in excerpts). **FREE.**
- **Project Gutenberg** (gutenberg.org) and the **Internet Archive** (archive.org) — public-domain originals and older translations (Du Bois, Tocqueville, Spencer, Comte; older Durkheim/Simmel/Mead/Weber). **FREE.**
- **Brock University "Mead Project"** and the **Robert Alun Jones "Durkheim Pages"** (formerly McMaster) — reputable scholarly text archives. **FREE** (verify specific URLs at point of use; academic archive links move).

**Two anthologies to own (they carry the whole program) — editions verified Sept 2026:**
- Craig Calhoun, Joseph Gerteis, James Moody, Steven Pfaff & Indermohan Virk (eds.), ***Classical Sociological Theory***, **4th ed. (Wiley-Blackwell, 2022; ISBN 9781119527367)** and ***Contemporary Sociological Theory***, **4th ed. (Wiley-Blackwell, 2022; ISBN 9781119527244)**. The standard curated primary-source selections. Supersede the 3rd editions (2012). **PAYWALLED.**
- One narrative companion — pick by taste: **Lewis A. Coser, *Masters of Sociological Thought*** (2nd ed., 1977; kept in print by Waveland Press, 2003 reissue, ISBN 9781577663072; warm, contextual — older editions **FREE** via archive.org); **Randall Collins, *Four Sociological Traditions*** (Oxford, 1994; conflict, Durkheimian, microinteractionist, rational-choice traditions — no newer edition); or **George Ritzer & Jeffrey Stepnisky, *Sociological Theory*, 11th ed. (SAGE, 2021)** — comprehensive textbook (note: the title moved from McGraw-Hill to SAGE at the 10th ed.). **PAYWALLED.**

For substantive breadth (Modules 14–15): **Anthony Giddens & Philip W. Sutton, *Sociology*, 9th ed. (Polity, 2021; ISBN 9781509539222)** — confirmed current, no 10th edition; the standard doorstop reference, dip into chapters rather than reading straight through. **PAYWALLED** (their shorter *Essential Concepts in Sociology* is a lighter alternative).

## DEPENDENCY MAP (the through-lines)
- **Modules 0–1** (orientation + Enlightenment roots) come first and underpin everything.
- **The classical spine (2–5)** — Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel, Du Bois — is the load-bearing core; **Module 6** synthesizes it.
- **Main intellectual through-lines to hold in your head:**
  - **Marx → Gramsci → Frankfurt School (9) → Bourdieu (10)** — the lineage of *domination, ideology, and cultural reproduction*.
  - **Weber → rationalization/bureaucracy → Foucault (11)** — the lineage of *rationality as a cage / disciplinary power*.
  - **Durkheim → functionalism (7) → cultural sociology / Goffman's ritual (8)** — the lineage of *solidarity, the sacred, and social order*.
  - **Simmel + Mead → symbolic interactionism → Goffman/Garfinkel (8)** — the lineage of *micro-interaction and the self*.
  - **The agency/structure and micro/macro debates (12–13)** are where the lineages collide; Bourdieu, Giddens, and Elias are the attempted syntheses.
- **Modules 14 (methods) and 15 (subfields)** are breadth caps applied after the theory core.

### MODULE 0 — Orientation: What Sociology Is
**Goals:** Grasp the "sociological imagination" — seeing private troubles as public issues — and why sociology exists as a distinct discipline.
**Prerequisites:** None.
**PRIMARY:**
- C. Wright Mills, *The Sociological Imagination* (Oxford, 1959) — read Ch. 1 ("The Promise") and the appendix ("On Intellectual Craftsmanship"). **PAYWALLED** (widely excerpted).
- Peter L. Berger, *Invitation to Sociology: A Humanistic Perspective* (Anchor, 1963) — Chs. 1–2. **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** the opening chapter of Giddens & Sutton, *Sociology*, for a map of the field.
**Exercise:** In one page, reframe a personal biographical fact (your career, your city, your migration history) as a "public issue" in Mills's sense.
**Calibration:** The sociological-imagination framing = **SETTLED** (foundational, universally taught).
**Time:** 1 week.

### MODULE 1 — Enlightenment & Early Social Thought
**Goals:** See where sociology came from — the shift from social philosophy to a science of society.
**Prerequisites:** Module 0.
**PRIMARY (selections only):**
- Auguste Comte — selections on positivism and the coining of "sociology" (via the Calhoun *Classical* reader). **PAYWALLED reader** / older texts **FREE** on archive.org.
- Alexis de Tocqueville, *Democracy in America* (1835/1840) — read the chapters on associations, individualism, and the "tyranny of the majority." Recommended modern edition: trans. Harvey C. Mansfield & Delba Winthrop (Univ. of Chicago Press, 2000) — **PAYWALLED**. Free version = older Henry Reeve (later Reeve–Bowen) translation, public domain, on **Project Gutenberg / archive.org — FREE**.
- Herbert Spencer — a short selection on social evolutionism/the organic analogy (via reader), read *critically* as a period piece. **FREE** (archive.org).
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Coser, *Masters of Sociological Thought*, chapters on Comte, Spencer, Tocqueville.
**Exercise:** Note what Comte's "positivism" promised and one reason later sociologists (esp. Weber) rejected the strong version.
**Calibration:** Comtean positivism as a program = **HYPE-WATCH** (historically pivotal, but the strong positivist claim is long superseded); Tocqueville on civil society = **SETTLED** (still generative).
**Time:** 2 weeks.

---

### MODULE 2 — Karl Marx
**Goals:** Historical materialism, alienation, commodity fetishism, class and class conflict, base/superstructure, ideology.
**Prerequisites:** Module 1.
**PRIMARY:** Use **Robert C. Tucker (ed.), *The Marx-Engels Reader*, 2nd ed. (Norton, 1978; ISBN 9780393090406)** as the spine — the standard teaching anthology (verified: no 3rd edition exists). Read:
- *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* — the alienation sections.
- *The German Ideology*, Part I — the materialist conception of history, ideology.
- *Manifesto of the Communist Party* (1848) — in full (short).
- "Preface" to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) — the base/superstructure passage.
- *Capital*, Vol. I — read Ch. 1 §4 ("The Fetishism of Commodities") and the chapter on the working day, as **selections**. Recommended full translation: Ben Fowkes (Penguin/New Left Review, 1976; ISBN 9780140445688) — **PAYWALLED**. Everything above is **FREE** at marxists.org (Manifesto = 1888 Moore/Engels translation; 1844 MSS = Milligan/Progress translation).
- **Translation watch-item:** a new *Capital* Vol. I translation appeared 17 Sept 2024 — Paul Reitter (trans.), Paul North & Paul Reitter (eds.), foreword Wendy Brown (Princeton UP; ISBN 9780691190075), the first new English translation in ~50 years and the only one based on the **2nd German edition (1872), the last revised by Marx himself**. Caveat: it translates a *different source edition* than Fowkes (who follows the 4th German edition with Marx's later French-edition revisions), so the two are **not interchangeable**. Keep Fowkes as the assigned edition; revisit if it becomes the syllabus standard.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Coser or Ritzer chapter on Marx; optionally Peter Singer, *Marx: A Very Short Introduction*.
**Exercise:** Explain commodity fetishism in your own words using a present-day example (a branded consumer good or a financial instrument).
**Calibration:** Alienation, commodity fetishism, class analysis = **SETTLED** as canonical, still-used tools. Strong *economic determinism* (the base rigidly determining the superstructure) = **CONTESTED** (revised by Gramsci and the Frankfurt School). Marx's specific predictions (immiseration, inevitable revolution) = **HYPE-WATCH** (empirically falsified as stated).
**Time:** 3–4 weeks.

### MODULE 3 — Émile Durkheim
**Goals:** Social facts, mechanical vs. organic solidarity, anomie, the sacred/profane, sociology as a science of a reality *sui generis*.
**Prerequisites:** Module 1.
**PRIMARY (selections unless noted):**
- *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) — read the preface and Chs. 1–2 ("What Is a Social Fact?"). Recommended: ed. Steven Lukes, trans. W. D. Halls (Free Press, 1982) — **PAYWALLED** / older trans. **FREE**.
- *The Division of Labor in Society* (1893) — read the introduction and the sections on solidarity and anomie. Trans. W. D. Halls (Free Press). **PAYWALLED**.
- *Suicide* (1897) — read the introduction and the typology (egoistic/altruistic/anomic/fatalistic) as **selections**; skim the statistics. **FREE** (older trans., archive.org).
- *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) — read the introduction and conclusion. **Recommended modern translation: Karen E. Fields (Free Press, 1995; ISBN 9780029079379)** — the complete/unabridged scholarly standard (**PAYWALLED**). The free version is the older **1915 Joseph Ward Swain translation** (archive.org) — usable, but not the recommended one. (Rival: Carol Cosman's abridged OUP 2001 edition.)
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Steven Lukes, *Émile Durkheim: His Life and Work* (1973) — the definitive intellectual biography (dip in); the Robert Alun Jones "Durkheim Pages." **PAYWALLED / FREE** respectively.
**Exercise:** Give a Durkheimian reading of a modern collective ritual (a sporting final, a national holiday, a viral online moment) using the sacred/profane and collective-effervescence ideas.
**Calibration:** "Social facts," solidarity, anomie, the sociology-of-religion framework = **SETTLED**. The *specific* empirical analysis in *Suicide* = **CONTESTED** (data-quality problems and the ecological-fallacy critique — inferring individual causes from group rates), though it remains the founding demonstration that an intensely "private" act is socially patterned.
**Time:** 3–4 weeks.

---

### MODULE 4 — Max Weber
**Goals:** *Verstehen* (interpretive understanding), rationalization and the "iron cage," the three types of legitimate authority, bureaucracy, and the class/status/party distinction.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 2–3 (Weber is partly in dialogue with Marx).
**PRIMARY (selections):**
- *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904–05) — read in full-ish (short and central). **Recommended modern translations: Stephen Kalberg (Oxford UP revised ed., 2011; the only translation of Weber's revised 1920 text published since 1930) or Peter Baehr & Gordon C. Wells (Penguin, 2002)** — **PAYWALLED**. The classic **1930 Talcott Parsons translation is FREE** on marxists.org and Wikisource (see jurisdiction caveat below).
- *From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology*, eds. H. H. Gerth & C. Wright Mills (Oxford, 1946) — read "Science as a Vocation," "Politics as a Vocation," "Bureaucracy," and "Class, Status, Party." **PAYWALLED** (some essays **FREE** online).
- *Economy and Society* (posthumous, 1922) — read only the short sections on the types of authority and the definition of the state, via the Gerth & Mills selections or the Roth & Wittich edition (UC Press). **Selections only.** **PAYWALLED**.
- **Jurisdiction caveat on the free Parsons translation:** public-domain status varies by country under life+70 rules; marxists.org and Wikisource host it as free, but confirm locally if you are in a life+70 jurisdiction.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Reinhard Bendix, *Max Weber: An Intellectual Portrait* (1960); or the Weber chapter in Collins, *Four Sociological Traditions*.
**Exercise:** Write a short "Marx vs. Weber on the origins of capitalism" comparison — material base vs. ideas/ethos — and state where you think each is strongest.
**Calibration:** Rationalization, the authority typology, bureaucracy, the multidimensional view of stratification (class *and* status *and* party) = **SETTLED**. The **Protestant-ethic causal thesis specifically = CONTESTED** — challenged from the start (R. H. Tawney, Kurt Samuelsson) and, in modern economic history, by **Sascha Becker & Ludger Woessmann, "Was Weber Wrong? A Human Capital Theory of Protestant Economic History," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 124(2), 2009**, which argues Protestant literacy/human capital (Bible-reading), instrumented by distance to Wittenberg, accounts for the Protestant–Catholic prosperity gap. Present this as *challenged*, not *disproven* — some read the human-capital account as complementary to Weber rather than a refutation. The interpretive, comparative-historical *method* remains canonical.
**Time:** 3–4 weeks.

### MODULE 5 — Georg Simmel & W. E. B. Du Bois
**Goals:** Simmel's *formal* sociology (social forms, the metropolis, the stranger, the social geometry of numbers) and Du Bois's double consciousness, the color line, and the veil — recovering a founder long excluded from the canon.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 2–4.
**PRIMARY:**
- Georg Simmel, "The Metropolis and Mental Life" (1903) and "The Stranger" (1908) — both short, read in full. In *The Sociology of Georg Simmel*, ed./trans. Kurt H. Wolff (Free Press, 1950) — **PAYWALLED**. Older English translations are **FREE** (archive.org and classic-text archives), but modern scholarly translations are in copyright. Optionally skim selections from *The Philosophy of Money* (1900; trans. Bottomore & Frisby, Routledge).
- W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) — read the "Forethought," Ch. 1 ("Of Our Spiritual Strivings"), and one or two later essays. **FREE** (Project Gutenberg, eBook #408; fully public domain). Optionally note *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) as an early empirical urban study.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** David Frisby, *Georg Simmel* (Routledge); **Aldon Morris, *The Scholar Denied: W. E. B. Du Bois and the Birth of Modern Sociology* (Univ. of California Press, 2015)** — argues the Du Bois–Atlanta School founded American empirical sociology (from ~1897), predating and eclipsing the Chicago School.
**Exercise:** Apply Simmel's "stranger" (the person who is *in* but not *of* a group) to a contemporary setting — expatriate life, online communities, or your own experience in Frankfurt.
**Calibration:** Simmel's formal sociology and micro-insights = **SETTLED** (and newly influential via network/relational sociology). Du Bois's status as a founding theorist = **SETTLED** — post-Morris (2015) his canonization is now mainstream, not a peripheral or faddish claim, and is unlikely to reverse.
**Time:** 2–3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 6 — The Classical Synthesis
**Goals:** Consolidate the founders into a working map; understand what problems each bequeathed to later theory (order, conflict, meaning, rationality).
**Prerequisites:** Modules 2–5.
**PRIMARY/SUPPLEMENTARY (read one synthesis):**
- Raymond Aron, *Main Currents in Sociological Thought* (2 vols.; Routledge Classics reissue, 2019, with a new introduction) — magisterial essays on Montesquieu/Comte/Marx (Vol. 1) and Durkheim/Pareto/Weber (Vol. 2). **PAYWALLED** (older ed. **FREE** on archive.org).
- or Randall Collins, *Four Sociological Traditions* (Oxford, 1994).
- or Anthony Giddens, *Capitalism and Modern Social Theory* (Cambridge, 1971) — a classic comparative treatment of Marx, Durkheim, and Weber.
**Exercise:** Draw your own one-page diagram of how the four founders answer "what holds society together, and what tears it apart?"
**Calibration:** The "founders" framing itself = **CONTESTED** in a productive way (the canon has been justly criticized as Eurocentric and male; Du Bois's inclusion and the Southern-theory critiques in Module 13 are part of the correction).
**Time:** 2 weeks.

---

### MODULE 7 — Structural Functionalism (Parsons & Merton)
**Goals:** The mid-century attempt at a grand theory of social order; the corrective turn to "middle-range theory."
**Prerequisites:** Module 3 (Durkheimian lineage).
**PRIMARY:**
- Talcott Parsons — read *about* rather than *through* him first (famously dense). Then sample *The Social System* (1951), Ch. 1, and/or the AGIL sections via a reader. **PAYWALLED.**
- Robert K. Merton, *Social Theory and Social Structure* (enlarged ed., Free Press, 1968) — read "Manifest and Latent Functions," "Social Structure and Anomie," "The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy," and the essay on middle-range theory. **PAYWALLED** (widely excerpted).
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** the functionalism chapter in Ritzer or Collins.
**Exercise:** Identify the *latent* (unintended, unrecognized) function of some institution or practice you know well from work or daily life.
**Calibration:** Parsonian **grand functionalism as a general theory = HYPE-WATCH / superseded** (critiqued for conservatism, tautology, and neglect of conflict and power) — this remains the disciplinary consensus. Merton's **middle-range concepts (manifest/latent functions, self-fulfilling prophecy, anomie/strain) = SETTLED** and still in active use. The broad move *away from grand theory toward middle-range theory* = **SETTLED**.
**Time:** 2–3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 8 — Symbolic Interactionism & Micro-Sociology (Mead, Goffman, Garfinkel)
**Goals:** How the self and social reality are produced in interaction; dramaturgy; the taken-for-granted order ethnomethodology exposes.
**Prerequisites:** Module 5 (Simmel), Module 0.
**PRIMARY:**
- George Herbert Mead, *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934) — read the sections on the "I" and the "me" and the genesis of the self through role-taking. **Recommended: the "Definitive Edition," annotated by Daniel R. Huebner & Hans Joas (ed. Charles W. Morris; Univ. of Chicago Press, 2015; ISBN 9780226112732)** — **PAYWALLED**. The free version is the **original 1934 Morris edition** (Brock University "Mead Project"; archive.org) — **FREE**. Herbert Blumer, *Symbolic Interactionism* (1969) — read the title essay.
- Erving Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (Anchor, 1959) — read in full-ish (accessible and delightful). Then sample *Stigma* (1963) and one essay from *Interaction Ritual* (1967). **PAYWALLED.**
- Harold Garfinkel, *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967) — read *about* it plus a short selection (the "breaching experiments"). **Selections only** (hard prose). **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** the interactionism chapter in Collins, *Four Sociological Traditions*.
**Exercise:** Conduct a tiny, harmless Goffmanian observation — describe the "front stage / back stage" behavior in a café, an office, or a video call.
**Calibration:** Symbolic interactionism and Goffman's dramaturgy = **SETTLED** (a permanent micro-sociological tradition). Ethnomethodology = **SETTLED-but-niche** (influential, methodologically demanding, somewhat sequestered).
**Time:** 3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 9 — Critical Theory & the Frankfurt School (through Habermas)
**Goals:** The Western-Marxist turn to culture, ideology, and reason; the culture industry; the public sphere and communicative action. **Local anchor:** the Institut für Sozialforschung, founded 1923 in Frankfurt — your city.
**Prerequisites:** Module 2 (Marx).
**PRIMARY (selections):**
- Max Horkheimer & Theodor W. Adorno, *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1947) — read "The Concept of Enlightenment" and "The Culture Industry." **Recommended: ed. Gunzelin Schmid Noerr, trans. Edmund Jephcott (Stanford UP, 2002; ISBN 9780804736336)** — the standard current edition, based on the German *Gesammelte Schriften*, superseding the 1972 Cumming translation. **PAYWALLED** (only excerpts free on marxists.org).
- Herbert Marcuse, *One-Dimensional Man* (Beacon, 1964) — read the introduction and Ch. 1. **FREE** (full text on marxists.org / marcuse.org).
- Jürgen Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962; trans. Thomas Burger, MIT Press, 1989) — read the introduction and the sections on the bourgeois public sphere. On *The Theory of Communicative Action* (2 vols.), read *about* it plus a short selection. **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Martin Jay, *The Dialectical Imagination* (1973) — the definitive history of the Institute, 1923–1950; or Stephen Eric Bronner, *Critical Theory: A Very Short Introduction*.
**Exercise:** Assess one Adorno–Horkheimer "culture industry" claim against a present-day media platform: where does it land, and where does it overreach?
**Calibration:** Critical Theory's influence = **SETTLED** (foundational for cultural and media sociology). Adorno–Horkheimer's *totalizing* pessimism about mass culture = **CONTESTED** (later audience/reception research finds more agency than the "culture dupe" model allows). Habermas's public-sphere concept = **SETTLED-influential**, its historical accuracy **CONTESTED**.
**Time:** 3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 10 — Pierre Bourdieu
**Goals:** Habitus, field, and the forms of capital (economic, cultural, social, symbolic); how inequality is reproduced through taste and education; reflexivity.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 2 and 9 (domination/reproduction lineage) and 4 (Weberian status).
**PRIMARY (selections):**
- "The Forms of Capital" (1986) — the essential short essay; read in full. **FREE** (widely available).
- *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste* (1979; trans. Richard Nice, Harvard, 1984) — read the introduction and one empirical chapter. **Selections.** **PAYWALLED.**
- *An Invitation to Reflexive Sociology* (with Loïc Wacquant, Univ. of Chicago Press, 1992) — the most accessible doorway to the whole framework; read Parts I–II. **PAYWALLED.**
- Optionally sample *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1972/1977) for the concept of habitus.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** the Bourdieu chapter in Ritzer; or David Swartz, *Culture and Power: The Sociology of Pierre Bourdieu* (1997).
**Exercise:** Analyze a domain you know (coffee, wine, menswear, music taste, tech-culture signaling) in Bourdieusian terms — what cultural capital is being displayed, and in what field?
**Calibration:** Habitus and the forms of capital = **SETTLED** as the dominant, most-cited framework in contemporary cultural sociology (by several bibliometric accounts Bourdieu is now cited more than Durkheim). The charge that Bourdieu is overly **deterministic / underweights agency and change = CONTESTED**. Its main named rival is **Jeffrey C. Alexander's "strong program" in cultural sociology** (Alexander & Smith, 2001/2003), which treats meaning as relatively autonomous and criticizes Bourdieu for reducing culture to class position — worth knowing as the live alternative.
**Time:** 3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 11 — Michel Foucault
**Goals:** Power/knowledge, disciplinary power and surveillance, biopower and governmentality — power as productive and capillary rather than merely repressive. (Foucault called himself a historian/philosopher, but his impact on sociology is enormous.)
**Prerequisites:** Module 4 (Weberian rationalization/discipline lineage).
**PRIMARY (selections):**
- *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison* (1975; trans. Alan Sheridan, Vintage, 1977) — read "The Body of the Condemned" and the "Panopticism" chapter. **Selections.** **PAYWALLED.**
- *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1: An Introduction* (1976; Vintage, 1978) — read Part IV/V on biopower and the "repressive hypothesis." **PAYWALLED.**
- *The Foucault Reader*, ed. Paul Rabinow (Pantheon, 1984) — good curated selections, including on governmentality. **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Gary Gutting, *Foucault: A Very Short Introduction*.
**Exercise:** Apply "panopticism" (visibility as a mechanism of control) to a contemporary system — workplace monitoring, platform metrics, credit scoring, or algorithmic risk models. Where does the metaphor illuminate, and where does it stretch thin?
**Calibration:** Foucault's *influence* = **SETTLED** (pervasive across the social sciences). His **historical/empirical accuracy = CONTESTED** (historians dispute several periodizations and claims). The loose, everywhere-application of "biopower" and "governmentality" = **HYPE-WATCH** (often decoration rather than analysis).
**Time:** 3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 12 — Structuration & Figurational Sociology (Giddens, Elias)
**Goals:** The attempts to dissolve the agency/structure and micro/macro dualisms — structure as both medium and outcome of action; long-run "figurations."
**Prerequisites:** Modules 7–11.
**PRIMARY (selections + secondary):**
- Anthony Giddens, *The Constitution of Society: Outline of the Theory of Structuration* (Polity, 1984) — read the introduction and the chapter presenting the duality of structure. His shorter *New Rules of Sociological Method* (1976) is an easier entry. **Selections.** **PAYWALLED.**
- Norbert Elias, *The Civilizing Process* (1939; revised one-volume ed., trans. Edmund Jephcott, Blackwell, 2000) — read the synopsis and the theoretical conclusion on figurations and interdependence. **Selections.** **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** the agency/structure chapter in a contemporary-theory reader; Ira Cohen on structuration.
**Exercise:** In a paragraph, state the agency/structure problem in your own words and explain how "duality of structure" tries to solve it.
**Calibration:** The agency/structure debate = **SETTLED** as a central theoretical problem. Structuration theory as a *solution* = **CONTESTED** (praised as a synthesis, criticized as hard to operationalize empirically). Elias's specific historical trajectory of "civilizing" = **CONTESTED** among historians, though his relational method is influential.
**Time:** 2–3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 13 — Contemporary Theory & Critiques
**Goals:** Sample the live contemporary conversation — rational choice, network/relational sociology, feminist and intersectional theory, modernity/globalization diagnoses, and the postcolonial/Southern critique of the Western canon.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 6–12.
**PRIMARY (short selections — pick across these):**
- **Rational choice:** James S. Coleman, *Foundations of Social Theory* (Harvard, 1990) — read the opening programmatic chapters only (it's vast). **PAYWALLED.**
- **Feminist / standpoint / intersectional:** Dorothy E. Smith, *The Everyday World as Problematic* (1987), intro; Patricia Hill Collins, *Black Feminist Thought* (2nd ed., 2000), the chapter defining intersectionality; Candace West & Don H. Zimmerman, "Doing Gender," *Gender & Society* 1(2), 1987. **PAYWALLED** (West & Zimmerman widely available).
- **Networks/relational:** Mark Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties," *American Journal of Sociology* 78(6), 1973 — read in full (short, seminal). **FREE** legal PDFs hosted by universities (Stanford, U. Washington, U. Penn); the authoritative version is on JSTOR (paywalled).
- **Modernity/globalization (read as essayistic diagnosis):** Zygmunt Bauman, *Liquid Modernity* (Polity, 2000), intro; Ulrich Beck, *Risk Society* (1986; trans. Mark Ritter, Sage, 1992), intro — another Germany-rooted thinker. **PAYWALLED.**
- **Postcolonial / Southern theory (the canon critiqued from outside):** Raewyn Connell, *Southern Theory* (Polity, 2007), intro; optionally note Edward Said, *Orientalism* (1978) as adjacent. **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** the Calhoun et al. *Contemporary Sociological Theory* reader (4th ed., 2022) ties these together.
**Exercise (optional, computational — the one place it's natural):** Load a small social network (a friendship or co-authorship dataset, or export your own contacts graph) and, in Python with `networkx`, compute degree and betweenness centrality; identify the "bridges" and connect what you see to Granovetter's weak-ties argument. Keep it exploratory — a concept-illustration, not a research project.
**Calibration:** "Strength of weak ties" = **SETTLED** (one of sociology's most robust, most-replicated findings). Intersectionality as a *concept* = **SETTLED-influential**; its rigorous *operationalization/measurement* = **CONTESTED**. Rational-choice sociology = **CONTESTED** (powerful but disputed as a general foundation). Bauman's "liquid modernity" and Beck's "risk society" = **CONTESTED / essayistic** — influential grand diagnoses that are hard to test empirically (lean HYPE-WATCH if treated as established fact rather than provocation).
**Time:** 3–4 weeks.

### MODULE 14 — Methods (Breadth, Light)
**Goals:** The logic of social inquiry — positivist vs. interpretive traditions, quantitative vs. qualitative methods, ethnography, comparative-historical analysis, survey research — plus an honest note on measurement, causation, and social-science credibility.
**Prerequisites:** The theory core (so you can see how method and theory connect).
**PRIMARY (survey text — pick one):**
- **Earl Babbie, *The Practice of Social Research*, 15th ed. (Cengage, ©2021; ISBN 9780357360767)** — the standard broad survey; read the chapters on research design, measurement, and the two traditions. **PAYWALLED.**
- or W. Lawrence Neuman, *Social Research Methods: Qualitative and Quantitative Approaches* (Pearson). **PAYWALLED.**
**SUPPLEMENTARY / the credibility discussion (now live in sociology, not just psychology):** Theda Skocpol's methodological appendix in *States and Social Revolutions* for comparative-historical method; and on reproducibility — **Jeremy Freese & David Peterson, "Replication in Social Science," *Annual Review of Sociology* 43 (2017)** (which notes sociologists had been largely absent from the replication debate and distinguishes verifiability, robustness, repeatability, and generalizability); **Moody, Keister & Ramos, "Reproducibility in the Social Sciences," *Annual Review of Sociology* 48 (2022)**; and the journal *Sociological Science*'s replication-package requirement (for submissions after 1 April 2023). You already know the quantitative side of this from your work.
**Exercise:** For one classic study in this curriculum (Durkheim's *Suicide* or Weber's *Protestant Ethic*), name the method used and one methodological objection a modern reviewer would raise.
**Calibration:** The quantitative/qualitative and positivist/interpretive splits = **SETTLED** as enduring methodological poles. That much social-science research faces reproducibility/credibility pressures — and that these now reach sociology directly = **SETTLED**.
**Time:** 2–3 weeks.

### MODULE 15 — Substantive Subfields (Breadth, Light)
**Goals:** Working familiarity with the main substantive areas via one or two touchstone works each. Read introductions and key articles, not whole monographs.
**Prerequisites:** Theory core.
**PRIMARY touchstones (sample widely, read lightly):**
- **Stratification & mobility:** Kingsley Davis & Wilbert Moore, "Some Principles of Stratification," *ASR* 1945 — *paired with* Melvin Tumin's 1953 critique; Peter Blau & Otis Dudley Duncan, *The American Occupational Structure* (1967) — the status-attainment model. **PAYWALLED / articles FREE.**
- **Power & elites:** C. Wright Mills, *The Power Elite* (1956), intro.
- **Race & ethnicity:** Du Bois (Module 5); Michael Omi & Howard Winant, *Racial Formation in the United States* (3rd ed., 2014), the "racial formation" chapter.
- **Gender & sexuality:** Arlie Russell Hochschild, *The Managed Heart* (1983) — emotional labor; and *The Second Shift* (1989). West & Zimmerman (Module 13).
- **Culture:** Ann Swidler, "Culture in Action: Symbols and Strategies," *ASR* 1986 — culture as a "tool kit."
- **Economic sociology:** Mark Granovetter, "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness," *AJS* 1985; Viviana Zelizer on the social meaning of money.
- **Political sociology & the state:** Theda Skocpol, *States and Social Revolutions* (1979), intro.
- **Urban:** the Chicago School — Robert Park & Ernest Burgess, *The City* (1925), selection; William Julius Wilson, *The Truly Disadvantaged* (1987).
- **Organizations:** Weber on bureaucracy (Module 4); Paul DiMaggio & Walter Powell, "The Iron Cage Revisited: Institutional Isomorphism…," *ASR* 1983.
- **Deviance & crime:** Howard S. Becker, *Outsiders* (1963) — labeling theory; plus Merton's strain (Module 7).
- **Religion:** Weber & Durkheim; Peter Berger, *The Sacred Canopy* (1967), and the secularization debate.
- **Social movements:** Doug McAdam, Sidney Tarrow & Charles Tilly, *Dynamics of Contention* (2001) — the political-process/contentious-politics tradition.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** use Giddens & Sutton, *Sociology* (9th ed., 2021), as the connective tissue — read the relevant chapter before each cluster of articles.
**Exercise:** Pick the two subfields closest to your interests and write a paragraph on each linking it back to one of the four founders.
**Calibration highlights:** Weak-ties/embeddedness in economic sociology = **SETTLED**. The **Davis-Moore functional theory of stratification = CONTESTED** (the Tumin critique is decisive for many). Labeling theory = **SETTLED-but-partial** (real but not a total account of deviance). The **strong secularization thesis = CONTESTED / superseded** — the claim that modernization necessarily erodes religion is rejected by most sociologists of religion given religion's global persistence; **Peter Berger, once its leading proponent, publicly recanted** (and edited *The Desecularization of the World*, 1999). Note the main continuing defender, **Steve Bruce**, who argued the recantation was unnecessary — so treat "secularization" as a live, plural debate, not a settled fact in either direction.
**Time:** 4–5 weeks (a sampler — resist the urge to go deep).

## TWO TRACKS

### MVP FAST-PATH (~3–4 months, part-time)
The minimum canonical spine for a solid generalist command of sociological *theory*:
1. **Orientation:** Mills, *The Sociological Imagination*, Ch. 1.
2. **The four founders (selections):** Marx (alienation + commodity fetishism + *Manifesto*), Durkheim (*Rules* Chs. 1–2 + *Suicide* typology + *Elementary Forms* intro/conclusion), Weber (*Protestant Ethic* + "Politics/Science as a Vocation" + "Class, Status, Party"), Simmel ("Metropolis" + "The Stranger"). Add Du Bois, *Souls*, Ch. 1.
3. **Three contemporary keystones:** Goffman, *Presentation of Self*; Bourdieu, "The Forms of Capital"; Foucault, "Panopticism."
4. **One narrative companion** read alongside: Collins, *Four Sociological Traditions* (or Coser).
5. **One robust empirical anchor:** Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties."

Do the **three writing exercises** (Marx-vs-Weber; a Durkheimian ritual reading; a Bourdieusian analysis of your own taste). Those alone convert the reading into durable understanding.

### FULL ARC (~1–2 years, part-time)
All 16 modules in order at ~8–10 hours/week. Budget roughly 2–4 weeks per module; the four-founder modules (2–5) and the contemporary-theory module (13) are the heaviest. Keep to **selections** as marked — the discipline of *not* reading everything is what makes this finishable in two years.

## Recommendations
1. **Buy the two anthologies first** (Calhoun et al., *Classical* and *Contemporary*, both 4th ed. 2022) and one narrative companion. They carry the whole program and save you assembling scattered PDFs.
2. **Read primary sources as excerpts, secondary sources for the map.** For the hard texts (Parsons, Garfinkel, Habermas's *Communicative Action*, Coleman), read *about* them first and sample only short primary selections. This single move is what keeps the program inside the 1–2-year budget.
3. **Exploit Frankfurt.** At Module 9, treat the Institut für Sozialforschung as a "visit the source" field trip; pair it with Martin Jay's *The Dialectical Imagination*. It makes Critical Theory concrete in a way reading alone won't.
4. **Use the free canon aggressively — but check the translation.** marxists.org, Gutenberg, and archive.org cover most classical primary texts; just remember that for Durkheim, Simmel, Mead, Tocqueville, and Weber the free file is an older public-domain translation. Spend money mainly on the *good modern translations* (Fields's Durkheim, Kalberg/Baehr's Weber, Fowkes's *Capital*) and the readers.
5. **Treat calibration tags as reading rules.** For any HYPE-WATCH or CONTESTED thesis (Protestant Ethic, Davis-Moore, strong secularization, Foucauldian "biopower" applications), read at least one critique before citing it approvingly.

## Caveats
- **Editions verified September 2026, but printings turn over.** The one active watch-item is Marx's *Capital*: the 2024 Reitter/North (Princeton) translation is an emerging rival to Fowkes, but it renders a *different German source edition*, so the two are not interchangeable — keep Fowkes as assigned and re-evaluate if it becomes the syllabus standard. Textbook metadata also blends "copyright year" and "release date" (e.g., Babbie's 15th ed. carries a 2021 copyright but shipped mid-2020).
- **"Free" often means an older translation.** Distinguish the recommended modern translation from the public-domain relic (flagged per module and in the table). Public-domain status of the 1930 Parsons Weber varies by jurisdiction; confirm locally in life+70 countries. Academic archive URLs (Brock "Mead Project," the "Durkheim Pages," university-hosted Granovetter PDFs) move over time — re-check at point of linking.
- **This is deliberately a theory-weighted, generalist program.** By design, Modules 14–15 (methods and subfields) are *samplers*, not deep sequences. If you later want research-level command of a single subfield — economic sociology and networks would be the natural fit given your background — that's a separate, deeper build.
- **The canon is itself contested.** The "four founders + Du Bois" framing reflects where the discipline now sits, but Modules 5, 6, and 13 deliberately surface the Eurocentrism-and-exclusion critique so you read the canon *and* its self-correction. The reappraisals folded into the calibration tags (Du Bois's canonization, the rejection of the strong secularization thesis, the human-capital challenge to Weber, Bourdieu's dominance vs. Alexander's strong program, sociology's late arrival to the replication debate) reflect the discipline's standing as of 2026 and will keep shifting — re-check any debate you plan to lean on.
