# A Curriculum in Music History

*Global scope: a Western art-music chronological spine, braided with popular music and world/folk traditions. Balanced method — guided listening, stylistic-analytic understanding, and cultural context. A parallel primary-source track (treatises, letters, criticism) throughout.*

---

## Preface

### What this subject is — and why it is really three subjects

"Music history" as a discipline has traditionally meant **historical musicology**: the study of Western art music, organised as a chronology of styles and works. But you asked for a *global* history, and that means braiding together three fields that are genuinely different in method, not one smooth story:

- **Historical musicology** (Western art music) — document- and score-based, strongly periodized, the natural chronological spine.
- **Popular music studies** — drawing on cultural studies and sociology; recording-era, industry- and identity-focused; only loosely periodized.
- **Ethnomusicology** — anthropological and fieldwork-based; studies music as culture, often in living oral traditions; explicitly *not* organised as a march toward European art music.

This curriculum uses the Western art-music chronology as its **backbone** (because it is the most periodized thread and the connective tissue to your theory work), weaves popular and world traditions in at their points of contact, and then gives each a dedicated in-depth phase. But hold the seams visibly: you are learning three disciplines' worth of history, and they do not share a single timeline or a single idea of what "counts."

### Three caveats to hold from day one

1. **There is no neutral, inevitable canon.** The familiar "great composers marching toward ever-greater sophistication" narrative is a nineteenth-century construction, and it has been thoroughly criticised from inside the field (the "new musicology" of Kerman, McClary, and others — and by Taruskin, whose history you'll use as your spine). Periods, canons, and "masterworks" are retrospective choices, not facts of nature.

2. **The "world music" category is itself suspect.** Lumping everything non-Western into one bin — and treating those traditions as timeless, unchanging "folk" backdrops to Western "history" — is a colonial habit that ethnomusicology has spent decades reckoning with. In this curriculum, world and folk traditions get sustained treatment **on their own terms** (Phase 11), not as quaint precursors to Bach. Their placement *late* in the sequence is a purely pedagogical choice (it lets you build the ethnomusicological toolkit after the Western narrative is familiar) and is **not** a claim that they are secondary or that they "come after" — you could equally begin there.

3. **Origins stories and genius myths are usually legend.** "Jazz was born in New Orleans in year X," "Robert Johnson sold his soul at the crossroads," "Beethoven the lone heroic sufferer" — tidy origins and romanticised biographies are almost always messier, more collective, and more documented-than-mythologised on close inspection. A recurring, well-evidenced pattern in popular music specifically is the erasure of Black originators in favour of white popularisers; that is history, not editorialising. You'll meet the model debunkings (Wald on the blues) as you go.

### The four tracks

Run them together within each phase:

1. **Narrative & Style** — read the history; understand *how and why* style changed (this is where your theory work pays off: you can hear what "the tonal system consolidating" or "atonality" actually means).
2. **Listening** — the repertoire itself, guided. History with the sound switched off is a reading list, not a musical education. Listen to every flagged work.
3. **Cultural Context** — music in its social, political, economic, and technological world (patronage, print, the concert market, recording, race, nation, ideology).
4. **Primary Sources** — the parallel documents track you asked for: treatises, composers' letters, manifestos, and criticism, read alongside the narrative so you meet each era in its own voice.

### Bridges to your existing curricula

This is the diachronic complement to your (synchronic) **music theory** work, and the connections are exact, not decorative:
- The history of **notation** *is* Phase 2 (medieval) — the thing your theory curriculum taught you to read was invented here.
- **Rameau's *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722)** — a primary source in Phase 4 — is literally the origin of the functional-harmony theory you studied. You will read the founding document of your own theory curriculum.
- **Sonata form**, **figured bass**, and the **rise and dissolution of tonality** are historical events (Phases 4–7, 10) whose *results* you already know as theory.

It also ties to your **rhetoric** and **classical** work: Baroque *musica poetica* mapped classical rhetorical figures directly onto music (the doctrine of the affections), and the birth of opera came from a Renaissance attempt to *recover Greek dramatic declamation* (the Florentine Camerata's Hellenism). Greek music theory and its *ethos* doctrine (Phase 1) sit beside the Plato and Aristotle you've read. Word-setting and text-music relations recur throughout and connect to your prosody curriculum's central question of stress and text.

### A note on troubling figures

You will study people whose politics or acts are repugnant — Wagner's antisemitism most prominently, but not only his. The honest historical stance separates *documenting* from *endorsing*: it neither erases the work nor excuses the person, and it treats the ugliness (and its later reception and instrumentalisation) as part of the record to be understood. This curriculum handles such cases squarely rather than avoiding them.

### Listening & source infrastructure

- **Scores:** IMSLP (imslp.org) — free, near-complete for public-domain art music. [Free]
- **Recordings:** Spotify / YouTube cover almost the entire repertoire; archive.org holds historical recordings; **Naxos Music Library** is the scholarly streaming standard (often free via a library card). [Largely free]
- Many primary treatises (Plato, Boethius, Zarlino, Rameau, Hanslick, Russolo, the Futurist and Cage manifestos) are public-domain and free online. [Free]

## Permanent Reference Library

**The chronological spine (Western art music).**
- **J. Peter Burkholder, Donald Jay Grout & Claude V. Palisca, *A History of Western Music*, latest ed. (Norton).** The standard textbook ("Grout"). Your practical working spine — comprehensive, paired with score and recorded anthologies below.
- **The Norton Anthology of Western Music (NAWM)** + **The Norton Recorded Anthology of Western Music**, latest ed. The scores and recordings that accompany Grout. Essential for the Listening track.
- **Richard Taruskin, *The Oxford History of Western Music*, 5 vols. (Oxford UP, 2005)** — or its one-volume **College Edition**. The magisterial, opinionated, anti-teleological narrative; read *with* Grout, not instead of it. Western-art-music only, and enormous — use selectively for depth and argument.

**The primary-source backbone (your parallel track).**
- **Oliver Strunk (ed.), rev. Leo Treitler, *Source Readings in Music History*, rev. ed. (Norton, 1998).** The anthology of Western primary documents from antiquity to the twentieth century — treatises, letters, criticism, organised by era. The single most important book for your primary-source track; nearly every source cited below is in it.

**Method / historiography (read early, revisit).**
- **Nicholas Cook, *Music: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford UP, 1998).** A brilliant, brief, critical orientation to what music and its study actually are. [Best on-ramp]
- **Joseph Kerman, *Contemplating Music* (Harvard UP, 1985; UK title *Musicology*).** The field's own self-critique — why the discipline studies what it studies.
- **Carl Dahlhaus, *Foundations of Music History*, trans. J. B. Robinson (Cambridge UP, 1983).** The classic on the philosophy and method of music history. [Advanced]

**Popular music.**
- **Larry Starr & Christopher Waterman, *American Popular Music: From Minstrelsy to MP3* (Oxford UP).** Standard textbook spine for the popular strand.
- **Ted Gioia, *The History of Jazz* (Oxford UP).** The best single-volume jazz history.

**World music / ethnomusicology.**
- **Jeff Todd Titon (ed.), *Worlds of Music: An Introduction to the Music of the World's Peoples* (Cengage).** The standard survey textbook (with recordings) — spine for the world strand.
- **Bruno Nettl, *The Study of Ethnomusicology* (U. of Illinois Press).** The discipline's reflective classic.
- **The Garland Encyclopedia of World Music (Routledge).** The multi-volume regional reference of record. [Reference]

**Listening skills (gentle, free).**
- **Craig Wright, Open Yale Course "Listening to Music"** (oyc.yale.edu) — a free, complete university listening course; excellent for the Listening track and as a soft on-ramp. [Free]

## Phase 0 — Orientation: how to do music history (and its problems)

**Goal.** Acquire the meta-tools before the narrative: what the three disciplines are and why they differ; the canon and periodization critiques; how to listen historically; how to read a primary source critically. This frames everything and front-loads the caveats above.

**Prerequisites.** None. (Your music-theory curriculum is a strong asset throughout but not strictly required.)

**Narrative & Style.** Cook, *Music: A Very Short Introduction* (whole); Kerman, *Contemplating Music* (selectively); Grout, Introduction.

**Cultural Context.** Philip Bohlman, *World Music: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford UP) — the "world music" category and its problems, read now so the global frame is critical from the start. [Recommended]

**Primary Sources.** Read Strunk's editorial apparatus and one short document from each of two distant eras (e.g. a medieval treatise excerpt and a modern manifesto) purely to practise *how* to read a source — its rhetoric, its assumptions, its silences.

**Exercises.**
- *Narrative*: Write a page on why "the canon" is a constructed choice, using Cook and Kerman.
- *Listening*: Do 2–3 sessions of Wright's Open Yale course to build a listening vocabulary.
- *Method*: Annotate one primary source for what it *assumes* rather than what it states.

**Minimum viable path.** Cook (whole) + two Wright lectures + the primary-source annotation.

**Time estimate.** ~2 weeks.

## Phase 1 — Antiquity & foundations

**Goal.** The ancient inheritance: Greek music theory and its ideas (Pythagorean ratios, the modes, the *ethos* doctrine), the little surviving ancient music, and the transmission into the Middle Ages — while noting that other traditions (China, India) were equally ancient and equally sophisticated.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0. (Your classical/Greek work is directly relevant.)

**Narrative & Style.** Grout, ch. 1; Taruskin vol. 1, opening, for the transmission story.

**Listening.** The few surviving fragments (the Seikilos epitaph; Delphic hymns) — brief but worth hearing.

**Cultural Context.** Music's place in Greek education and philosophy; *ethos* (the belief that modes shape character).

**Primary Sources.** Plato, *Republic* (on the modes and their moral effects) and Aristotle, *Politics* VIII — which you may already have from your classical reading; **Boethius, *De institutione musica*** (the text that carried Greek theory to the Latin Middle Ages). All in Strunk. [Public domain]

**Exercises.**
- *Narrative*: Explain the *ethos* doctrine and one way its assumptions differ from a modern view of music.
- *Cross-connection*: Relate Pythagorean interval ratios to the acoustics you met in your theory curriculum.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout ch. 1 + the Boethius and Plato excerpts.

**Time estimate.** ~2 weeks.

## Phase 2 — The Middle Ages (c. 500–1400)

**Goal.** Plainchant and the invention of notation; the birth of polyphony; the fourteenth-century flowering (Ars Nova) and secular song. This is where the written language of Western music was created — the direct prehistory of your theory curriculum.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 1.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout, chs. on the Middle Ages; Taruskin vol. 1 for depth. Key figures/genres: Gregorian chant; early organum → the Notre Dame school (Léonin, Pérotin); the motet; Guillaume de Machaut (Ars Nova); the troubadours/trouvères and Minnesang.

**Listening (NAWM).** Chant; Notre Dame organum; a Machaut ballade and his *Messe de Nostre Dame*; a troubadour song.

**Cultural Context.** The Church, monastery, and cathedral as musical institutions; orality vs the new literacy; the rise of the university.

**Primary Sources.** **Guido of Arezzo** on solmisation and staff notation (the *Micrologus* / the Epistle) — the origin of the do-re-mi and staff you learned as theory; the *Musica enchiriadis* (early polyphony). In Strunk. [Public domain]

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Describe how organum → Notre Dame polyphony → the isorhythmic motet increases in rhythmic and contrapuntal complexity.
- *Cross-connection*: Compare Guido's notation innovations to the staff literacy in your theory curriculum — what problem was each solving?

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's medieval chapters + the chant/organum/Machaut listening + Guido.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

---

## Phase 3 — The Renaissance (c. 1400–1600)

**Goal.** The age of imitative polyphony and the Franco-Flemish masters; the mass, motet, and madrigal; the Reformation's musical consequences; the arrival of music printing. Rich in rhetoric-and-music connections.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 2.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout's Renaissance chapters; Taruskin for depth. Figures/genres: Du Fay; **Josquin des Prez** and the Franco-Flemish school; Palestrina and the *stile antico*; Lassus; the Italian and English madrigal (Monteverdi's early books; Weelkes, Wilbye); Luther and the chorale; the Council of Trent.

**Listening (NAWM).** A Josquin motet (e.g. *Ave Maria … virgo serena*); Palestrina (*Pope Marcellus Mass*, Kyrie/Gloria); an Italian madrigal with vivid word-painting; a Lutheran chorale.

**Cultural Context.** Humanism and *musica reservata*; word-painting as musical rhetoric; printing (Petrucci) democratising music; the Reformation splitting sacred practice.

**Primary Sources.** **Gioseffo Zarlino, *Le istitutioni harmoniche*** (the great Renaissance theory treatise); Glarean, *Dodecachordon* (the modes); the Council of Trent's deliberations on church music. In Strunk. [Zarlino public domain]

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Analyse how a madrigal "paints" its text (rising lines on "ascend," dissonance on "pain") — an explicit tie to rhetorical figures from your rhetoric curriculum.
- *Cross-connection*: Relate Renaissance modal theory to the modes you met in your theory curriculum.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's Renaissance chapters + the Josquin/Palestrina/madrigal listening + Zarlino excerpt.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

---

## Phase 4 — The Baroque (c. 1600–1750)

**Goal.** The seconda prattica and the birth of opera; basso continuo; the consolidation of tonality; the concerto, sonata, and suite; Bach and Handel. The single richest phase for tying music history to your theory and rhetoric work.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 3.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout's Baroque chapters; Taruskin for depth. Figures/genres: **Monteverdi** (the *Orfeo*; the *prima/seconda prattica* debate); the Florentine Camerata and the invention of opera; Corelli and the concerto/sonata; Vivaldi; **Bach** and **Handel** as the summit; French (Lully, Rameau) vs Italian styles.

**Listening (NAWM).** Monteverdi (*Orfeo* excerpt; a late madrigal); a Corelli trio sonata; Vivaldi concerto; Bach (a fugue from the *WTC*; a cantata movement; a *Brandenburg*); Handel (a *Messiah* chorus).

**Cultural Context.** Court and church patronage; the doctrine of the affections; opera as a new public art; the birth of the professional composer.

**Primary Sources (a bumper phase).**
- The **Artusi–Monteverdi controversy** (the defence of the "second practice" — dissonance in the service of the text). In Strunk.
- **Jean-Philippe Rameau, *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722)** — *the founding document of functional-harmony theory*, i.e. the origin of your other curriculum. Read at least the excerpts in Strunk. [Public domain]
- Johann Mattheson on the affections; the Bach obituary/documents.

**Exercises.**
- *Cross-connection (do this one properly)*: Read the Rameau excerpt and connect it explicitly to the tonic/dominant function you learned as theory — you are reading the theory's birth certificate.
- *Style*: Explain what "the seconda prattica" changed, using the Artusi–Monteverdi exchange.
- *Rhetoric tie*: Map one Baroque "figure" (a musical-rhetorical device) onto a figure from your rhetoric curriculum.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's Baroque chapters + the Monteverdi/Bach/Handel listening + Rameau and the Artusi–Monteverdi exchange.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 5 — The Classical era (c. 1750–1820)

**Goal.** The galant style; the crystallising of sonata form and the symphony/quartet; Haydn, Mozart, and early Beethoven; opera reform; the rise of the public concert and the music market.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 4.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout's Classical chapters; Taruskin for depth. Figures/genres: the *galant* and *empfindsamer* styles; **Haydn** (the symphony and quartet); **Mozart** (concerto, opera, symphony); early **Beethoven**; Gluck's operatic reform.

**Listening (NAWM).** A Haydn symphony (a *London* symphony) and a quartet; Mozart (a piano concerto; a *Don Giovanni* or *Figaro* scene; the *Jupiter* finale); early Beethoven (a piano sonata; Symphony No. 1 or 3).

**Cultural Context.** Enlightenment aesthetics; patronage giving way to the public concert and publishing market (Mozart as an early "freelance"); Vienna as a musical capital.

**Primary Sources.** **C. P. E. Bach, *Essay on the True Art of Playing Keyboard Instruments*** (performance and the *empfindsamer* aesthetic); **Mozart's letters** (a vivid, human primary source); Charles Burney's travel journals (a first-hand tour of European musical life); Rousseau and the *querelle des bouffons* documents. In Strunk / widely available. [Mozart letters, Burney public domain]

**Exercises.**
- *Cross-connection*: Diagram the sonata form of one first movement, using the form concepts from your theory curriculum — then note how the *historical* accounts differ from the *textbook* model (see Evidence note on sonata form).
- *Cultural*: Use Mozart's letters and Burney to describe how a composer actually made a living in 1780.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's Classical chapters + the Haydn/Mozart/Beethoven listening + Mozart's letters (a selection).

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

---

## Phase 6 — The Romantic century (c. 1800–1900)

**Goal.** Beethoven as the hinge into Romanticism; the Lied; the great absolute-vs-programme-music debate; virtuosity; musical nationalism (the natural bridge to folk/world material); Wagner and music drama; the late-Romantic summit. The richest phase for criticism as a primary source.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 5.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout's Romantic chapters; Taruskin for the arguments. Figures/genres: middle/late **Beethoven** (the "heroic" style and the late works); **Schubert** and the Lied; Berlioz and programme music; Chopin and Liszt (the piano and virtuosity); Schumann and Brahms; the "New German School" (Liszt, **Wagner**) vs the "absolute music" camp (Brahms, Hanslick); nationalism (the Russian "Mighty Handful"; Dvořák; Grieg; Smetana — and, of interest given your languages, **Chopin** and later Polish/Russian currents); Verdi vs Wagner in opera; Mahler and Strauss at the close.

**Listening (NAWM).** A late Beethoven work (a movement of the Ninth or a late quartet); Schubert Lieder (*Erlkönig*; *Winterreise* excerpt); a Berlioz *Symphonie fantastique* movement; Chopin and Liszt piano works; a Wagner scene (*Tristan* Prelude — the "*Tristan* chord," a hinge in the tonal story you know as theory); a Verdi scene; a Mahler movement.

**Cultural Context.** Romantic aesthetics (music as the highest, most "ineffable" art); the concert hall and the canon forming; nationalism and folk-music collection; the composer as prophet/genius; and, handled squarely, the ideological uses of music (Wagner).

**Primary Sources (criticism-rich).**
- E. T. A. Hoffmann's review of Beethoven's Fifth (music as the Romantic sublime).
- **Eduard Hanslick, *On the Musically Beautiful* (1854)** — the manifesto of "absolute music," against programme music. [Public domain]
- **Wagner's prose** — the *Gesamtkunstwerk* essays; and, read critically as historical evidence, *Das Judentum in der Musik* (his antisemitism — documented, not excused; see the note on troubling figures).
- Berlioz's *Memoirs* and his *Treatise on Instrumentation*; Schumann's criticism (the *Neue Zeitschrift für Musik*). In Strunk / public domain.

**Exercises.**
- *Debate*: Reconstruct the absolute-vs-programme-music argument from Hanslick and a New German School source; state where you think each is strongest (an argument-analysis task suited to your rhetoric/logic work).
- *Cross-connection*: Locate the *Tristan* chord in the score and explain, in your theory vocabulary, why it destabilises tonality — the beginning of the end of the common-practice system.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's Romantic chapters + the Beethoven/Schubert/Wagner/Mahler listening + Hanslick and one Wagner essay.

**Time estimate.** ~6 weeks.

## Phase 7 — The modernist rupture in art music (c. 1900–1945)

**Goal.** The break with tonality and the many modernisms; impressionism; the Second Viennese School; Stravinsky; Bartók (the art/folk bridge); and music under totalitarianism.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 6.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout's early-twentieth-century chapters; Taruskin vols. 4 for the arguments. Figures/genres: **Debussy** (impressionism, the loosening of function); **Schoenberg** and the Second Viennese School (**Berg, Webern**) — free atonality then the twelve-tone method; **Stravinsky** (*The Rite of Spring* and its 1913 riot; then neoclassicism); **Bartók** (folk-music fieldwork feeding art music — himself an ethnomusicologist); Ives (American maverick); Shostakovich and Prokofiev under Soviet pressure.

**Listening (NAWM).** Debussy (*Prélude à l'après-midi*); Schoenberg (*Pierrot lunaire*; a twelve-tone piece); Webern (a miniature); Stravinsky (*Rite* excerpt; a neoclassical movement); Bartók (a string quartet; *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta*).

**Cultural Context.** Modernism across the arts; the audience split from new music; music and the state (Nazi "*Entartete Musik*"; Soviet Socialist Realism and the Shostakovich case).

**Primary Sources.** **Arnold Schoenberg, *Style and Idea*** (his defence of atonality/twelve-tone); **Ferruccio Busoni, *Sketch of a New Aesthetic of Music***; **Luigi Russolo, *The Art of Noises* (1913 Futurist manifesto)** [public domain]; **Bartók's essays** on folk music (ed. Suchoff) — the art/folk bridge in the composer's own words.

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Explain the twelve-tone method and why Schoenberg saw it as a *continuation* of the tradition, not a rejection.
- *Cross-connection*: Use your theory vocabulary to describe what "atonality" removes from the common-practice system of Phases 4–6.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's early-20th chapters + the Debussy/Schoenberg/Stravinsky/Bartók listening + Schoenberg and Russolo.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 8 — Popular music I: roots and the recording era

**Goal.** Give popular music its own sustained treatment. Its African-American foundations; the birth and evolution of jazz; the transformation wrought by recording; the American song industry. This is where the honest account of race, appropriation, and the industry belongs.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0's method; helpful to have Phases 6–7 for context.

**Narrative & Style.** Starr & Waterman, *American Popular Music* (early chapters); **Gioia, *The History of Jazz***. Topics: spirituals, work songs, and the blues; ragtime; the birth of jazz (New Orleans → Chicago → swing → bebop → cool/modal/free); Tin Pan Alley and the Great American Songbook; country and folk roots; the rise of the recording industry.

**Listening.** Early blues (Bessie Smith; and a Robert Johnson track — heard *against* the myth, per Wald); Louis Armstrong; Duke Ellington; a Charlie Parker bebop side; Miles Davis (*Kind of Blue*, modal); a Coltrane track; a Gershwin/Porter standard.

**Cultural Context.** Slavery and its musical legacy; the Great Migration; segregation and the music industry; recording and radio remaking the audience; the documented pattern of Black origination and white commercial capture.

**Primary Sources.** W. E. B. Du Bois on the "sorrow songs" (*The Souls of Black Folk*) [public domain]; **Amiri Baraka (LeRoi Jones), *Blues People* (1963)** — the landmark account of Black American music as social history; blues and jazz oral histories and interviews; early jazz criticism; Alan Lomax's field documentation.

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Trace one line of jazz evolution (e.g. swing → bebop) and say what changed harmonically and rhythmically.
- *Calibration*: Read Wald's *Escaping the Delta* and write a paragraph on how the "Delta blues / crossroads" myth was constructed versus what the record shows.

**Minimum viable path.** Starr & Waterman early chapters + Gioia (selectively) + the blues/Armstrong/Parker/*Kind of Blue* listening + Baraka.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 9 — Popular music II: the postwar explosion

**Goal.** From rock 'n' roll to the digital present: rock, soul, funk, the album era, reggae, punk, hip-hop, electronic dance music, and globalised pop — with the cultural-studies lens (subculture, identity, technology, industry).

**Prerequisites.** Phase 8.

**Narrative & Style.** Starr & Waterman (later chapters). Topics: rock 'n' roll's emergence; soul, R&B, funk, Motown; the 1960s (Dylan; the Beatles; the album as an art form); reggae; disco; punk and its aftermath; **the birth of hip-hop** (1970s Bronx) and its rise to dominance; house/techno and electronic dance music; the globalisation of pop; the streaming era.

**Listening.** A curated arc: Chuck Berry / early rock 'n' roll; a Motown single; James Brown (funk); the Beatles (a *Revolver*/*Sgt. Pepper* track); Bob Dylan; a reggae track (Marley); a punk track; foundational hip-hop (Grandmaster Flash; Public Enemy; later, a landmark album); a house/techno track; a contemporary global-pop example.

**Cultural Context.** Youth subcultures; race and crossover; the LP and then the digital file/stream as format-shaping-content; MTV and the visual turn; sampling, copyright, and technology; globalisation and the internet.

**Primary Sources.** **Greil Marcus, *Mystery Train* (1975)** — the classic of serious rock writing; **Lester Bangs** (collected essays) — the voice of rock criticism; **Jeff Chang, *Can't Stop Won't Stop* (2005)** — the definitive hip-hop history; artist interviews and hip-hop oral histories.

**Exercises.**
- *Cultural*: Take one genre (e.g. hip-hop or house) and explain how a *technology* (the sampler, the drum machine, the turntable) shaped the music.
- *Criticism*: Read a Bangs or Marcus essay and analyse its critical stance — what does it value, and why?

**Minimum viable path.** Starr & Waterman later chapters + the listening arc + Chang (selectively) and one Marcus/Bangs essay.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 10 — Art music since 1945

**Goal.** The postwar avant-garde and after: total serialism; Cage and indeterminacy; electronic music; minimalism (with its non-Western and popular connections); and the postmodern pluralism of the present. A manifesto-rich phase.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 7.

**Narrative & Style.** Grout's post-1945 chapters; Taruskin vol. 5. Topics: integral serialism (**Boulez, Stockhausen**; Darmstadt); **John Cage**, chance, and indeterminacy (*4′33″*); *musique concrète* and electronic music; **minimalism** (Riley, **Reich**, **Glass** — note its debts to West African and Indonesian music and its ties to popular music); spectralism; the New Complexity; postmodern eclecticism and crossover.

**Listening.** A Boulez or Stockhausen work; Cage (*4′33″*; a prepared-piano piece); an early electronic/*concrète* work; Reich (*Music for 18 Musicians* or *Different Trains*); Glass; a spectral work (Grisey).

**Cultural Context.** The academy and the avant-garde; the ever-widening gap between new art music and the public; technology (the studio, the computer) as a compositional medium; the dissolving art/popular boundary.

**Primary Sources.** **John Cage, *Silence* (1961)** — the manifestos of indeterminacy; **Milton Babbitt, "Who Cares if You Listen?" (1958)** — the notorious essay (note: the confrontational title was the *editor's*; Babbitt's was "The Composer as Specialist" — a small but telling reception fact); **Steve Reich, *Writings on Music***; Boulez's polemics.

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Contrast total serialism and Cageian indeterminacy — opposite responses to the same question about control.
- *Cross-connection*: Explain how minimalism's use of process and repetition draws on non-Western music (previewing Phase 11) and feeds back into popular music.

**Minimum viable path.** Grout's post-1945 chapters + the Cage/Reich/electronic listening + Cage and the Babbitt essay.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 11 — World & folk traditions in depth (the ethnomusicological strand)

**Goal.** Sustained treatment of the world's major music traditions **on their own terms** — not as influences on, or precursors to, the West. Build the ethnomusicological toolkit and survey several major art-music and folk traditions in depth. (Reminder: this phase is placed late only for pedagogical convenience; its lateness implies no hierarchy — you could as well have begun here.)

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0's method; the discipline's concepts are self-contained.

**Narrative & Method.** **Titon (ed.), *Worlds of Music*** (the survey spine, with recordings); **Nettl, *The Study of Ethnomusicology*** (the discipline's concepts and self-reckoning); **Merriam, *The Anthropology of Music*** (music as culture); the relevant **Garland Encyclopedia** regional volumes for depth.

**Traditions to study (survey, then go deep by interest).**
- **South Asia:** Hindustani and Carnatic classical music — *raga* and *tala*, improvisation, the guru–shishya tradition.
- **East Asia:** Chinese traditions; Japanese *gagaku*, *koto*, and *shakuhachi*; Korean traditions.
- **Middle East / North Africa / Turkey / Persia:** the *maqam*/*makam*/*dastgah* modal systems.
- **Indonesia:** Javanese and Balinese *gamelan* (whose scales and textures shaped Debussy, Cage, and minimalism — a cross-current, not a root).
- **Sub-Saharan Africa:** West African drumming, polyrhythm, and its diaspora (the deep source behind Phase 8).
- **The Americas & Europe:** Latin American traditions; European folk musics and the "folk" concept itself.

**Cultural Context.** Ethnomusicology's own history and its colonial entanglements; the politics of collection, representation, and "salvage"; music, ritual, and identity; globalisation and hybridity.

**Primary Sources.** Ethnomusicological classics (Merriam; Nettl) **and — critically — sources from within these traditions**, not only Western observers; Bartók's folk-music essays again, now as a case study in how a Western composer collected and used folk material.

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Choose one tradition and describe its organising system (e.g. *raga* + *tala*, or *maqam*) in its own terms, resisting Western-theory translation where it distorts.
- *Method/critique*: Write a paragraph on one way early ethnomusicology's colonial context shaped what it recorded and how.

**Minimum viable path.** Titon (survey chapters) + Nettl (selectively) + deep study of *one* tradition of your choice with its recordings.

**Time estimate.** ~6–8 weeks (adjust with how many traditions you go deep on).

## Phase 12 — Capstone: cross-cutting themes & synthesis

**Goal.** Pull the braided strands together through themes that cut across all of them, and complete a synthesis project.

**Prerequisites.** All prior phases (or the MVP spine).

**Cross-cutting themes to trace.**
- **Technology drove music history:** notation → printing → recording → digital/streaming — each reshaped what music *is* and who makes it.
- **The art/popular/folk boundary is constructed**, not natural — and it has moved repeatedly.
- **Cross-pollination and globalisation:** gamelan → Debussy/minimalism; African rhythm → the entire Atlantic popular tradition; the internet-era global circulation.

**Capstone project (pick one).**
- Trace a single thread across the whole span — one *instrument* (e.g. the piano, or the drum), one *genre* (opera; the song), or one *cross-cultural exchange* — integrating narrative, listening, context, and at least two primary sources.
- Or: an in-depth study of one composer/tradition/era, with a source-grounded argument.

**Listening.** A final integrative listening project: follow a full score of one major work while listening, and separately listen across three traditions from Phase 11 to hear the world beyond the canon.

**Time estimate.** ~2–3 weeks.

## Evidence quality — what is settled, contested, and overstated

Per your usual preference, an explicit calibration of the claims you will meet:

- **The canon and its teleology** *(the central construction).* The "great works marching toward greater sophistication" story is a nineteenth-century invention, criticised from within the field (Kerman; McClary; Taruskin). Treat the canon as a defensible *choice*, not a natural fact — the same epistemic stance you take toward foot-scansion in prosody and toward "theory as universal law."
- **The "world music" category and musical Eurocentrism** *(a real disciplinary problem).* Lumping non-Western traditions together, and treating them as static "folk" backdrops to Western "history," is a colonial habit. Ethnomusicology now studies these traditions as living and historical in their own right; so should you.
- **Periodization** *(convenience, not fact).* "Renaissance," "Baroque," "Classical," "Romantic" are retrospective labels with fuzzy, contested edges — "Baroque" began as an insult; no composer knew they were "Classical." Useful shorthand; not natural kinds.
- **Genius and origin myths** *(usually legend).* Romanticised biographies (Mozart the divine child, Beethoven the lone sufferer, Robert Johnson at the crossroads) are more constructed than documented; Wald on the blues is the model correction. And "genre X was born in year Y with person Z" almost always oversimplifies a collective, gradual process.
- **Erasure of Black originators in popular music** *(well-documented, not editorial).* The recurring capture of Black-originated forms by white popularisers is a matter of record; naming it is history, not opinion.
- **"Authenticity"** *(largely a construction).* "Pure, untouched folk tradition" is often a product of the very collectors who claimed only to record it; likewise, "historically informed performance" (HIP) is a modern interpretive stance, not a neutral recovery of how music "really sounded." Both are genuinely contested.
- **Sonata form (and textbook forms generally)** *(a retrospective model).* The tidy exposition–development–recapitulation template was codified by nineteenth-century theorists *after* the music; eighteenth-century composers did not work from it. The textbook form is a useful analytic lens, not a recipe they followed.
- **Notation privileges some music and silences others** *(methodological humility).* Because history leans on documents, notated art traditions are richly "historical" while oral traditions can look (falsely) timeless. What survives shapes what we think happened.
- **"Who Cares if You Listen?"** *(a reception artefact).* The essay's provocative title was the magazine editor's, not Babbitt's — a small caution about taking even famous primary sources at face value.

## Beyond this curriculum

Natural continuations, none required:

- **Historiography and method in depth** — Dahlhaus, *Foundations of Music History*; the "new musicology" (McClary, *Feminine Endings*; Kerman); the sound-studies / media turn (Jonathan Sterne, *The Audible Past*).
- **Performance practice** — the HIP debates and the recovery of historical performance (a whole field; start with the *Cambridge History of Musical Performance*).
- **Single-era or single-tradition deep dives** — take any one Grout chapter or any one Phase 11 tradition to specialist depth via the Garland Encyclopedia and monographs.
- **National traditions of personal interest** — given your languages, **Polish** (Chopin; Szymanowski; Lutosławski; Górecki; Penderecki) and **Russian** (Glinka and the Mighty Handful; Tchaikovsky; Stravinsky; Shostakovich) music history would extend both this curriculum and your language/history work.
- **Analytical musicology** — pairing this history with deeper analysis (Schenkerian and post-tonal), connecting directly back to your theory curriculum's "Beyond" section.
- **The convergence point** — text-and-music (song, opera, word-setting) is where this curriculum, your **music theory**, your **prosody**, and your **rhetoric** work all meet; a focused study of the art song or of opera would braid all four.
