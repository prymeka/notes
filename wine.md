# Wine: A Research Primer

*Winemaking, terroir, flavor chemistry, and the world's great regions*

*Compiled May 2026*

---

## Introduction

Wine is a deceptively simple beverage: crushed grapes, yeast, time. From this minimal recipe emerges one of humanity's most documented, classified, and obsessed-over agricultural products. The distance between a $5 supermarket Cabernet and a Romanée-Conti Grand Cru is enormous, and most of that distance is explained by four interlocking factors: where the grapes grew, which variety they were, how the winemaker handled the fermentation and aging, and how long the resulting wine had to evolve.

This document compiles current research and authoritative writing on wine — drawing from peer-reviewed papers in journals like *OENO One* and *npj Science of Food*, from the encyclopedic works of Jancis Robinson and Karen MacNeil, and from leading wine publications like *Decanter*, *Wine Enthusiast*, *SevenFifty Daily*, and *Wine Folly*. It is structured to mirror the coffee research primer: process, chemistry, terroir, regions, and a reading list at the end.

## The winemaking process

The vine is harvested between summer and autumn (later for cool climates, earlier for warm ones). Picking can be by hand for premium and small-parcel work, or by machine for volume. From harvest, the path diverges sharply by colour.

### Red wines

Red grapes are crushed and the juice ferments *with* the skins, seeds, and sometimes stems. This contact — called maceration — extracts colour (from anthocyanins in the skins), tannins (from skins, seeds, and stems), and a host of flavour compounds. The crushed mixture is called *must*.

Many quality red winemaking programmes begin with **cold maceration** (also called cold soak). One Italian producer describes a 24–48 hour cryomaceration before fermentation; in research literature, cold maceration at 4–8°C is described as promoting anthocyanin stability and more selective extraction of phenolics from skins and seeds (MDPI, *Beverages*, 2025). The **primary alcoholic fermentation** then runs five to fourteen days, depending on style. Yeast (either native or inoculated) converts sugars into ethanol and CO₂. As fermentation proceeds, the skins float to the top of the tank, forming what California winemakers call a "cap" — a layer of skins and seeds that must be mixed back through the liquid by *punch-down* (a paddle pushing the cap down) or *pump-over* (juice pumped from below and sprayed over the top).

After fermentation, the wine is **pressed** — separated from the solids — and the resulting young wine is moved to a second container. A **malolactic fermentation** usually follows, in which bacteria (typically *Oenococcus oeni*) convert the sharp malic acid in the wine into the much softer lactic acid (Wine Enthusiast, 2023). Almost all red wines and many premium whites go through malolactic. The maturation phase — French winemakers call it *élevage* ("raising" or "upbringing") — can last from a few months to many years (MasterClass, 2026), in oak barrels, concrete, stainless steel, amphorae, or some combination. Finally the wine is racked, fined, filtered (or not), and bottled, where it may continue to evolve for years to decades.

### White wines

For most white wines, the grapes are pressed *before* fermentation, separating the juice from the skins almost immediately to avoid colour and tannin extraction (Boroli, 2024). The clear juice is then fermented at cool temperatures (10–18°C) to preserve aromatic compounds. Whites can be fermented in stainless steel (preserving bright, fresh character) or in oak barrels (adding richness, vanilla, and texture).

Treatment of the lees — the dead yeast cells left after fermentation — is one of the most important style decisions. "Lees stirring" (*bâtonnage*) adds creamy, savoury complexity, classic in white Burgundy. Many premium whites go through malolactic fermentation, which is what gives Chardonnays their famous buttery character: diacetyl, a byproduct of the bacterial conversion, smells like melted butter and reads as creaminess in wine (Wine Enthusiast).

### Rosé, sparkling, and other styles

Rosés are typically made by either brief skin contact with red grapes (a few hours, the *saignée* or "bleeding" method, where some juice is run off after a short maceration) or by direct pressing of red grapes. Sparkling wines either undergo a second fermentation in the bottle (the traditional method used in Champagne, Cava, and high-end English sparkling) or in pressurised tanks (the Charmat method used for Prosecco). Sweet wines are made by stopping fermentation before all the sugar is consumed, by adding spirit to fortify (as in Port), or by concentrating sugars in the grape — through late harvest, dehydration (*appassimento* for Amarone), freezing (ice wine), or noble rot (*Botrytis cinerea*, as in Sauternes and Tokaji).

### Oak: the most important non-grape ingredient

Wine has been aged in oak for over 2,000 years, since the Roman Empire replaced clay amphorae with wooden barrels for practical and sensory reasons (MDPI, *Beverages*, 2025). Two oak species dominate today: American white oak (*Quercus alba*) and European oak (mainly *Quercus robur* and *Quercus petraea* from France). American oak — accounting for roughly 45% of the standing white oak east of the Rocky Mountains (Iowa State Extension) — gives bolder vanilla, coconut, and dill flavors. French oak imparts more subtle spice, toasted bread, and cedar.

Barrels are **toasted** over fire during construction (cooperage), and the toast level — light, medium, or heavy — determines how much of the wood's flavour compounds reach the wine. Toasting also creates aromatic compounds: vanillin from lignin breakdown, furfural from sugars, and various pyrazines. Even the source forest matters: Tronçais, Limousin, Allier, and Vosges are recognised French oak sources, each with subtly different profiles. New barrels impart the strongest flavours; older barrels ("neutral oak") allow micro-oxygenation through the wood pores without adding much extra flavour. The percentage of new oak versus used is a critical winemaking decision.

## Where wine flavors come from

When you read "black cherry, violet, leather, tobacco" on a wine label, those aren't poetic licence. They map onto specific molecules. Wine chemistry has been intensively studied for decades, and the major aroma compound families are well characterised. Crucially, *nothing is added* — these compounds come from grapes, from yeast fermentation, from bacteria, from oak, and from the slow chemical reactions that occur over years of aging.

### Esters: the fruity layer

Esters are organic compounds formed when alcohols react with acids, principally during fermentation. They are responsible for most of the fresh fruity, floral, and sweet aromas in young wines (EverWonderWine). Specific esters have specific signatures:

- **Isoamyl acetate** — banana and pear-drop candy (a defining note in Beaujolais Nouveau).
- **Ethyl hexanoate** — pineapple, apple, pear.
- **Phenylethyl acetate** — rose, honey, floral.
- **Ethyl acetate** — fruity in tiny amounts, nail-polish-remover fault at higher levels.

Esters are unique in the wine-aroma family because, as fermentation specialist Michael Jones of Scott Labs has noted in *SevenFifty Daily* (2021), they tell us essentially nothing about a wine's origin — they smell wonderful but are unstable, peak at end of fermentation, and degrade almost entirely within the first year of a wine's life, leaving no long-term imprint. Every other major wine aroma compound — terpenes, thiols, pyrazines, norisoprenoids — originates in the grape and reflects the interaction between variety and place.

### Terpenes: citrus, rose, and the Riesling fingerprint

Terpenes (and the closely related norisoprenoids) reside primarily in grape skins and are abundant elsewhere in nature — in citrus peels, in conifers, in herbs (Wine Enthusiast, 2023). In wine, the major terpenes include:

- **Linalool** — floral, citrus, classic in Muscat, Gewürztraminer, and Riesling.
- **Geraniol** — rose petal.
- **Citronellol** — lemon and citronella.
- **Beta-ionone** (a norisoprenoid) — the haunting scent of violets in Pinot Noir and Syrah.
- **Beta-damascenone** (a norisoprenoid ketone) — floral aromas with red-fruit undertow.

This is why a Riesling can smell remarkably like lime peel: the underlying chemistry is genuinely similar. Aromatic varieties (Riesling, Muscat, Gewürztraminer, Viognier, Torrontés) are aromatic because they are terpene-rich. Pinot Noir's perfume comes substantially from norisoprenoids.

### Thiols: tropical fruit and the Sauvignon Blanc signature

Thiols are sulfur-containing compounds with extraordinary detection thresholds — perceptible at parts per trillion. They can both make and break a wine. In small concentrations they deliver tropical, fruity character (EverWonderWine):

- **3-mercaptohexanol (3MH)** — grapefruit, passion fruit, boxwood; defines Sauvignon Blanc, especially from Marlborough, New Zealand.
- **4-mercapto-4-methyl-2-pentanone (4MMP)** — cat pee / blackcurrant bud / tropical, also Sauvignon Blanc.

At higher concentrations or with the wrong sulfur compounds, thiols become faults — rotten egg, struck match, garlic. Pinot Noir and Malbec carry notable thiol expressions described as smoky and spice-cabinet-like, which is part of why both pair so well with grilled meats (Wine Cooler Direct).

### Pyrazines: green pepper and the Cabernet signature

Methoxypyrazines are responsible for the green-bell-pepper, asparagus, and herbaceous character of under-ripe Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc, Sauvignon Blanc, and Carmenère. Specifically, 2-methoxy-3-isobutylpyrazine (IBMP) is the molecule. In properly ripe Bordeaux Cabernet, it sits in the background as a structural element; in cool-climate or under-ripe versions it dominates (sometimes pleasantly, sometimes not).

### Compounds from oak and aging

Once wine enters oak, a new vocabulary of compounds enters:

- **Vanillin** — from lignin breakdown during toasting, gives vanilla aroma.
- **Whisky lactones** (cis- and trans-β-methyl-γ-octalactone) — coconut, dill, particularly prominent in American oak.
- **Eugenol** — clove and spice.
- **Furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural** — caramel, almond, toast (from heat-driven sugar reactions in the wood).
- **Guaiacol and 4-ethylguaiacol** — smoke, bacon, leather.

Diacetyl, mentioned earlier as the butter note from malolactic fermentation, is another key non-grape-derived compound. When malolactic-fermented Chardonnay sits in new American oak, the combination of buttery diacetyl with sweet vanillin can produce the buttered-popcorn flavour profile that defines (and divides opinion on) many California Chardonnays.

### Tertiary aromas: time in bottle

As wine ages in bottle, fresh primary fruit fades and a new set of "tertiary" aromas emerges through slow chemical evolution: leather, tobacco, dried fruit, forest floor, truffle, petroleum (the famous TDN — 1,1,6-trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene — that develops in aged Riesling), mushroom, and game. These come from the integration and breakdown of earlier compounds plus slow micro-oxidation through the cork. This is why some wines reward years or decades of aging: they're not just "older" — they're chemically transformed.

## Terroir: the original concept

Wine is where terroir was invented as an idea. The word *terroir* was first used in Burgundy in the 1930s as a marketing tool to differentiate wines (Nature Communications, 2022). It has since spread to coffee, cheese, cocoa, and other fermented or place-driven products — but wine remains the most rigorous and codified expression.

The International Organisation of Vine and Wine (OIV) defines terroir as an area where collective knowledge of interactions between the physical environment, the biological environment, and human practices combine to give products distinctive characteristics tied to that place. As one author at *ScienceInsights* put it bluntly: a Burgundy doesn't taste like a California wine even when both are Pinot Noir, and that is what terroir is.

### Soil

French experts traditionally emphasize soil above all other terroir factors. Great wines come from a wide diversity of soil types, as catalogued by the journal *OENO One* (2018): gravelly Quaternary alluvium in Pauillac, Bordeaux; clayey lime-rich Jurassic limestone in Mazis-Chambertin, Burgundy; heavy clay on Paleogene substratum in Saint-Émilion, Bordeaux.

Common factors across these celebrated soils, Westgarth Wines notes, are moderate but adequate nutrient levels, a balanced water supply that curbs excessive vine growth, and optimal drainage to prevent waterlogging. The classic examples include:

- **Médoc (Bordeaux Left Bank)** — deep stony-gravelly sands with excellent drainage, producing Cabernet Sauvignon with depth and intensity.
- **Pomerol (Bordeaux Right Bank)** — heavy clay-based soils, producing full-bodied Merlot-driven wines.
- **Burgundy Grand Crus** — limestone and clay in marly soils, lending mineral quality and structure.
- **Champagne and Chablis** — chalky soils derived from a Cretaceous seabed, ideal drainage and a key contributor to the saline minerality of the wines.
- **Mosel (Germany)** — slate-based soils on steep slopes that absorb heat by day and release it at night, essential for ripening Riesling in a cool climate.

Soil affects vines primarily by regulating water supply. The best terroirs maintain a delicate water balance — enough for vine health, not so much that vigour explodes and dilutes fruit concentration. In Burgundy, the empirical pattern is that the highest-quality wines come from the middle and upper slopes; lower-slope colluvial soils are deeper and richer, but produce lower-quality wines (OENO One, 2018).

### Climate and topography

Climate sets the broad envelope. Warm climates (Napa Valley, Barossa Valley, Mendoza, southern Italy) produce wines with riper fruit, higher alcohol, and softer acidity. Cool climates (Burgundy, Mosel, Champagne, Marlborough, Central Otago) produce wines with delicate flavours, higher acidity, and lower alcohol (Hope Family Wines).

The most important single climatic variable for quality is **diurnal variation** — the swing between warm days and cool nights. Warm days drive sugar accumulation and flavour development; cool nights preserve acidity. Regions famous for this swing — Mendoza, Washington's Columbia Valley, Coonawarra, the Andes generally — consistently produce wines with both ripeness and freshness.

Topography concentrates climate. Slope aspect (which direction it faces) determines sun exposure. Altitude lowers temperature (about 0.6°C per 100m), allowing high-altitude vineyards in warm countries to produce cooler-climate-style wines. In Burgundy's Côte d'Or, the Grand Cru vineyards sit on east- and southeast-facing mid-slopes — capturing morning sun, sheltered from wind, with the slope itself providing drainage and frost protection (Westgarth Wines).

### Microbial terroir: a new frontier

Recent research has expanded terroir into the microbial dimension. A landmark 2016 paper in *mBio* (Bokulich et al.) demonstrated that different grape-growing regions of California have distinct, identifiable microbial patterns correlated with local weather conditions, and that these patterns can be distinguished even between adjacent vineyards within Napa and Sonoma. A 2022 global survey of vineyard soils in *Communications Biology* found that vineyard microbiota correlate with biogeographical patterns and contribute to vine growth, grape quality, and winemaking outcomes.

In other words: the yeast and bacteria living on grape skins and in vineyard soils may themselves be region-specific contributors to wine flavour. A growing body of work suggests this is the underappreciated fourth pillar of terroir, alongside soil, climate, and human practice.

### Empirical proof: terroir is now measurable

A 2025 study in *npj Science of Food* tested 75 French sparkling wines from Champagne and Burgundy using isotopic ratios of strontium — an element absorbed from bedrock into the vine. The technique achieved 100% accuracy classifying wines by region. Researchers also identified rubidium as a cheaper alternative marker, maintaining over 90% accuracy at 75% lower analytical cost. Terroir is no longer just philosophy; you can verify it with a mass spectrometer.

## Major wine regions

### France: Burgundy

Burgundy is wine's most extreme expression of terroir. The region is a thin north-south strip of eastern France with a continental climate — hot summers, cold winters — and limestone-marl soils that lend a particular mineral character to its wines (Liquid Kosher, 2024). Burgundy makes essentially two wines: red, from Pinot Noir, and white, from Chardonnay (with small amounts of Aligoté, Gamay, and others).

What sets Burgundy apart is fragmentation. Burgundian winegrowers classified their vineyards into *climats* — micro-terroirs — from very early on. There are now 1,463 official climats (Société Générale Private Banking). A single hill can have dozens of different named vineyards, owned by dozens of different growers, all producing distinguishable wines from the same grape variety. The hierarchy runs: Régional → Village → Premier Cru → Grand Cru, with the Grand Cru status reserved for just 33 vineyards in the Côte d'Or, the heart of Burgundy (Wine Folly).

This obsession with site comes partly from history: after the phylloxera aphid devastated French vineyards in the 1870s, Burgundian winegrowers only replanted the best terroirs, concentrating on varietal wines (Société Générale). The result is a wine region where understanding the difference between Gevrey-Chambertin and Vosne-Romanée — neighboring villages — is something serious enthusiasts spend years on.

Burgundy is also home to the most expensive wines ever sold. In 2018, a single bottle of 1945 Domaine de la Romanée-Conti sold at Sotheby's for $558,000 (Karen MacNeil, *The Wine Bible*).

### France: Bordeaux

Bordeaux is the volume and prestige counterpoint to Burgundy. Located in southwestern France with a maritime climate moderated by the Atlantic Ocean and the Gironde estuary, it produces blended red wines from primarily Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and Cabernet Franc, plus smaller plantings of Petit Verdot and Malbec (Decanter Tours, 2025).

Bordeaux divides into the Left Bank and Right Bank of the Gironde:

- **Left Bank** (Médoc, including Pauillac, Saint-Julien, Margaux, Saint-Estèphe; plus Pessac-Léognan and Graves) — gravelly soils, Cabernet Sauvignon-dominant blends, structured and age-worthy.
- **Right Bank** (Saint-Émilion, Pomerol) — clay and limestone, Merlot-dominant blends, softer and more approachable young, but still capable of decades of aging.

Bordeaux's classification dates to the 1855 Bordeaux Wine Official Classification commissioned by Napoleon III, ranking Médoc estates from First Growth (*Premier Cru*) down to Fifth Growth based on the prices their wines commanded. The five First Growths — Lafite Rothschild, Latour, Margaux, Haut-Brion, and (since 1973) Mouton Rothschild — remain among the most valuable wines in the world.

Sauternes, south of the city, makes some of the world's great sweet wines from Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc affected by noble rot. Pessac-Léognan and parts of Graves produce excellent dry whites from the same two varieties.

### France: other key regions

Beyond Burgundy and Bordeaux, France has several other world-significant regions:

- **Champagne** — chalky soils, cool climate, Chardonnay/Pinot Noir/Pinot Meunier, traditional-method sparkling.
- **Rhône Valley** — Northern Rhône for Syrah (Côte-Rôtie, Hermitage) and Viognier (Condrieu); Southern Rhône for Grenache-Syrah-Mourvèdre blends (Châteauneuf-du-Pape).
- **Loire Valley** — Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé for Sauvignon Blanc; Vouvray for Chenin Blanc; Muscadet near the Atlantic; Chinon and Bourgueil for Cabernet Franc.
- **Alsace** — single-varietal aromatic whites: Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Gris, Muscat.
- **Languedoc-Roussillon** — France's largest wine region by volume, with rapidly improving quality and a diversity of styles.

### Italy: Piedmont

Piedmont, in northwest Italy, is home to Nebbiolo — a thin-skinned, late-ripening, fiercely tannic and acidic red grape whose name likely derives from *nebbia*, the fog that rolls over the Langhe hills in autumn (Decanter). Two adjacent DOCGs — Barolo and Barbaresco — both produce 100% Nebbiolo wines of extraordinary aging potential. Barolo is required to age at least 38 months after harvest (18 in oak); Barbaresco needs 26 months (9 in oak). The two areas sit just kilometers apart but produce distinguishable wines: Barbaresco is generally considered slightly more elegant and earlier-maturing; Barolo more austere, more powerful, longer-lived (Vinodelice, 2026).

Typical Nebbiolo flavour profile: red cherry, rose petal, tar, truffle, leather, tobacco, white pepper, licorice. Top Barolos can age for decades; the modernist-vs-traditionalist debate (shorter vs. longer fermentations, smaller new oak vs. large old casks) has shaped Piedmont's last 30 years of winemaking.

### Italy: Tuscany

Tuscany's signature grape is Sangiovese, the backbone of Chianti, Brunello di Montalcino, and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. Sangiovese gives wines of high acidity and grippy tannins with flavours of sour cherry, plum, tomato leaf, dried herbs, and earth.

- **Chianti Classico** — heart of the Chianti zone, Sangiovese-dominant blends; reformed dramatically in the 1990s after years of overcropping and quality decline.
- **Brunello di Montalcino** — 100% Sangiovese (the local clone is called Brunello, "little brown one"), minimum 48 months aging including 24 in wood (Decanter). Among Italy's most prestigious wines.
- **Vino Nobile di Montepulciano** — Sangiovese-based wines from around Montepulciano, slightly more rustic than Brunello.
- **Bolgheri (Super Tuscans)** — coastal Tuscany, where producers ignored regional regulations starting in the 1970s and planted Bordeaux varieties, producing wines (Sassicaia, Ornellaia, Tignanello) that initially had to be sold as table wine but are now globally celebrated.

### Italy: other notable regions

Italy has over 500 indigenous grape varieties and 78 DOCGs (SommGeo). Other significant zones include Veneto (Amarone, Soave, Prosecco), Sicily (Etna's Nerello Mascalese, Nero d'Avola), Campania (Aglianico, Fiano, Greco di Tufo), Friuli (white wines including Friulano), and the Trentino-Alto Adige region (high-altitude Pinot Bianco, Pinot Grigio, Gewürztraminer).

### Spain

Spain has more land under vine than any other country, and its flagship grape is Tempranillo, the world's fourth most-planted variety (Wikipedia). The two heartlands are Rioja and Ribera del Duero.

Rioja sits in north-central Spain, protected from Atlantic weather by the Cantabrian Mountains. Top-quality Riojas are red-fruited, with elegant structure and a savoury, oak-influenced complexity. The region pioneered the use of American oak — which gives a more sweet, vanilla-coconut profile than French oak — and Spain's Reserva and Gran Reserva classifications enforce minimum aging requirements: Gran Reserva must age at least 24 months in oak and 36 more in bottle before release (Vivino).

Ribera del Duero, south of Rioja, has a harsher continental climate (locals describe it as "ten months of winter and two of hell") at higher altitude. The Tempranillo here — called Tinto Fino or Tinta del País — ripens more fully and produces denser, more concentrated, more black-fruited wines. Vega Sicilia in Ribera del Duero is widely considered Spain's most prestigious estate (Butterfield & Robinson).

Other Spanish regions of note: Priorat (powerful Garnacha and Cariñena from slate *llicorella* soils), Rías Baixas (Albariño whites), Jerez (Sherry — fortified, oxidatively aged in solera systems), and Cava (traditional-method sparkling from Catalonia).

### California: Napa Valley

Napa Valley is America's most celebrated wine region and the country's first AVA (American Viticultural Area), designated in 1981 (SevenFifty Daily). Despite producing only about 4% of California's wine grape harvest, Napa accounts for a disproportionate share of US wine prestige (Napa Vintners). The valley stretches 30 miles from north to south, flanked by the Mayacamas Mountains to the west and the Vaca Mountains to the east.

Cabernet Sauvignon is Napa's signature, producing wines of ripe fruit concentration and balanced acidity thanks to warm sunny days moderated by fog and breezes from San Francisco Bay. The valley has 16 official sub-appellations, each with distinct microclimates and soils: Oakville and Rutherford for benchmark Cabernet, Stags Leap District for elegance and floral notes, Howell Mountain and Spring Mountain for mountain-grown intensity, Los Carneros for cool-climate Pinot Noir and Chardonnay (Visit Napa Valley).

Napa's reputation was cemented by the 1976 "Judgment of Paris," in which a blind tasting organized by Steven Spurrier saw California Cabernets and Chardonnays beat top Bordeaux and Burgundies — a result that legitimised the New World on the global stage.

### California: Sonoma and the Central Coast

Sonoma County, west of Napa, contains 18 AVAs and over 400 wineries across a far more diverse climate range than Napa (Wine Enthusiast, 2023). The coastal Russian River Valley and Sonoma Coast are cool, fog-influenced areas excellent for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Inland zones like Alexander Valley, Knights Valley, and Dry Creek warm up for Cabernet Sauvignon and Zinfandel; Rockpile and Dry Creek Valley specialize in old-vine Zinfandel.

Further south, the Central Coast (Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo, Monterey) has emerged as a serious cool-climate region — the Santa Maria and Santa Rita Hills AVAs in particular for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, with Paso Robles to the north producing Rhône-style blends and Bordeaux varieties.

### Other significant New World regions

- **Oregon's Willamette Valley** — cool-climate Pinot Noir of restraint and finesse, often compared to Burgundy.
- **Washington State** — Columbia Valley and its sub-AVAs (Walla Walla, Red Mountain, Yakima Valley) producing some of America's best Cabernet, Merlot, and Syrah.
- **Australia** — Barossa Valley for powerhouse Shiraz; Coonawarra for terra rossa Cabernet; Margaret River for Bordeaux varieties; Hunter Valley for distinctive Semillon; Tasmania and Yarra Valley for cool-climate Pinot/Chardonnay and sparkling.
- **New Zealand** — Marlborough for thiol-driven Sauvignon Blanc that redefined what the variety could taste like; Central Otago and Martinborough for Pinot Noir.
- **Argentina** — Mendoza for high-altitude Malbec; the Uco Valley pushing into ever-higher elevations.
- **Chile** — Maipo, Colchagua, and Aconcagua for Cabernet; Casablanca and San Antonio for cool-climate whites; Itata and Bío Bío for old-vine País and Cinsault.
- **South Africa** — Stellenbosch, Swartland, and Hemel-en-Aarde producing world-class Chenin Blanc, Cabernet, Pinotage, and Pinot Noir.
- **Germany** — Mosel, Rheingau, Pfalz, Nahe for Riesling across a sweetness spectrum (Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, etc.).
- **Portugal** — Douro Valley (still and Port), Alentejo, and Vinho Verde, with many distinctive indigenous varieties.
- **Hungary, Greece, Austria, Georgia, Lebanon** — each with serious traditions and increasingly visible exports.

## Further reading and key references

If you want to build a wine library, this is the consensus best-of from sommelier programs and serious wine education.

### Books

- **Jancis Robinson and Hugh Johnson**, *The World Atlas of Wine* (8th edition, 2019). The standard reference, with detailed maps and rigorous regional coverage. The book most professionals cite as authoritative.
- **Jancis Robinson**, *The Oxford Companion to Wine* (5th edition, 2023). Encyclopedic A-Z reference covering varieties, regions, science, and history. A single-page entry on malolactic fermentation is more thorough than most magazine articles.
- **Jancis Robinson, Julia Harding, and José Vouillamoz**, *Wine Grapes* (2012). The definitive guide to grape varieties — 1,368 varieties identified — drawing on DNA evidence to trace lineages.
- **Karen MacNeil**, *The Wine Bible* (3rd edition, 2022). The single bestselling wine book in the US, over one million copies sold (Karen MacNeil). Praised for engaging writing and breadth — Robinson's books are denser, MacNeil's is more readable.
- **Hugh Johnson**, *Pocket Wine Book* (updated annually). Compact reference with vintage-by-vintage advice.
- **Andrew Jefford**, *The New France* — opinionated, lyrical writing on French regions.
- **Bianca Bosker**, *Cork Dork* — accessible, journalistic deep-dive into sommelier culture and the science of taste.

### Online resources

- **jancisrobinson.com** — Robinson's subscription site, daily content from one of the world's most authoritative critics.
- **Wine Folly** (winefolly.com) — Madeline Puckette's accessible visual guides, especially useful for grape varieties and regional maps.
- **Decanter** (decanter.com) — UK-based publication with strong regional coverage, classed-growth analysis, and tasting notes.
- **Wine Enthusiast** (wineenthusiast.com) — broad coverage, tasting database, accessible explainers on wine chemistry and regions.
- **SevenFifty Daily** (daily.sevenfifty.com) — trade-oriented, particularly strong on winemaking and industry analysis.
- **GuildSomm** (guildsomm.com) — sommelier-oriented, in-depth regional and varietal study materials.

### Peer-reviewed starting points

- Bokulich et al. (2016), "Associations among wine grape microbiome, metabolome, and fermentation behavior," *mBio* (PMC4959672).
- Mezzasalma et al. (2022), "A global microbiome survey of vineyard soils," *Communications Biology* (PMC8933554).
- van Leeuwen et al. (2018), "Soil-related terroir factors: a review," *OENO One* (oeno-one.eu).
- Tarapoulouzi et al. (2025), Sr/Rb isotopic terroir classification, *npj Science of Food*.
- Pérez-Magariño et al. (2025), "Red wine aging in oak barrels: toasting levels, aromatics, and phenolics," *Beverages* (MDPI).

### Practical exercise

To put this into practice with the same approach suggested for coffee: buy four wines from the same retailer — a Burgundy Pinot Noir, an Oregon Pinot Noir, a Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc, and a Sancerre (Loire Sauvignon Blanc). Same variety across two pairs, different terroirs. Taste them blind, side by side, using a wine aroma wheel for vocabulary. The differences will map directly onto everything in this document: variety, climate, soil, processing.
