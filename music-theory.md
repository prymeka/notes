# A Curriculum in Music Theory

*Balanced depth: notation literacy → functional harmony → light score analysis, with minimal counterpoint. Light, mostly-conceptual ear training alongside. Common-practice Western tonal idiom. A compact vocal/singing-pedagogy addendum at the end.*

---

## Preface

### What this subject is, and where it sits

This curriculum gives you the conceptual and notational system behind Western tonal music: how pitch and rhythm are written, how the expressive markings work, how chords are built and how they function in succession, and how phrases and forms are assembled. It is the layer your performance book assumed but did not teach — the framework in which a term like *diminuendo* or *legato* or *cadence* has a precise, systematic meaning.

It connects to two things you already have under way. It is the sibling of your **prosody** work: both study how sound is patterned in time, and the two meet directly in song (text-stress against musical-stress), which is flagged as a bridge at the end. And its closing **vocal** addendum dovetails with your accent and voice-science work — phonation, breath, resonance, and formants are the same machinery viewed from the singing side.

### The one caveat to hold from day one

"Music theory," as taught in every standard textbook including the ones below, is **not a set of universal laws of music**. It is a *descriptive grammar of a specific repertoire* — roughly European art music c. 1650–1900 ("common-practice tonality"). Its "rules" (resolve the leading tone, avoid parallel fifths, a dominant wants to go to a tonic) are **stylistic norms of that idiom**, routinely and deliberately broken in folk, jazz, pop, pre-1650 modal music, post-1900 art music, and most non-Western traditions. Hold "theory" the way you are learning to hold foot-scansion in prosody: a powerful, near-universal *convention* that describes a body of practice — not a fact about all music. Wherever a book says music "must" or "cannot," mentally append: *in the common-practice style*.

### The tracks

Run them together, weighted as you specified:

1. **Theory & Analysis** (primary) — reading the concepts; working written exercises; light score analysis once harmony is in place.
2. **Ear & Listening** (light) — mostly conceptual, with guided listening: hearing major vs minor, recognising cadences, catching the expressive markings by ear. Driven mainly by free browser trainers, not heavy dictation.
3. **Keyboard** (light, supporting) — a piano keyboard (even a cheap MIDI controller or a free on-screen one) is the single best aid for *seeing* intervals, scales, and chords. You are not learning to play; you are using the keyboard as a diagram you can hear. Optional but high-leverage.

### A genuine bridge from your existing work

The music-information-retrieval material you have studied is, in effect, the computational extraction of exactly the objects in this curriculum: **chroma** features are the twelve pitch-classes of Phase 3; **beat-tracking and tempograms** operate on the metre of Phase 2; **key- and chord-detection** estimate the harmony of Phases 4–5; **HPSS** separates the harmonic from the percussive. You have been measuring these structures from the signal; here you learn them from the inside. Your physics background also makes the acoustic layer (the harmonic series, frequency ratios, equal temperament) immediately legible.

### Tools & notation-software note

A free notation editor — **MuseScore** (museScore.org) — lets you enter examples and hear them back, which turns silent theory into sound. Recommended from Phase 0. For ear training, the free browser trainers below do most of the work.

## Phase 0 — Orientation & notation literacy

**Goal.** Read the basic written language of music: the staff, clefs and the grand staff, note names, the piano-keyboard layout, note/rest values, simple time signatures, basic rhythm, and accidentals (sharp/flat/natural). Be able to read a simple melody on paper and locate its notes on a keyboard.

**Prerequisites.** None.

**Primary readings.**
- Kostka/Payne, the opening chapter(s) on elements of pitch and rhythm; **or** *Alfred's Essentials* Bk 1; **or** Open Music Theory's "Fundamentals" unit. (Use one as spine; the others as cross-check.)
- AB Guide Part I, opening sections (staff, clefs, note values, time signatures).
- musictheory.net "Basics" lessons.

**Supplementary.**
- Gardner Read, *Music Notation: A Manual of Modern Practice*, 2nd ed. (Crescendo, 1979) — the notation reference of record. [Reference/advanced]

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Name notes on treble and bass staves (musictheory.net "Note Identification"); write a C-major scale; notate a simple given rhythm.
- *Ear/Listening*: Distinguish higher/lower pitch; tap the steady beat of recordings and feel where it groups (twos vs threes).
- *Keyboard*: Find every C, then all white keys, then locate the black-key groups; play a one-octave C-major scale.

**Minimum viable path.** One fundamentals source's notation chapter + musictheory.net Basics; the note-naming and scale-writing exercises.

**Time estimate.** ~2–3 weeks.

## Phase 1 — The expressive vocabulary (dynamics, tempo, articulation)

**Goal.** Master the markings that govern *how* music is performed without changing its pitches or rhythms — the layer that brought you here. This is mostly vocabulary, so it is conceptually light but worth front-loading for the immediate payoff: after this phase you can read the performance directions on almost any score.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0 (you read these terms on a staff).

**Primary readings.**
- **AB Guide Part I & II, the sections on tempo, dynamics, articulation, and "terms and signs."** The best systematic coverage; ABRSM drills exactly these.
- Oxford Dictionary of Music — for any term not in the guide.

**The vocabulary to command (grouped).**
- *Dynamics (loudness):* `pp pianissimo`, `p piano`, `mp mezzo-piano`, `mf mezzo-forte`, `f forte`, `ff fortissimo` (and the extremes `ppp`/`fff`); change over time — `crescendo` (cresc., or the opening hairpin) getting louder, **`diminuendo` / `decrescendo`** (dim., or the closing hairpin) getting softer; sudden accent on one note — `sforzando` (sfz/sf), `forte-piano` (fp); `subito` (suddenly), `poco a poco` (little by little).
- *Tempo (speed):* slow to fast — `grave`, `largo`, `lento`, `adagio`, `andante`, `moderato`, `allegretto`, `allegro`, `vivace`, `presto`, `prestissimo`; change — `accelerando` (speeding up), `ritardando`/`rallentando` (slowing down), `a tempo` (back to the original speed), `rubato` (expressive give-and-take in timing), `fermata` (a held pause); the metronome mark (e.g. ♩ = 120 beats per minute).
- *Articulation (how notes are attacked/connected):* `legato` (smooth, connected — shown by a slur), `staccato` (short, detached — a dot), `staccatissimo` (very short), `tenuto` (held to full value — a line), `marcato` (marked/stressed), the `accent` (>), the `slur` vs the `tie` (a slur connects different pitches smoothly; a tie joins two notes of the *same* pitch into one duration).
- *Common character/expression words (often Italian/German/French):* `dolce` (sweetly), `cantabile` (singingly), `espressivo`, `con brio` (with spirit), `con moto` (with motion), `maestoso` (majestic), `grazioso`, `agitato`, `tranquillo`; German e.g. `langsam` (slow), `schnell` (fast), `mit Ausdruck` (with expression); French e.g. `lent` (slow), `vif` (lively), `doux` (soft/sweet).

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Build a one-page glossary in your own words. Take any score (free at IMSLP.org) and translate *every* non-note marking on the first page.
- *Ear/Listening*: With a recording and score together, mark where you *hear* the crescendos, the ritardando, the staccato vs legato. The point is to attach the term to the sound.

**Minimum viable path.** AB Guide's tempo/dynamics/articulation sections + the score-translation exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~2 weeks.

## Phase 2 — Rhythm & metre in depth

**Goal.** Understand how time is organised: simple vs compound metre, duple/triple/quadruple, beaming and dotted rhythms, ties, rests, syncopation, and tuplets. Be able to read and clap moderately complex rhythms and identify a piece's metre.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–1.

**Primary readings.**
- Kostka/Payne, the rhythm-and-metre chapter; Open Music Theory's metre unit.
- AB Guide Part I on time signatures (simple and compound).

**Concepts.** Beat, subdivision, and grouping; simple metre (beat divides in two: 2/4, 3/4, 4/4) vs compound metre (beat divides in three: 6/8, 9/8, 12/8); the difference between 6/8 and 3/4; common time (C) and cut time (¢); dotted notes and ties; syncopation (accent off the expected beat); tuplets (triplets, etc.); anacrusis (upbeat).

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Re-bar a given rhythm from 3/4 into 6/8 and explain what changes. Identify simple vs compound for ten excerpts.
- *Ear/Listening*: Tap the beat and the subdivision of recordings; decide duple vs triple by ear; clap-back short rhythms (teoria.com / musictheory.net rhythm trainers). *(This is exactly the level beat-tracking and tempograms operate on, computed rather than felt.)*

**Minimum viable path.** One rhythm chapter + the simple-vs-compound ear exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~2–3 weeks.

## Phase 3 — Pitch, intervals, scales & keys

**Goal.** Command the pitch system: intervals (by number and quality), the major and the three minor scales, the circle of fifths and all key signatures, and a conceptual grasp of the modes. This is the foundation for all harmony.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–2.

**Primary readings.**
- Kostka/Payne, chapters on scales, key signatures, and intervals.
- musictheory.net lessons on intervals, scales, and the circle of fifths (plus their trainers).

**Concepts.** The chromatic scale and the twelve pitch-classes; intervals = *number* (2nd, 3rd … octave) + *quality* (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished); consonance vs dissonance; enharmonic equivalents (F♯ = G♭); the major scale (W-W-H-W-W-W-H); the three minor scales (natural, harmonic, melodic) and why the harmonic minor exists (to raise the leading tone); the circle of fifths and how key signatures are generated; the diatonic modes (Ionian/Dorian/Phrygian/Lydian/Mixolydian/Aeolian/Locrian) — introduced conceptually. *(The twelve pitch-classes here are precisely what chroma features encode; the circle of fifths is the geometry behind key-detection.)*

**Acoustic aside (optional, suits your background).** Consonant intervals correspond to simple frequency ratios (octave 2:1, perfect fifth 3:2); the major triad sits low in the harmonic series. This has real acoustic basis — but see the Evidence note on *not* over-reading it as proof that tonality is "natural."

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Spell every major and (natural/harmonic/melodic) minor scale; identify intervals above a given note; write key signatures from the circle of fifths.
- *Ear/Listening (light)*: Major vs minor (the single most useful aural distinction); recognise a handful of signpost intervals (octave, perfect fifth, perhaps using song mnemonics). Keep this light — recognition, not exhaustive dictation. The **Functional Ear Trainer** app (free) is excellent for hearing scale degrees in a key. [Free]
- *Keyboard*: Play the major and minor scales; play and hear each interval from middle C.

**Minimum viable path.** Intervals + major/minor scales + circle of fifths; the scale-spelling exercise; major-vs-minor by ear.

**Time estimate.** ~4–5 weeks (the densest fundamentals phase).

## Phase 4 — Chords: triads & sevenths

**Goal.** Build and identify chords: the four triad qualities and their inversions, the principal seventh chords, and the three notations you will meet (figured bass, Roman numerals, and lead-sheet chord symbols).

**Prerequisites.** Phase 3 (intervals and scales).

**Primary readings.**
- Kostka/Payne, chapters on triads, seventh chords, and inversions; Open Music Theory's chords unit.

**Concepts.** Triads = stacked thirds: major, minor, diminished, augmented; root position vs first/second inversion; the diatonic triads of a key and their Roman numerals (I ii iii IV V vi vii°); seventh chords (especially the **dominant seventh, V7**, plus the diatonic seventh chords); the three notations — *figured bass* (Baroque shorthand: 6, 6/4, 7…), *Roman numerals* (function within a key — the analytic default here), and *lead-sheet symbols* (Cmaj7, Dm7, G7 — the popular/jazz convention). *(Chord-detection systems estimate exactly these objects from audio.)*

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Spell all four triad qualities on every note; write the diatonic triads of three keys with Roman numerals; identify a chord's quality and inversion from notation.
- *Ear/Listening (light)*: Distinguish major / minor / diminished triads by ear; hear root-position vs inverted as "stable vs leaning" (light touch).
- *Keyboard*: Build triads and a V7 in several keys; hear the difference between qualities.

**Minimum viable path.** Triad qualities + inversions + the dominant seventh + Roman-numeral labelling; the triad-spelling exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~3–4 weeks.

## Phase 5 — Diatonic harmony & function

**Goal.** The heart of the curriculum: how chords *function* and connect. Understand tonic/subdominant/dominant function, common diatonic progressions, the cadences, harmonic rhythm, nonchord tones, and the *basic* voice-leading norms. This is "harmony" proper.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 3–4.

**Primary readings.**
- Kostka/Payne, the core harmony chapters: diatonic chord function, harmonic progression, cadences, nonchord tones, and part-writing principles.
- Open Music Theory's voice-leading and harmony units.

**Concepts.**
- *Function:* tonic (I, rest/home), dominant (V, tension wanting to resolve to I), subdominant/pre-dominant (IV, ii, departure); the basic phrase model T–PD–D–T.
- *Cadences (harmonic punctuation):* perfect authentic (V–I, both root position, soprano on tonic — the strong full stop), imperfect authentic (weaker V–I), half cadence (ending on V — a comma), plagal (IV–I — the "Amen"), deceptive (V–vi — the thwarted resolution).
- *Nonchord tones:* passing tone, neighbour tone, suspension, appoggiatura, anticipation — the notes that create melodic motion against the harmony.
- *Voice leading (the norms):* move voices smoothly and independently; **avoid parallel fifths and octaves** — but learn this explicitly as a *stylistic convention* of common-practice part-writing, not a universal prohibition (it is broken constantly elsewhere; see Evidence note).
- *Minimal counterpoint (as scoped):* just the core idea — two or more melodically independent voices combined — plus a taste of first-species (note-against-note) two-voice writing, enough to see *why* the parallel-fifths norm exists. Full species counterpoint is left to "Beyond."

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Roman-numeral analyse short progressions; harmonise a simple given melody with I, IV, V (and V7); label the cadence at each phrase end; identify nonchord tones in an excerpt.
- *Ear/Listening (light)*: **Recognise cadence types by ear** — high-value functional hearing (does this phrase end "open" on V or "closed" on I?); hear a suspension resolve.
- *Keyboard*: Play I–IV–V7–I in a few keys; play the four cadence types and hear their different "punctuation."

**Minimum viable path.** Function (T/PD/D) + the four main cadences + V7 resolution + the parallel-fifths norm; the melody-harmonisation and cadence-labelling exercises; cadence recognition by ear.

**Time estimate.** ~6–8 weeks (the central phase; do not rush it).

## Phase 6 — Phrase, form & light analysis

**Goal.** Move above the chord to musical architecture, then put the whole system to work in light analysis. Understand motive/phrase/period/sentence and the standard forms, and be able to analyse a short piece for its harmony, cadences, and form.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–5.

**Primary readings.**
- Kostka/Payne, the chapters on phrase structure and form.
- Douglass Green, *Form in Tonal Music*, 2nd ed. (Holt, 1979) — clear standard on forms. [Supplementary]

**Concepts.** Motive, phrase, and the cadence as phrase-punctuation; the **period** (antecedent + consequent) and the **sentence** (presentation + continuation + cadence); standard forms in overview — binary (AB), ternary (ABA), theme and variations, rondo (ABACA…), minuet-and-trio, and **sonata form** (exposition–development–recapitulation) at the conceptual level; the **12-bar blues** as a popular touchstone that shows function in a non-classical guise.

**Light analysis (the payoff).** With harmony in hand, analyse:
- A **Bach chorale** phrase — the canonical harmony-analysis corpus: *371 Harmonized Chorales*, ed. Albert Riemenschneider (Schirmer). Label chords with Roman numerals and identify the cadences.
- A short **character piece** — e.g. a movement from Schumann's *Album for the Young* or a Bach minuet (free at IMSLP) — for form, cadences, and how the expressive markings (Phase 1) shape it.

**Exercises.**
- *Theory*: Roman-numeral analyse one chorale phrase; diagram the form of one short piece (label sections, find the cadences that articulate them).
- *Ear/Listening*: Follow a short piece and try to *hear* the sections return and the cadences land.

**Minimum viable path.** Period vs sentence + binary/ternary + sonata-form concept; one chorale-phrase analysis and one short-piece form diagram.

**Time estimate.** ~4–5 weeks.

---

## Phase 7 — Capstone

**Goal.** Integrate everything on one or two complete short pieces.

**Prerequisites.** All prior phases.

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Take one short complete piece (a chorale plus a simple character piece) and produce a full read: key and metre, a Roman-numeral harmonic analysis with every cadence labelled, the form diagrammed, the nonchord tones identified, and a paragraph on how the dynamic/tempo/articulation markings serve the structure.
- *Listening*: Choose one piece and follow the full score while listening, two or three times, until you can hear the harmony and form you analysed on paper.
- *(Optional) Keyboard/MuseScore*: Harmonise a simple eight-bar melody of your own and hear it back.

**Time estimate.** ~2–3 weeks.

## Addendum — Vocal & singing pedagogy (light; for further reading)

*A compact closing unit, as scoped: core terminology, a little theory and practice, and a further-reading list. It connects directly to your accent and voice-science work — phonation, breath, resonance, and formants are the same apparatus from the singing side, so treat this as the musical face of material you have partly met.*

**Core terminology to know.**
- *Registers:* chest voice (modal), head voice, the **mixed/middle** register, falsetto, and (high, rare) whistle register — perceptual/functional labels for laryngeal and resonance behaviour, **not** literal claims about where the sound resonates (see caution).
- *The passaggio:* the transition zone(s) between registers (primo and secondo passaggio) — the technical crux of classical singing.
- *Vibrato:* the slight, regular pitch/intensity oscillation of a freely produced tone.
- *Tessitura vs range:* range is the full span of notes possible; tessitura is where a part *sits* comfortably and persistently.
- *Fach / voice types:* soprano, mezzo-soprano, contralto; tenor, baritone, bass — classification by range, tessitura, and timbre.
- *Resonance & placement; the singer's formant:* the clustering of upper resonances (~2.8–3.4 kHz) that lets a voice carry over an orchestra — **a directly acoustic, signal-level phenomenon** you can connect to your formant knowledge.
- *Breath support (appoggio); onset* (coordinated vs glottal vs breathy); *messa di voce* (a controlled crescendo–diminuendo on one sustained note — the classic control exercise); *legato* line and *portamento*; *vowel modification* across the range.

**A little theory + practice (kept light, with a safety note).** A few safe, low-strain exercises: gentle humming and lip-trills to warm up; **sirens/glides** smoothly through the passaggio (no push at the transitions); sustained single vowels working toward *messa di voce*; legato five-note scales on movable-do solfège. *Stop at the first sign of strain.* (Your accent/voice work already flagged throat soreness with sustained loud use — the same sustainability principles apply: support from the breath, never force at the larynx.)

**Further reading.**
- **Richard Miller, *The Structure of Singing: System and Art in Vocal Technique* (Schirmer, 1986).** The standard scholarly-yet-practical pedagogy text. The anchor.
- Richard Miller, *On the Art of Singing* (Oxford UP, 1996) — accessible essays.
- James McKinney, *The Diagnosis and Correction of Vocal Faults* (rev. ed., Waveland) — practical, problem-oriented classic.
- **Scott McCoy, *Your Voice: An Inside View*, 3rd ed. (Inside View Press, 2019).** Modern voice science + pedagogy with clear anatomy; the best bridge to your physiology and accent interests.
- Johan Sundberg, *The Science of the Singing Voice* (Northern Illinois UP, 1987) — the classic acoustic study (formants, the singer's formant). [Signal/acoustics-friendly]
- Ingo Titze, *Principles of Voice Production* (NCVS, 2000) — the voice-science reference (biomechanics and acoustics of phonation). [Advanced; directly in your signal-processing/physiology wheelhouse]

**Time estimate.** ~1–2 weeks (reading + light practice).

## Minimum viable curriculum (the ~80% path)

If time is short, this skeleton delivers most of the conceptual yield:

1. **Notation** — one fundamentals source + musictheory.net Basics.
2. **Expressive vocabulary** — AB Guide's tempo/dynamics/articulation sections + the score-translation exercise. *(This alone resolves the question that started all this.)*
3. **Intervals, scales, keys** — intervals + major/minor + circle of fifths; major-vs-minor by ear.
4. **Chords** — triad qualities + inversions + the dominant seventh, with Roman numerals.
5. **Function & cadences** — T/PD/D + the four cadences + V7 resolution; harmonise a simple melody; recognise cadences by ear.
6. **One analysis** — Roman-numeral analyse a single Bach chorale phrase and label its cadences.

This omits deep rhythm, full form study, and the capstone — all worth returning to, none deleted.

## Evidence quality — what is settled, contested, and overstated

Per your usual preference, an explicit calibration:

- **"Music theory" = universal law** *(the central overstatement).* Standard theory is a *descriptive grammar of common-practice tonal repertoire*, not the rules of all music. Its prohibitions (parallel fifths, unresolved leading tones) are stylistic norms of that idiom and are routine elsewhere. Treat theory as style-grammar, exactly as you treat foot-scansion in prosody.
- **The harmonic series "explains" tonality** *(partly true, widely overstated).* Consonance has a real acoustic basis (simple ratios; the major triad low in the series). But the leap from there to "the major scale and functional tonality are natural/universal" does not follow — tonality is substantially a cultural construction. Be sceptical of "physics proves tonality" claims.
- **Equal temperament as "in tune"** *(useful simplification).* The 12-tone equal temperament you will assume is a compromise; just-intonation intervals differ measurably, and historical temperaments matter for early music. The piano is, by design, slightly and evenly "out of tune."
- **Fixed-do vs movable-do solfège** *(genuine pedagogical split).* Movable-do trains *function* (scale-degree hearing) and suits tonal ear training; fixed-do (note-names) is standard in France, Italy, and Russia and suits score-reading and atonal work. Neither is "correct." This curriculum's light ear-track leans movable-do for functional hearing.
- **Absolute (perfect) pitch** *(largely a non-target).* It is essentially not acquirable to a genuine standard in adulthood; **relative pitch** is the trainable and far more useful skill, and is what ear training develops. Do not chase perfect pitch.
- **Roman-numeral analysis is one lens** *(not the only truth).* Figured bass, Riemannian function theory (T/S/D), and Schenkerian reduction are alternative or complementary frameworks. Roman numerals are the pedagogical default used here, not the last word.
- **Vocal folk-claims** *(addendum caution).* "Sing from your diaphragm" is anatomically loose (the diaphragm's controlled role is on *inhalation*; "support"/appoggio is real but defined variably across schools); "chest/head voice" are functional-perceptual labels, not literal resonance locations; register terminology is genuinely inconsistent between teachers and traditions. Read pedagogy comparatively, not dogmatically.

## Beyond this curriculum

Natural continuations, none required:

- **Full counterpoint** — the species method you only tasted here: Johann Joseph Fux, *The Study of Counterpoint* (*Gradus ad Parnassum*, 1725), trans. Alfred Mann (Norton, 1965); then Peter Schubert (*Modal Counterpoint*) or Kent Kennan (*Counterpoint*) for tonal/18th-century practice.
- **Deeper analysis** — chromatic harmony (the later Kostka/Payne chapters); William Caplin, *Classical Form* / *Analyzing Classical Form* (OUP) for the formal-functions approach; and, for the structural-reduction lens, Tom Pankhurst's *SchenkerGUIDE* → Forte & Gilbert, *Introduction to Schenkerian Analysis*.
- **Other idioms** — jazz/popular harmony (Mark Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book*; Dariusz Terefenko, *Jazz Theory*), and 20th-century/post-tonal theory (Joseph Straus, *Introduction to Post-Tonal Theory*). Each shows how thoroughly the common-practice "rules" are optional.
- **The prosody-of-song bridge** — the interface between textual stress (your prosody curriculum) and musical stress: text-setting and word-music. This is the connective tissue between this curriculum, your prosody work, and the accent/voice material — the natural place all three threads meet.
