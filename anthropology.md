# A Four-Field Anthropology Curriculum for the Serious Generalist

## TL;DR
- **You can build genuine four-field literacy in 12–24 months part-time** by anchoring on two free AAA open-access textbooks (*Perspectives* for cultural, *Explorations* for biological), reading ~15 primary works (mostly as SELECTIONS), and sequencing them through 15 modules with sociocultural theory as the center of gravity.
- **Read primary sources in the best modern editions but lean hard on well-chosen selections and readers** — full cover-to-cover reading is reserved for a handful of ethnographies (Malinowski, Evans-Pritchard, one modern monograph) and short seminal essays; everything else is excerpts via McGee & Warms or Erickson & Murphy.
- **Calibrate as you go**: unilineal evolutionism and Kulturkreis diffusionism are superseded; biological "race" is discredited (race is social, not biological); the strong Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is dead but weak linguistic relativity has renewed support; Out-of-Africa-with-admixture is settled in its core; and Mead-Freeman, Sahlins's "affluent society," Chagnon/Tierney, and Graeber & Wengrow's *Dawn of Everything* are all live CONTESTED debates.

## Key Findings

**The scaffolding is free and excellent.** Two peer-reviewed, open-access textbooks from the American Anthropological Association carry the entire program: *Perspectives: An Open Introduction to Cultural Anthropology* (2nd ed., 2020, eds. Nina Brown, Thomas McIlwraith, Laura Tubelle de González) and *Explorations: An Open Invitation to Biological Anthropology* (2nd ed., 2023, eds. Beth Shook, Lara Braff, Katie Nelson, Kelsie Aguilera). Both are CC BY-NC and free at pressbooks/americananthro sites; print copies are cheap.

**Most classics are legally free.** Morgan, Tylor, Frazer, Malinowski's *Argonauts*, Mead's *Coming of Age*, and older translations of Mauss are public-domain on the Internet Archive and Project Gutenberg. But for a few, the free version is an inferior old translation: for Mauss's *The Gift*, prefer the modern translations (W. D. Halls 1990, or Jane Guyer's 2016 expanded HAU edition) over the free 1954 Cunnison.

**Two theory readers do most of the heavy lifting.** Erickson & Murphy, *A History of Anthropological Theory* (6th ed., 2021) plus its companion *Readings*; and McGee & Warms, *Anthropological Theory: An Introductory History* (7th ed., 2019, Rowman & Littlefield). Either reader lets you read seminal essays in curated excerpts rather than hunting monographs.

**Frankfurt gives this learner unique "visit the source" anchors.** The Frobenius Institute — founded 1898 in Berlin as the *Stiftung Afrika-Archiv* by Leo Frobenius, moved to Munich in 1920 as the *Forschungsinstitut für Kulturmorphologie*, and affiliated with Goethe University Frankfurt since 1925 — and the Weltkulturen Museum (which opened on 22 October 1904 as the *Städtisches Völkermuseum* under founder-director Dr. Bernhard Hagen with 4,000 objects) sit at the heart of the superseded Kulturkreis/Kulturmorphologie diffusionist school — a living local archive of a contested chapter of the discipline's history.

## Details

### How to use this plan
- **Depth:** generalist. Read short seminal *essays* in full; read 3–4 *ethnographies* fully; read everything else as SELECTIONS via the two theory readers.
- **Editions:** current editions verified below; FREE/PAYWALLED flagged.
- **Calibration tags:** SETTLED (broad consensus), CONTESTED (live scholarly dispute), OPEN (genuinely unresolved), HYPE-WATCH (popular narrative outruns evidence or is superseded).
- **Languages:** the learner reads German, Spanish, Polish, Russian; German-language options are noted where genuinely useful (Frankfurt anchors; Mauss/Lévi-Strauss in French if desired, though English is fine).

### MODULE 0 — Orientation: what anthropology is
**Goals:** Grasp the four-field concept; the culture concept; cultural relativism vs. ethnocentrism; emic/etic; and anthropology's entanglement with colonialism and its ongoing reckoning.
**Prerequisites:** None.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- *Perspectives* (2nd ed., 2020), Ch. 1 ("Introduction to Anthropology") and Ch. 2 ("The Culture Concept") — read in full. FREE (pressbooks.pub/perspectives).
- *Explorations* (2nd ed., 2023), Ch. 1 ("Introduction to Biological Anthropology") — read in full. FREE.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Eric Wolf's characterization — "Anthropology is both the most scientific of the humanities and the most humanistic of the sciences" (Wolf 1964:88; often misattributed to Kroeber) — as a framing motto; and the emic/etic distinction as framed by Kenneth Pike / Marvin Harris (encyclopedic entry).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Write one page defining "culture" in your own words, then compare against Tylor's 1871 definition (Module 1).
**Estimated time:** 2 weeks.

### MODULE 1 — Founding & the rejection of evolutionism
**Goals:** Understand 19th-century unilineal cultural evolutionism and why it was rejected; Boas and historical particularism as the birth of American cultural anthropology; the origins of the modern critique of the race concept; and the German Kulturkreis diffusionist school as a superseded local chapter.
**Prerequisites:** Module 0.
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS unless noted):**
- Lewis Henry Morgan, *Ancient Society* (1877) — SELECTIONS (the "Ethnical Periods" framework). FREE (Internet Archive; Project Gutenberg). CALIBRATION: HYPE-WATCH/superseded.
- Edward B. Tylor, *Primitive Culture* (1871) — SELECTIONS (Ch. 1, the culture definition; the "survivals" concept). FREE (Internet Archive). CALIBRATION: culture definition ENDURING; unilineal scheme superseded.
- James Frazer, *The Golden Bough* (1890; abridged 1922) — SELECTIONS (opening chapters on sympathetic magic). FREE (Project Gutenberg, 1922 abridged ed.). CALIBRATION: HYPE-WATCH (armchair comparativism, superseded method; enduring cultural influence).
- Franz Boas, *Race, Language and Culture* (1940) — SELECTIONS via McGee & Warms; plus Boas's critique of the comparative method. Some Boas is FREE (Internet Archive).
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** Adam Kuper, *The Reinvention of Primitive Society* (2005) — on how "primitive society" was a Victorian invention. On Kulturkreis: the Frobenius Institute and Weltkulturen Museum histories (frobenius-institut.de; weltkulturenmuseum.de). CALIBRATION: Kulturkreis diffusionism (Frobenius, Graebner) = superseded.
**Frankfurt anchor:** Visit the Weltkulturen Museum (Schaumainkai) and the Frobenius Institute's Leo Frobenius Library on the Campus Westend — with 135,494 volumes (end of 2022), it is the most important specialist library for ethnology in the German-speaking world.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Contrast Tylor's and Boas's stances on whether cultures can be ranked.
**Estimated time:** 4 weeks.

### MODULE 2 — British social anthropology
**Goals:** The fieldwork/participant-observation revolution; functionalism (Malinowski) vs. structural-functionalism (Radcliffe-Brown); the Durkheim→Mauss lineage and *The Gift*.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 0–1.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- Bronisław Malinowski, *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) — read the Introduction ("The Subject, Method and Scope") IN FULL, plus SELECTIONS on the Kula. FREE (Internet Archive; Project Gutenberg #55822).
- A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952) — SELECTIONS via reader.
- Marcel Mauss, *The Gift* (*Essai sur le don*, 1925) — read IN FULL (it is short). RECOMMENDED EDITION: W. D. Halls translation (Routledge, 1990) or Jane I. Guyer's expanded edition (HAU Books, 2016), which restores the original 1923–24 *L'Année Sociologique* context (memorial and Mauss's reviews of Boas, Frazer, Malinowski). The free 1954 Cunnison translation is abridged/dated. Halls/Guyer PAYWALLED.
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** Émile Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) — SELECTIONS, as the background to Mauss. Annette Weiner, *Inalienable Possessions* (1992) — the major reappraisal of Malinowski/Mauss on exchange. On Malinowski: the posthumous *A Diary in the Strict Sense of the Term* (1967) as the reflexive counterweight.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Diagram Kula exchange partnerships from the *Argonauts* introduction.
**Estimated time:** 4–5 weeks.

### MODULE 3 — French structuralism
**Goals:** Structuralism as method; kinship as exchange; myth as structure; the Année Sociologique background.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 1–2 (especially Mauss).
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS):**
- Claude Lévi-Strauss, *Structural Anthropology* (1958; Eng. 1963) — read "The Structural Study of Myth" and "Linguistics and Anthropology" in full. PAYWALLED (Basic Books).
- Lévi-Strauss, *The Savage Mind* (*La Pensée sauvage*, 1962; Eng. 1966) — SELECTIONS (bricolage; the "science of the concrete"). PAYWALLED.
- Lévi-Strauss, *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949; rev. Eng. 1969) — SELECTIONS (the incest taboo and exchange; read via secondary summary). PAYWALLED.
- The *Mythologiques* (esp. *The Raw and the Cooked*, 1964) — read ABOUT rather than through; SELECTIONS only.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Edmund Leach, *Claude Lévi-Strauss* (Fontana Modern Masters) as the accessible companion.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Take one myth and map its binary oppositions.
**Estimated time:** 3–4 weeks.

### MODULE 4 — Symbolic & interpretive anthropology
**Goals:** Thick description and the interpretive turn; ritual, liminality, communitas; symbolic classification and pollution.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 1–3.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- Clifford Geertz, *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973) — read "Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of Culture" and "Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight" IN FULL; SELECTIONS of the rest. PAYWALLED (Basic Books); a 2017 anniversary edition exists.
- Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure* (1969) — SELECTIONS (liminality; communitas). PAYWALLED.
- Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966) — read the "Secular Defilement" chapter and the Abominations of Leviticus analysis; SELECTIONS otherwise. PAYWALLED (Routledge Classics).
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** Talal Asad's critique of Geertz's definition of religion ("Anthropological Conceptions of Religion," 1983). Vincent Crapanzano's critique of Geertz's cockfight ("Hermes' Dilemma," in *Writing Culture*, Module 6).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Write a one-page "thick description" of a mundane scene.
**Estimated time:** 3–4 weeks.

---

### MODULE 5 — Marxist, practice, and political-economy anthropology
**Goals:** Materialist and political-economy approaches; practice theory; the world-system view of "peoples without history"; commodity histories.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 1–4.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- Marshall Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics* (1972) — read "The Original Affluent Society" IN FULL; SELECTIONS otherwise. CALIBRATION: CONTESTED (see below).
- Sherry Ortner, "Theory in Anthropology since the Sixties" (*Comparative Studies in Society and History*, 1984) — read IN FULL. A superb map of the whole field. Often PAYWALLED (JSTOR); frequently in reader.
- Eric Wolf, *Europe and the People Without History* (1982) — SELECTIONS (Introduction; the "modes of production" chapters). PAYWALLED.
- Sidney Mintz, *Sweetness and Power* (1985) — read IN FULL if time allows; else SELECTIONS. PAYWALLED.
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** On Sahlins: Nurit Bird-David, "Beyond 'The Original Affluent Society': A Culturalist Reformulation" (*Current Anthropology* 33(1):25–47, 1992) and David Kaplan, "The Darker Side of the 'Original Affluent Society'" (2000). CALIBRATION: Sahlins's "original affluent society" = CONTESTED — Sahlins argued hunter-gatherers met their needs on ~3–5 hours of food-getting work per day; Bird-David and Kaplan show this figure rests on short, unrepresentative studies (McCarthy & McArthur's Arnhem Land sample; Richard Lee's four-week !Kung study) and conflates work/leisure categories. The *cultural* insight (limited wants → affluence) survives; the *quantitative* claim does not.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Trace one commodity in your kitchen back through its colonial history (Mintz-style).
**Estimated time:** 4 weeks.

### MODULE 6 — The reflexive/postcolonial/critical turn
**Goals:** The "crisis of representation"; anthropology and colonialism; Orientalism as adjacent critique; decolonizing the discipline.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 1–5.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- James Clifford & George Marcus (eds.), *Writing Culture: The Poetics and Politics of Ethnography* (1986) — read Clifford's Introduction and Mary Louise Pratt's / Renato Rosaldo's essays; SELECTIONS. PAYWALLED (UC Press).
- Talal Asad (ed.), *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973) — SELECTIONS (Asad's introduction).
- Lila Abu-Lughod, "Writing Against Culture" (1991) — read IN FULL.
- Edward Said, *Orientalism* (1978) — SELECTIONS (Introduction). Adjacent, not anthropology proper. PAYWALLED.
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** Clifford Geertz, *Works and Lives* (1988) on ethnographic authority; critiques of *Writing Culture* for omitting feminist anthropology (Frances Mascia-Lees et al., 1989).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Rewrite a paragraph of classic ethnography identifying its rhetorical "authority" devices.
**Estimated time:** 3 weeks.

### MODULE 7 — Kinship, exchange, and economic anthropology
**Goals:** Classic kinship theory and its collapse (Schneider); reciprocity/gift vs. commodity; the formalist–substantivist debate; Polanyi's influence.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 2–3, 5.
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS):**
- David M. Schneider, *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984) — SELECTIONS. CALIBRATION: classic unilineal/genealogical kinship theory = substantially critiqued; Schneider's demolition is now canonical.
- Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation* (1944), and "The Economy as Instituted Process" (1957) — SELECTIONS.
- Marshall Sahlins, "On the Sociology of Primitive Exchange" (in *Stone Age Economics*) — reciprocity typology.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Chris Gregory, *Gifts and Commodities* (1982). On the formalist–substantivist debate, a reader overview.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Map generalized/balanced/negative reciprocity onto real relationships you have.
**Estimated time:** 3 weeks.

---

### MODULE 8 — Reading canonical ethnographies in depth
**Goals:** Sustained close reading of 2–4 monographs to see theory operate in practice.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 1–6.
**PRIMARY readings (READ MORE FULLY):**
- E. E. Evans-Pritchard, *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande* (1937) — the abridged edition (1976, Clarendon) is the standard teaching text. Read the witchcraft/oracle logic in full. CALIBRATION: enduring classic on the rationality of belief systems.
- E. E. Evans-Pritchard, *The Nuer* (1940) — SELECTIONS (ecology; the "segmentary lineage" and time-reckoning). CALIBRATION: segmentary-lineage model later contested but foundational.
- One modern ethnography IN FULL — choose one: Philippe Bourgois, *In Search of Respect: Selling Crack in El Barrio* (1995; 2nd ed. 2003), or Nancy Scheper-Hughes, *Death Without Weeping* (1992).
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** On Malinowski's *Argonauts* as constructed narrative, Robert Thornton's 1985 analysis of its literary influences (Frazer, Mach, Conrad).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Write a 2-page review situating your chosen modern ethnography in the theory arc.
**Estimated time:** 5–6 weeks.

---

### MODULE 9 — Linguistic anthropology
**Goals:** Linguistic relativity (strong vs. weak); the ethnography of communication; language ideology; semiotics in anthropology.
**Prerequisites:** Module 1 (Boas).
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS):**
- Edward Sapir, *Language* (1921) — SELECTIONS. FREE (Project Gutenberg/Internet Archive).
- Benjamin Lee Whorf, *Language, Thought, and Reality* (posthumous, 1956) — SELECTIONS (the key essays on linguistic relativity).
- Dell Hymes, "The Ethnography of Speaking" (1962) — SELECTIONS.
- *Perspectives* chapter on language — FREE.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Lera Boroditsky's experimental work on time/space/color (2001, 2003) for the weak-relativity revival. CALIBRATION: the STRONG Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (linguistic determinism) = largely rejected (undone in part by Berlin & Kay's universalist color findings); WEAK linguistic relativity = renewed, replicated empirical support (color, spatial cognition, grammatical gender). Note: Sapir and Whorf never co-authored or framed a single "hypothesis"; the label is posthumous.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Using your own languages (German/Spanish/Polish/Russian), list three grammatical categories English lacks and hypothesize any cognitive nudge — then note why this is only weak-relativity evidence.
**Estimated time:** 3 weeks.

### MODULE 10 — Archaeology: theory and method
**Goals:** Culture history → processual/"New Archaeology" → post-processual; dating and method; major transitions (Paleolithic, Neolithic Revolution, rise of states); the recent grand-narrative debate.
**Prerequisites:** Module 0.
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS):**
- V. Gordon Childe, *Man Makes Himself* (1936) and *What Happened in History* (1942) — SELECTIONS (the "Neolithic Revolution" and "Urban Revolution" concepts). Some editions FREE (Internet Archive).
- Lewis Binford, "Archaeology as Anthropology" (1962) — read IN FULL (short). CALIBRATION: processualism = foundational, partly superseded.
- Ian Hodder, *Reading the Past* (1986; 3rd ed. 2003 with Scott Hutson) — SELECTIONS (the post-processual critique; note Hodder's *Symbols in Action*, 1982, as the crystallizing work).
- Colin Renfrew & Paul Bahn, *Archaeology: Theories, Methods and Practice* (current ed.) — the standard textbook for method (stratigraphy, radiocarbon, seriation). Use as reference. PAYWALLED.
- David Graeber & David Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* (2021) — read SELECTIONS (Ch. 1–3) plus at least one critical review. CALIBRATION: CONTESTED/HYPE-WATCH.
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** On *Dawn of Everything*: Ian Morris, "Against Method" (*American Journal of Archaeology* 126(3), 2022) and other specialist reviews arguing it misrepresents evidence, engages sources selectively, and fights "fictitious enemies." It has been influential and popularizing but is disputed by specialists.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Radiocarbon half-life calculation — given a sample with 25% of original ¹⁴C, compute its age (≈2 half-lives ≈ 11,460 years). Genuinely intrinsic to dating; OPTIONAL.
**Estimated time:** 4–5 weeks.

### MODULE 11 — Biological anthropology I: human evolution
**Goals:** Primatology; the hominin fossil record; Out-of-Africa vs. multiregional and its resolution; Neanderthals, Denisovans, and archaic admixture via ancient DNA.
**Prerequisites:** *Explorations* Ch. 1–4 (evolutionary theory, genetics, forces of evolution) — FREE.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- *Explorations* (2nd ed., 2023), chapters on primates, the fossil record, and *Homo sapiens* — read IN FULL. FREE.
- Jane Goodall, *In the Shadow of Man* (1971) — SELECTIONS. Frans de Waal, *Chimpanzee Politics* (1982) — SELECTIONS.
- David Reich, *Who We Are and How We Got Here: Ancient DNA and the New Science of the Human Past* (2018, Oxford/Pantheon) — read Part I IN FULL (Neanderthal admixture; ancient DNA), SELECTIONS of regional chapters. PAYWALLED.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Smithsonian Human Origins Program / "What Does It Mean to Be Human?" website (humanorigins.si.edu) — FREE scaffolding for the fossil record.
**CALIBRATION:** Out-of-Africa with admixture = SETTLED in its core, superseding strict multiregionalism. Per NIH MedlinePlus Genetics, Neanderthal DNA is "about 1 to 2 percent in people of European or Asian background," and "the percentage of Denisovan DNA is highest in the Melanesian population (4 to 6 percent)." The details (African internal structure, "ghost" populations, number/dates of admixture events) = OPEN.
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium — given an allele frequency, compute expected genotype frequencies. Intrinsic to pop-gen; OPTIONAL.
**Estimated time:** 5 weeks.

---

### MODULE 12 — Biological anthropology II: human variation and the race concept
**Goals:** Why biological "race" is rejected; clinal/continuous variation; the apportionment of diversity and its reassessment; human adaptation; forensic nuance.
**Prerequisites:** Module 11.
**PRIMARY readings:**
- Richard Lewontin, "The Apportionment of Human Diversity" (1972) — read IN FULL (short). Lewontin partitioned human genetic diversity as **85.4% within populations, 8.3% among populations within "races," and 6.3% among "races"** — i.e., less than 15% of human diversity is accounted for by differences between human groups (figures per Lewontin 1972; see Edge, Ramachandran & Rosenberg, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 377:20200405, 2022).
- *Explorations* chapters on human variation and race — FREE.
- The **AAA Statement on Race** (adopted by the AAA Executive Board on 17 May 1998; principal drafter Audrey Smedley) — read IN FULL. FREE (americananthro.org/about/policies/statement-on-race/). It states that "human populations are not unambiguous, clearly demarcated, biologically distinct groups" and that "most physical variation, about 94%, lies within so-called racial groups." This remains the AAA's standing position statement.
**SUPPLEMENTARY / critique:** A. W. F. Edwards, "Human Genetic Diversity: Lewontin's Fallacy" (2003) — AND the rebuttals showing Edwards answered a *different* question (whether an individual can be *classified* into a group given many loci) than Lewontin (the *apportionment* of diversity); see the 2022 *Philosophical Transactions B* 50th-anniversary theme issue. Also useful: the **AABA/AAPA Statement on Race and Racism** (27 March 2019; from the biological-anthropology body, a *separate* organization from the AAA), which states "Race does not provide an accurate representation of human biological variation." CALIBRATION: biological typological race = DISCREDITED; race as social (not biological) category = mainstream SETTLED position. The Lewontin/Edwards exchange is frequently weaponized online; the scientific consensus is that racial taxonomy is not biologically useful for studying human variation.
**Free resources:** AAA "RACE: Are We So Different?" project (understandingrace.org, developed by the AAA with the Science Museum of Minnesota; "explains differences among people and reveals the reality and unreality of race"); Smithsonian Human Origins (humanorigins.si.edu).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Plot a cline (e.g., skin-pigmentation vs. latitude) and note how continuous variation defeats discrete "racial" bins.
**Estimated time:** 3 weeks.

### MODULE 13 — Contemporary & applied anthropology
**Goals:** Medical anthropology; the ontological turn; multispecies/more-than-human; digital anthropology; anthropology of science; applied/development work.
**Prerequisites:** Modules 4–8.
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS):**
- Paul Farmer, *Pathologies of Power* (2003) — SELECTIONS (structural violence).
- Arthur Kleinman, *The Illness Narratives* (1988) — SELECTIONS.
- Eduardo Kohn, *How Forests Think* (2013) — SELECTIONS (multispecies/ontological turn). CALIBRATION: ontological turn = CONTESTED/OPEN.
- *Perspectives* chapters on medical, digital, and applied anthropology — FREE.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** Bruno Latour, *We Have Never Been Modern* (1991) as STS background; a critique of the ontological turn (e.g., Lucas Bessire & David Bond, 2014).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** One-paragraph "illness narrative" from your own experience, analyzed emically.
**Estimated time:** 3–4 weeks.

---

### MODULE 14 — Methods & ethics
**Goals:** Fieldwork and participant observation; reflexivity/positionality; the discipline's major ethics controversies; a light note on cross-cultural quantitative methods.
**Prerequisites:** Module 8.
**PRIMARY readings (SELECTIONS):**
- On method: a chapter from H. Russell Bernard, *Research Methods in Anthropology* (6th ed.) — SELECTIONS.
- Margaret Mead, *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928) — SELECTIONS. FREE (Internet Archive). Read alongside Derek Freeman, *Margaret Mead and Samoa* (1983) — SELECTIONS. CALIBRATION: Mead–Freeman = CONTESTED; current specialist consensus (Paul Shankman, *The Trashing of Margaret Mead*, 2009) is that Freeman's critique was itself deeply flawed — relying on misrepresentation, selective evidence, and an implausible "hoaxing" claim — while Mead over-generalized from a small sample on Ta'ū. Mead's core finding (that adolescent "storm and stress" is not universal) largely stands.
- On the Chagnon/Yanomami affair: Patrick Tierney, *Darkness in El Dorado* (2000) — read ABOUT rather than through; plus the AAA El Dorado Task Force saga. CALIBRATION: CONTESTED — the AAA accepted the Task Force report (2002), then, after a 2005 member referendum, rescinded its acceptance; Tierney's central allegation that Chagnon and geneticist James Neel caused a measles epidemic was rejected as biologically impossible by the National Academy of Sciences and the American Society of Human Genetics.
- On military anthropology: the Human Terrain System debate and the AAA's critical response.
**SUPPLEMENTARY:** On cross-cultural quantitative comparison, George Murdock and the Human Relations Area Files (HRAF). CALIBRATION: HRAF/eHRAF is a legitimate tool but "Galton's problem" (cultural non-independence) limits naive correlation. ACCESS: eHRAF World Cultures is partly PAYWALLED (institutional subscription).
**Light exercise (OPTIONAL):** Draft a one-page fieldwork ethics protocol (consent, harm, positionality).
**Estimated time:** 3 weeks.

## Dependency Map
- **Module 0** → everything.
- **Module 1** (Boas, evolutionism) → prerequisite for 2, 9, 12.
- **Module 2** (Malinowski, Mauss, Durkheim) → prerequisite for 3, 5, 7, 8.
- **Module 3** (structuralism) → feeds 4, 7.
- **Modules 4–6** (interpretive → political economy → reflexive turn) are best read in sequence; 6 depends on 1–5.
- **Module 8** (ethnographies) depends on 1–6.
- **Module 9** (linguistic) depends on 1.
- **Module 10** (archaeology) depends only on 0.
- **Module 11** (human evolution) depends on *Explorations* Ch. 1–4; **Module 12** (variation/race) depends on 11.
- **Modules 13–14** depend on 4–8.
- **Parallelizable tracks:** the biological/archaeology track (10–12) can run in parallel with the sociocultural spine (1–8), since they share only Module 0.

## MVP Fast-Path (compressed canonical spine, ~4–6 months)
For a reader who wants the core fast, read these in order:
1. *Perspectives* Ch. 1–2 + *Explorations* Ch. 1 (four-field orientation). FREE.
2. Tylor + Boas selections (Module 1) via McGee & Warms reader.
3. Malinowski, *Argonauts* introduction (full) + Mauss, *The Gift* (full, Halls/Guyer).
4. Lévi-Strauss, "The Structural Study of Myth" (full).
5. Geertz, "Thick Description" + "Deep Play" (full).
6. Sahlins, "The Original Affluent Society" + Ortner, "Theory Since the Sixties" (full).
7. Abu-Lughod, "Writing Against Culture" (full).
8. Evans-Pritchard, *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande* (abridged, full).
9. Lewontin (1972) + AAA Statement on Race + *Explorations* race chapter.
10. Reich, *Who We Are*, Part I.

This spine (≈10 items, most short) delivers a defensible generalist command of the four fields.

## Full Arc (the complete ~1–2-year program)
Modules 0–14 in dependency order, with the biological/archaeology track (10–12) interleaved after Module 6. At ~3–5 weeks/module and 5–8 hours/week, the full arc runs ≈14–20 months. Budget the two theory readers (Erickson & Murphy; McGee & Warms) as your constant companions throughout.

## Recommended secondary/survey texts (verify current editions)
- **Four-field intro textbook:** Conrad Kottak, *Anthropology: Appreciating Human Diversity* (19th ed., McGraw-Hill). PAYWALLED; older editions cheap/used.
- **Theory histories + readers:** Erickson & Murphy, *A History of Anthropological Theory* (6th ed., 2021, University of Toronto Press) + companion *Readings for a History of Anthropological Theory* (6th ed.). McGee & Warms, *Anthropological Theory: An Introductory History* (7th ed., 2019, Rowman & Littlefield) — 43 essays 1860–present with excellent editorial apparatus. Adam Kuper's histories (*Anthropology and Anthropologists*; *The Reinvention of Primitive Society*).

## Recommendations
1. **Start this week, free:** read *Perspectives* Ch. 1–2 and *Explorations* Ch. 1, and buy one theory reader (McGee & Warms 7th ed. is the more essay-rich; Erickson & Murphy 6th ed. is the better narrative history). This costs ~€40 and unlocks the great majority of the SELECTIONS.
2. **Run two tracks in parallel** to avoid fatigue: the sociocultural spine (Modules 1–8) as your main line, and the biology/archaeology track (10–12) as a lighter parallel read. They share only Module 0.
3. **Reserve full reading for high-return short texts** (Mauss, the two Geertz essays, Ortner 1984, Abu-Lughod, Binford 1962, Lewontin 1972) and for 2–3 ethnographies. Everything else is SELECTIONS.
4. **Use Frankfurt as a lab:** visit the Weltkulturen Museum and the Frobenius Library while reading Module 1; it turns the superseded Kulturkreis school into a tangible experience and gives you access to the German-speaking world's premier ethnology collection.
5. **Benchmarks that change the plan:** if after the MVP Fast-Path you find theory more compelling than material culture, deepen Modules 3–6 and treat 10–12 as survey-only; if the reverse, expand the biology/archaeology track with a dedicated method textbook (Renfrew & Bahn) and skim the reflexive turn. If you find yourself wanting the journal literature and reading monographs whole, you have crossed from "generalist" into "pre-research" territory — a natural point to consider a formal course at Goethe University's Institut für Ethnologie.

## Caveats
- **This is a generalist program, not research training.** It deliberately reads most primary works as selections; a research-level reader would read the monographs whole and add the journal literature.
- **Calibration tags reflect current mainstream scholarship and may shift**, especially in the fast-moving ancient-DNA field (Reich's 2018 regional chapters are already partly dated) and in the still-live *Dawn of Everything* and ontological-turn debates.
- **Do not conflate the two "race statement" bodies:** the 1998 statement is from the American Anthropological Association (AAA); the 2019 statement is from the American Association of Biological Anthropologists (AABA, formerly AAPA) — separate organizations that are frequently confused.
- **Editions:** confirm the latest printing at purchase; textbook editions (Kottak, Renfrew & Bahn, the theory readers) update every few years, but any recent edition suffices for a generalist.
- **Free-access classics** are public-domain scans; some are older translations or editions, which matters most for Mauss and Lévi-Strauss — use the modern translations where flagged.
