# Classical Latin Self-Study Resources: A Verified Research Dossier for a Reading-Only "C1" Curriculum

## TL;DR
- **Build the spine on Ørberg's *Lingua Latina Per Se Illustrata* (comprehensible input) hybridized with a systematic grammar (Wheelock or Neumann's *Companion*), then transition to authentic texts via the free Dickinson College Commentaries and Geoffrey Steadman facing-vocabulary readers** — this hybrid is the best fit for a disciplined, analytically-minded adult whose Slavic case-intuition and Romance vocabulary let him move fast.
- **The natural formal reading-proficiency benchmark is the German state Latinum**, sat in Hesse as an *Ergänzungsprüfung* administered centrally by the Staatliches Schulamt Gießen (3-hour translation of ~180 words of unadapted classical prose, dictionary allowed, €90). It certifies roughly Cicero-level reading — the best available proxy for "C1 reading."
- **Latin has an unusually large free/public-domain resource base** (Perseus, Lewis & Short via Logeion, Whitaker's Words, DCC, Steadman PDFs, archive.org grammars); the main things worth paying for are LLPSI print books, a modern desk grammar/dictionary, and optionally the Legentibus audio app (~€/$9.99/month).

## Framing: How "C1 Reading" Maps to Latin Milestones

CEFR was designed for living languages and is not officially defined for Latin; there is no DELF/CILS/Goethe-Zertifikat equivalent. The sensible way to operationalize "C1 reading" is against the **traditional author difficulty ladder** and the German Latinum, which is the closest institutional analogue.

The traditional/evidence-based difficulty ladder (corroborated by teacher and self-learner consensus on Textkit and by the German Latinum author canons) runs:

1. **Adapted/beginner Latin** (LLPSI *Familia Romana*, graded readers) — pre-authentic.
2. **Easiest authentic prose**: **Eutropius** (*Breviarium* — its "plain style made it long a favorite elementary Latin schoolbook") and **Cornelius Nepos** (biographies; "his simple style of writing has made him a comparatively easy and popular author"). Often the true first authentic texts.
3. **Caesar** (*De Bello Gallico*) — the classic first "real" author; clear, paratactic military prose.
4. **Cicero** — the prose standard (speeches like the *Catilinarians*, then letters, then philosophy/*De Officiis*). This is the pivot point: the German KMK Latinum benchmarks essentially at Cicero level.
5. **Ovid** — the most accessible major verse (elegiac couplets and hexameter: *Metamorphoses*, *Amores*).
6. **Vergil** (*Aeneid*) — the epic hexameter standard; harder word order and poetic vocabulary.
7. **Hardest tier**: **Livy** and **Sallust** (dense historiography), **Catullus** and **Horace** (compressed lyric; Horace's *Odes* famously difficult), **Seneca**, and **Tacitus** — universally cited as the hardest major prose author ("when it comes to Tacitus 'it's back to diapers'").

**Concrete mapping for phase benchmarks:**
- **~B1 reading**: can read adapted Latin (finished *Familia Romana*) and easiest authentic prose (Eutropius, Nepos) with help.
- **~B2 reading**: reads Caesar and easier Cicero largely unaided, dictionary for rare words. This is roughly the **Kleines Latinum** level (Caesar-based).
- **~C1 reading (the target)**: reads unadapted Cicero (speeches and philosophy), Ovid, and Vergil with fluency and stylistic appreciation, dictionary only for rare vocabulary; can make progress on Livy/Sallust/Tacitus with a commentary. This aligns with the **Großes Latinum** canon (Caesar, Livy, Sallust, Cicero, Tacitus, Ovid, Vergil, Catullus, Horace) and sits above the "normal" KMK-Latinum.

## Key Findings

1. **Best spine = LLPSI + explicit grammar hybrid.** For volume of comprehensible input LLPSI is unmatched; but an analytical adult benefits from a systematic grammar layer (Wheelock or Neumann's *Companion*) to make morphology/syntax explicit. This hybrid answers the "decoding vs. reading" critique of pure grammar-translation while giving the learner the explicit scaffolding he prefers.
2. **The learner's profile compresses the timeline.** His Polish/Russian case-and-gender intuition removes the single biggest obstacle English speakers face (the six-case system, free word order, synthetic morphology); his Spanish + emerging French/Italian give heavy Latin-derived vocabulary transfer. A motivated adult with these advantages should reach comfortable Caesar/Cicero reading substantially faster than the typical 2-year university sequence.
3. **The certification path is clear and local.** The Hesse state Latinum (Ergänzungsprüfung via Staatliches Schulamt Gießen) is the appropriate formal benchmark; Goethe-Universität Frankfurt offers internal certificates but explicitly directs pure self-learners to the state exam for the portable KMK-Latinum.
4. **Free resources are exceptionally strong for Latin.** A reader can go from beginner to advanced almost entirely on free material (Perseus, DCC, Steadman, Logeion/Lewis & Short, Whitaker's Words, archive.org grammars), buying only a few key print books.

## Details by Resource Category

### 1. Core Course / Method

**A. Natural / comprehensible-input: Hans Ørberg, *Lingua Latina Per Se Illustrata* (LLPSI).** Published by Domus Latina (Denmark); distributed in the English-speaking world by Hackett Publishing (formerly Focus/R. Pullins). Entirely in Latin with marginal notes and illustrations; teaches grammar and vocabulary inductively.
- ***Familia Romana* (Pars I)**, 2nd edition, full-color — ISBN 9781585104208 (Hackett/Focus). 35 chapters describing a 2nd-century Roman family; culminates in readings from classical poets and Donatus's *Ars Grammatica*. Each chapter has *lectiones*, a *Grammatica Latina* section, and three *Pensa* exercises. **Paid.**
- ***Roma Aeterna* (Pars II)** — the advanced continuation (chapters 36–56), largely adapted classical texts (Livy, etc.). Second edition, full-color, from Hackett. **Paid.**
- **Companion apparatus:**
  - *Latine Disco* — Ørberg's English student guide for *Familia Romana*.
  - *A Companion to Familia Romana* (2nd ed.), Jeanne Marie Neumann (Hackett) — English running commentary on the grammar; incorporates the *Grammatica Latina* and Latin–English vocabulary; **explicitly designed to replace/serve as a substitute for *Latine Disco*** and is the single best add-on for an analytical self-learner. A parallel *A Companion to Roma Aeterna* exists.
  - *Grammatica Latina* booklet — ISBN 9781585102235 (2005).
  - *Exercitia Latina I* (for *Familia Romana*); *Exercitia Latina II* (for *Roma Aeterna*).
  - *Colloquia Personarum* (2nd ed., full-color) — supplementary dialogues keyed to chapters 1–24.
  - *Fabulae Syrae*, Luigi Miraglia — ISBN 9781585104284; 50 myths keyed to chapters, from Pygmalion to ~200 verses of Ovid's *Metamorphoses*; usable concurrently (chs. 26–34) or as a review reader.
  - Answer keys / indices from Hackett; some PDF ancillaries (e.g. for *Epitome Historiae Sacrae*) are free to adopters.
- **Audio:** Ørberg audio exists (hosted via Latinitium); the **Legentibus app carries *Familia Romana* in text + synchronized professional audio** (though without marginalia/illustrations).

**B. Grammar-translation: *Wheelock's Latin*, 7th edition** (Frederic M. Wheelock, rev. Richard A. LaFleur), HarperCollins, 2011 — ISBN 9780061997211 (hb), 9780061997228 (pb). 40 chapters, authentic readings (Cicero, Vergil, and others), self-tutorial exercises **each with an answer key for independent study**. Ecosystem:
  - *Workbook for Wheelock's Latin* (LaFleur & Comeau).
  - *38 Latin Stories* (Groton & May), 5th ed., keyed to Wheelock's 7th — graded prose readings (18 original mythology pieces + 20 adaptations from Caesar, Catullus, Cicero, Horace, Livy, Petronius, Pliny, Quintilian, Sallust, Terence, Vergil; heavy Cicero emphasis); facing vocabulary. **Paid** (print + digital via Shelfit).
  - *Scribblers, Sculptors, and Scribes* (LaFleur) — a companion of authentic Latin.
  - *Wheelock's Latin Reader* — post-Wheelock authentic selections.

**C. Reading-method school courses:**
  - **Cambridge Latin Course (CLC)** — 5th UK edition (6th in North America), Cambridge University Press; Book 1 with digital access ISBN 9781009162647. Per Wikipedia it is "the most-used Latin course in the country for secondary school pupils… used by 85% of Latin-teaching schools," and CUP markets it as the most widely used Latin GCSE course in UK secondary schools (over 4 million copies sold). Story-driven (Caecilius). **Classroom-oriented; expensive (~$50/book × 5); comparatively weak on explicit grammar and repetition for a self-learner.**
  - **Oxford Latin Course** (Balme & Morwood), OUP — considered "a little more challenging and robust on the grammar" than CLC, but still assumes teacher support.
  - **Ecce Romani** — reading course; also classroom-oriented.
  - *Self-study verdict:* All three are built around a teacher; for a disciplined autodidact they are **inferior to LLPSI (more input, fully self-contained) or Wheelock (fully self-tutorial with keys).**

**Comparative recommendation for THIS learner:** Use **LLPSI *Familia Romana* as the input spine**, paired with **Neumann's *Companion* (or Wheelock) as the explicit-grammar layer.** This hybrid delivers both the reading volume that builds automatic parsing (addressing the "decoding vs. reading" problem) and the systematic morphology/syntax an analytical learner wants. His case background means the explicit grammar will be *fast* rather than laborious. **A hybrid is strongly advisable — not either/or.**

### 2. Reference Grammars

- **Allen & Greenough, *New Latin Grammar*** — the traditional English standby; **public-domain and free** (Perseus hyperlinked/searchable version; a revised DCC digital version; archive.org; PDFs). In-print reprints exist (Dover; the 2001 reprint adds Anne Mahoney's *Overview of Latin Syntax*). Cited as "A&G." **Best single free desk reference for an English speaker;** a classics librarian review calls it "possibly the best available in English."
- **Gildersleeve & Lodge, *Latin Grammar*** (3rd ed., 1895/1900) — "G&L"; celebrated for lucidity, especially syntax. **Public-domain (archive.org); in-print reprint** from Bolchazy-Carducci (ISBN 9780865163539, ~$27) and Dover. Many prefer G&L's syntax treatment.
- **Bennett, *New Latin Grammar*** — concise; public-domain and free.
- **Advanced syntax:** **E.C. Woodcock, *A New Latin Syntax*** (1959; Bristol Classical Press/Duckworth reprint, ISBN 9780862920425) — a historical, example-rich account of constructions from Plautus to Tacitus, arranged as a progressive syntax revision course; **available on archive.org**; inexpensive used. Excellent for an analytical reader wanting *why*, not just *what*.
- **Modern scholarly option:** **Harm Pinkster, *The Oxford Latin Syntax*** — Vol. 1 *The Simple Clause* (2015, ISBN 9780199283613, ~$210) and Vol. 2 *The Complex Sentence and Discourse* (ISBN 9780199230563), OUP. Successor to Kühner-Stegmann; BMCR calls it "the point of reference for the next century of Latin scholarship." Descriptive, uses modern linguistics but non-technical. **Expensive, paywalled; a reference to consult (ideally at a library), not to own early.**
- **Best single desk reference:** **Allen & Greenough** (free, comprehensive, section-numbered for stable citation), supplemented by **Woodcock** for advanced syntax and **Pinkster** consulted at libraries for the hardest questions.

### 3. Dictionaries & Lexical Tools

- **Oxford Latin Dictionary (OLD)**, 2nd ed. 2012 (P.G.W. Glare, ed.), OUP — ISBN 9780199580316. The scholarly gold standard for Classical Latin (sources to AD 200); 40,000 headwords, 100,000 senses, ~2,400 pp., 2 vols. **Expensive** (list around £300+/€/$). **Paywalled**; overkill for early study but the ultimate reference.
- **Lewis & Short, *A Latin Dictionary*** (1879) — the previous standard; **free** via Perseus and **Logeion**. Fuller in later Latin than OLD but older scholarship.
- **Logeion** (University of Chicago, since 2011) — **free** open-access aggregator of Latin/Greek lexica (Lewis & Short, DMLBS, etc.) plus frequency/collocation data from Perseus; web + app. **The everyday lookup tool.**
- **Whitaker's Words** — **free** parser/dictionary (~39,000 entries, written in Ada) that deduces the dictionary form and full morphological analysis of any inflected form; invaluable for a beginner reading authentic text. Online mirrors + downloadable (SourceForge; Notre Dame archive).
- **Perseus Word Study Tool** — **free**; parses words in-text and links to lexica.
- **Cassell's Latin Dictionary** (D.P. Simpson) — solid mid-size Latin-English/English-Latin; **paid**, in print.
- **Good collegiate/intermediate print option:** the *Bantam New College Latin & English Dictionary* (Traupman) or Cassell's — adequate desk dictionaries; **paid.** *Exam note:* the Hesse Latinum requires a *Latin–German* dictionary (Stowasser, Pons, or Langenscheidt).

### 4. Core Vocabulary / Frequency

- **DCC Latin Core Vocabulary** — roughly **1,000 headwords/lemmas** that, per Christopher Francese, "account for approximately 70–80% of the word forms found in a typical text, excluding proper names. For Caesar's *Gallic War*, the figure is 91%; for Vergil's *Aeneid*, 81%" (the 91%/81% are the top per-work figures; the corpus-wide typical coverage is ~70–80%). Created 2012–13 by a Dickinson team led by Francese. **Free**, downloadable in multiple formats and **languages (including Latin–Spanish by Francisco Javier Pérez Cartagena and Latin–Italian by Gian Paolo Ciceri — directly useful for this learner; no German version yet as of the DCC blog).** Also in print: *Core Latin and Ancient Greek Vocabularies* (Francese, ISBN 9781947822061).
- **Frequency scholarship:** Diederich's frequency studies; Muccigrosso (2004) and Major (2008) — experts recommend mastering the ~1,500 lemmas covering 80% of the corpus by the end of second-year college study.
- **Decks/apps:** DCC core is on Quizlet and as downloadable spreadsheets/Anki; Legentibus and Memrise carry Latin decks.
- **Highest-yield approach for a reader:** LLPSI builds vocabulary *in context* (words recur naturally), which favors durable acquisition; **supplement with explicit spaced-repetition of the DCC 1,000 core** so the highest-frequency words are locked in early. For this learner, use the **Latin–Spanish/Italian DCC lists** to exploit Romance transfer. This dual approach (contextual + frequency SRS) is the highest-yield strategy.

  - *Sermones Romani* (ISBN 9781585101955, 2012) — lightly adapted Cicero, Tacitus, Martial et al.; readable immediately after *Familia Romana*.
  - *Fabellae Latinae*; *Colloquia Personarum*; *Fabulae Syrae* (see §1).
  - Ørberg's graded editions of *Caesar: De Bello Gallico (I, IV, V)*, *Sallustius & Cicero: Catilina*, *Ovid: Ars Amatoria*, *Plautus: Amphitryo*, *Vergil: Aeneis I & IV* — bridges from LLPSI into authentic authors.
- ***38 Latin Stories*** — see §1 (Wheelock ecosystem). **Paid.**
- **Geoffrey Steadman's facing-vocabulary readers** — see §6. **Free PDFs.**
- **Other:** Latinitium's *Pugio Bruti* and *Ad Alpes* (Rouse) — easy-Latin readers with audiobooks (paid).

### 7. Author Difficulty Ladder toward C1 (with usual entry points)

Confirmed sequence and standard entry works:
1. **Eutropius**, *Breviarium ab Urbe Condita* (esp. Book 1) — easiest; also teaches the outline of Roman history. **Nepos**, *Lives* (e.g. *Hannibal*, *Atticus*, *Miltiades*) — simple style; a common escape from "synthetic Latin," intermediate between Caesar and Cicero.
2. **Caesar**, *De Bello Gallico* Book 1 (the Helvetian campaign) — the standard first "real" author.
3. **Cicero** — entry via the ***Catilinarian* orations** (esp. *First Catilinarian*), then **letters** (*Ad Familiares/Ad Atticum*), then philosophy (*De Amicitia*, *De Senectute*, *De Officiis*). The prose standard.
4. **Ovid** — *Metamorphoses* selections and *Amores* Book 1 (accessible elegiac/hexameter verse).
5. **Vergil**, *Aeneid* Books 1, 2, 4, 6 (the epic standard).
6. **Livy** (Book 1 or 21), **Sallust** (*Catiline*), **Catullus** (shorter polymetrics first), **Horace** (*Satires* before *Odes*), **Seneca** (*Epistulae Morales*), and finally **Tacitus** (*Agricola*/*Germania* before the *Annals*) — the hardest tier.

This ladder is mirrored by the German Latinum canons: Kleines Latinum ≈ Caesar; KMK-Latinum ≈ Cicero (+ Ovid at the interpretation level); Großes Latinum ≈ + Livy, Sallust, Tacitus, Vergil, Horace, Catullus.

### 8. Latin Prose Composition (optional accelerator)

Famously effective for internalizing syntax even for reading-only goals (Latinitium: composition books "seek to consolidate the students' knowledge of Latin syntax and lexicon while also… drastically improv[ing] their reading comprehension"; many consider it "the sine qua non for really mastering Latin"). Flag as **optional accelerator, not a target.**
- **North & Hillard, *Latin Prose Composition*** — the standard starter (Latinitium recommends starting here); **public-domain**, free PDFs; in-print reset edition (Focus, ISBN 9780941051910).
- **Bradley's Arnold *Latin Prose Composition*** — the advanced "gold standard" (Mountford revision): Bristol Classical Press ISBN 9780862921507; Bolchazy-Carducci reset ed. ISBN 9780865165953. A *Key* exists (Cambridge Library Collection reprint). **Public-domain original** also free.
- **Mountford** editions = the modern typeset revisions of both.

For this learner: a *light* dose of North & Hillard (a few exercises per grammar topic) would powerfully cement the case/syntax system he already half-knows from Slavic — high ROI, but keep it subordinate to reading.

A limited free collection is always available.

### 10. Optional Active/Spoken Accelerators

The pedagogical argument (Krashen's comprehensible-input hypothesis, applied to Latin by e.g. Elisabetta Venditti, "Using Comprehensible Input in the Latin Classroom," *Journal of Classics Teaching* 22, 2021) is that **understanding large volumes of meaningful input — including audio — builds the automatic, left-to-right parsing that distinguishes "reading" from "decoding."** Even for a reading-only goal, **listening to Latin you can understand (Legentibus audio, the Latinitium podcast, Ørberg audio) reinforces word order, vowel quantity, and chunking**, which speeds reading fluency. Keep speaking/writing optional; but **passive listening is a genuine, low-cost reading accelerator** and is recommended in moderation. Spoken-Latin communities (conventicula; SALVI; Paideia; Accademia Vivarium Novum) exist but are beyond the reading-only scope.

### 11. Germany Context & Certification — the Latinum

**This is the natural formal reading-proficiency benchmark for this learner; Latin has no DELF/CILS equivalent.**

**What it certifies (KMK Vereinbarung of 22.09.2005):** the ability to understand, in content, structure and meaning, "lateinische Originaltexte im sprachlichen Schwierigkeitsgrad inhaltlich anspruchsvollerer Stellen (bezogen auf Bereiche der politischen Rede, der Philosophie und der Historiographie)," demonstrated by an accurate translation into appropriate German (plus, where required, interpretation). The KMK's own attached sample tasks (*Aufgabenbeispiele*) are all **Cicero** (*Philippics* 2.116ff.; *De natura deorum* 1.6f.; *Tusculans* 5.10) plus one medieval text (Otto von Freising) — i.e. **Cicero-level unadapted prose is the benchmark.** The 2005 agreement defines the level by domain rather than by a fixed author list.

**Levels and author canons:**
- **Kleines Latinum**: easier prose, principally **Caesar** (and easier Cicero).
- **KMK-Latinum ("das Latinum")**: **Cicero, Ovid and comparable authors** — the nationally standardized, portable qualification proven with the Abitur or an Ergänzungsprüfung.
- **Großes Latinum**: adds harder/poetic authors — the Hessian course canon lists **Caesar, Livius, Sallust, Cicero, Tacitus, Ovid, Vergil, Catull, Horaz**. This maps most closely to the learner's "C1" target.

**How an adult in Hesse sits it (state Ergänzungsprüfung):**
- Administered centrally by the **Staatliches Schulamt für den Landkreis Gießen und den Vogelsbergkreis** (Schubertstraße 60, 35392 Gießen; lgn@kultus.hessen.de / Poststelle.SSA.Giessen@kultus.hessen.de), with candidates assigned to a Prüfungsschule in Hesse.
- **Structure:** written = a **translation of ~180 words of unadapted Latin in three hours (three *Zeitstunden*), dictionary allowed** (Stowasser/Pons/Langenscheidt, Latin–German, "ohne Ergänzungen"; supplied if you cannot bring one); oral = translate ~50 words + a follow-up examination discussion (20 min exam, 30 min preparation). Overall grade weighted **2:1 written:oral**; pass = at least "ausreichend."
- **Eligibility:** for adults not attending a Gymnasium/Abendgymnasium/Hessenkolleg; requires proof of Hesse residence or workplace (last 12 months) OR current Hesse university enrollment OR a Hesse-acquired university-entrance qualification. A **report of preparation with an exact reading list (naming the author focused on)** must be submitted.
- **Fee:** **€90** ("Prüfungsgebühr in Höhe von 90,00 €"; waived for Grundsicherung/Sozialhilfe recipients). Held twice yearly (spring March/April; autumn Oct/Nov); deadlines **31 January** and **31 July**; next exam listed **1 October 2026**. Official sample exams (Latinum Beispielklausuren I & II) are downloadable from the Schulämter Hessen page.

**Goethe-Universität Frankfurt (local alternative):** The Institut für Klassische Philologie does **not** itself award the state Latinum; it offers internal certificates — "Lateinkenntnisse" (2-hour easier Caesar exam), "Sprachprüfung in Latein (ehemals Kleines Latinum)" (3-hour medium Caesar text + oral), and "Anspruchsvolle Lateinkenntnisse" ("im Umfang des Latinums"; a recent prep course reads **Cicero, *Pro Marcello***; fee €40). These are recognized within Hesse but not guaranteed portable outside it, and are generally tied to enrolled students in specific programs. For the fully portable KMK-Latinum the university explicitly directs learners to the **state Ergänzungsprüfung via Schulamt Gießen** — the appropriate route for a pure self-learner.

**Verdict:** Target the **KMK-Latinum via the Hesse state Ergänzungsprüfung** as the "C1 reading" milestone; if aiming higher for stylistic appreciation of verse/hard prose, prepare to **Großes Latinum** author breadth. Otherwise the standard informal yardstick applies: "can read Caesar/Cicero unseen with only a dictionary for rare words."

## Cross-Cutting: The Pedagogy Debate (calibration tags)

- **Grammar-translation (Wheelock; Jenney; etc.)** — *contested*. Strength: systematic, explicit, efficient for analytical adults; fully self-tutorial with answer keys. Critique ("decoding vs. reading"): can produce students who *decode* by hunting for the verb and reassembling into English rather than *reading* Latin in order. Best used as a *reference/scaffold*, less so as the sole method.
- **Reading method (Cambridge/Oxford/Ecce Romani)** — *contested*. Strength: contextual, story-driven, more input than pure grammar. Weakness for self-study: teacher-dependent, expensive, uneven grammar exposition and repetition; some editions criticized for too little grammar for autodidacts.
- **Natural / comprehensible-input (Ørberg LLPSI, active Latin)** — *surging; partly settled, partly hype*. Strength: unmatched volume of comprehensible input; builds genuine reading fluency and "thinking in Latin." The recent surge (Legentibus, CI YouTube, spoken-Latin) is real and evidence-informed (Krashen; Venditti 2021), but online endorsement can be uncritical, and LLPSI alone can leave analytical adults wanting explicit grammar and can feel slow.
- **Reasonable synthesis (settled among many practitioners):** **LLPSI for input volume + a systematic grammar (Wheelock/Neumann *Companion*/A&G) for explicit morphology and syntax + early, heavy authentic reading via DCC/Steadman + optional prose composition and audio.** This is exactly the hybrid recommended here.

## Realistic Timeline

There is no FSI category for Latin (not taught there). Reasoned estimates, triangulated from the university sequence, LLPSI self-study reports, and this learner's accelerators:
- **Traditional benchmark:** US universities cover elementary grammar in ~1 year (2 semesters) or an ~8-week summer intensive (≈5 hrs/day, e.g. University of Chicago) and then read Caesar/Sallust/Cicero; comfortable Cicero/Vergil reading typically emerges over ~2–4 years.
- **LLPSI self-study reports:** finishing *Familia Romana* + *Roma Aeterna* (with supplements) yields ability to read a lot of Latin "rather ok with a dictionary for extremely rare words"; the Vulgate becomes accessible after ~25 chapters of *Familia Romana*.
- **This learner (Slavic-case + Romance-vocabulary advantages, motivated adult):** plausibly reaches **comfortable Caesar/easier-Cicero reading (≈B2 / Kleines-to-KMK Latinum)** in roughly **12–18 months** of consistent study, and **fluent unaided reading of standard Cicero/Ovid/Vergil (≈C1 / KMK-to-Großes Latinum)** in roughly **2.5–4 years**, with Tacitus/Horace/Livy remaining a lifelong stretch requiring commentaries. The case/gender/word-order advantage (the usual bottleneck for English speakers) plus vocabulary transfer should compress the front end substantially; be cautious about over-optimism on the hardest verse/prose, which is genuinely difficult for everyone.

## Sequencing: morphology → syntax → reading

For a reading-only goal with this profile:
1. **Front-load morphology fast** (declensions, conjugations) — his Slavic case-sense makes this quick; use LLPSI chapters + A&G/Companion for explicit paradigms + DCC-core SRS.
2. **Layer syntax** (cases in use, participles, ablative absolute, indirect statement/*accusativus cum infinitivo*, subjunctive uses, *cum*-clauses, conditionals) via LLPSI's inductive presentation reinforced by Woodcock/A&G — and optionally a light dose of North & Hillard composition to cement it.
3. **Shift weight to extensive reading early and keep increasing it:** LLPSI supplements → Eutropius/Nepos (DCC/Steadman) → Caesar → Cicero → Ovid → Vergil → hard tier. Keep a dictionary/parser (Logeion, Whitaker's) and facing-vocabulary editions to sustain volume without frustration.
4. **Ratio:** explicit grammar should dominate only the first phase; thereafter reading volume should dominate, with grammar consulted as a reference when the text raises a question. Extensive comprehensible reading — not more grammar drills — is what produces fluent reading.

## Recommendations (staged)

**Phase 0 — Foundation (months 0–3):** LLPSI *Familia Romana* (chs. 1–~15) + Neumann's *Companion* for explicit grammar; begin DCC 1,000-core SRS (use the Latin–Spanish/Italian lists); install Logeion + Whitaker's Words. *Benchmark to advance:* read *Familia Romana* chapters at sight with rare reliance on marginal notes.

**Phase 1 — Complete the base (months 3–9):** Finish *Familia Romana* + *Colloquia Personarum*/*Fabulae Syrae*; add *Epitome Historiae Sacrae* and *Sermones Romani*; optionally start North & Hillard lightly. Begin audio (Legentibus/Ørberg). *Benchmark:* finish *Familia Romana*; read Ritchie's *Fabulae Faciles* comfortably.

**Phase 2 — First authentic prose (months 9–18):** Eutropius and Nepos (Steadman/DCC) → Caesar *DBG* 1 (Steadman *College Caesar* + DCC). Start *Roma Aeterna*. *Benchmark ≈ Kleines/KMK Latinum:* translate an unseen Caesar passage with a dictionary.

**Phase 3 — The prose standard + accessible verse (months 18–36):** Cicero (*First Catilinarian* → letters → *De Officiis*, via Steadman/DCC) and Ovid (*Amores* 1/*Metamorphoses*, DCC). Finish *Roma Aeterna*. *Benchmark ≈ KMK Latinum / C1 reading:* read unseen Cicero/Ovid fluently, dictionary for rare words. **This is the point to sit the Hesse state Latinum.**

**Phase 4 — Epic and the hard tier (year 3+):** Vergil *Aeneid* (Pharr + DCC), then Livy, Sallust, Catullus, Horace, Seneca, Tacitus (green-and-yellow / Steadman / Bloomsbury commentaries). Consult Woodcock/Pinkster for syntax. *Benchmark ≈ Großes Latinum / advanced C1:* stylistic appreciation of verse and hard prose with a commentary.

**Thresholds that change the plan:**
- If LLPSI feels too slow/soft on grammar → increase Wheelock/A&G weight and add composition.
- If parsing is still effortful entering Phase 2 → add more graded/audio input before authentic text (don't push into Caesar prematurely).
- If the goal shifts to certification speed → drill past Hesse Beispielklausuren (Cicero-level prose) with a Latin–German dictionary (Stowasser) directly, and submit the required *Lektüreliste* naming Cicero as the focus author.

## Caveats

- **CEFR levels are unofficial for Latin**; the B1/B2/C1 tags here are pedagogical analogies pegged to the author ladder and the German Latinum, not certified scales.
- **Timeline estimates are reasoned, not from a controlled study** (no FSI data for Latin); individual pace varies widely. The learner's specific accelerators are real, but the hardest authors (Tacitus, Horace) resist quick mastery for everyone.
- **Editions/prices change**: verify current ISBNs/editions at Hackett, Bolchazy-Carducci, OUP, and CUP before purchase; several LLPSI ancillaries have multiple editions (confirm 2nd/full-color where noted). Pharr's *Aeneid* is often dated 1964 by the current publisher but originated with D.C. Heath in 1930.
- **The DCC core-coverage figure** is ~70–80% corpus-wide; the frequently-quoted 91% (Caesar) and 81% (Vergil) are the top per-work figures — cite them precisely.
- **The exact author(s) in the two official Hesse Latinum Beispielklausuren could not be confirmed from the primary PDFs**; the benchmark author is Cicero (per the KMK sample tasks and prep-institute guidance), but open the two sample-exam PDFs directly before exam prep to confirm.
- **Free vs. paid is flagged throughout;** note that public-domain grammars/dictionaries are old (pre-1930s scholarship) though still standard — pair them with a modern reference (Pinkster) for contested points.
- Some sources here were forums/blogs (Textkit, Latinitium, the "Found in Antiquity" and "Emma Williams" teacher blogs); these are used only for consensus/qualitative points, with primary/publisher and official government sources preferred for all facts.
