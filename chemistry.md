# Chemistry: A Graduate-Level Self-Study Curriculum

**Target:** read primary literature fluently on chemistry topics spanning physiology, pharmacology, mining, and electronics; do problem sets; defend mechanisms.
**Budget:** ~7+ hrs/week over ~34 weeks (~250 hrs total).
**Sequencing:** starts after the biology curriculum finishes (~mid-2027). Standalone, but the biology curriculum's Phase 1 (general chem + Clayden basics) and Phase 2 (Lehninger biochemistry) are treated as prior knowledge — this curriculum builds on them and revisits organic + biochem at higher rigor rather than re-reading.
**Strategy:** physical chemistry as the trunk (leveraging your physics background), organic revisited more deeply with a physical-organic layer, inorganic + analytical essentials, enzyme mechanism and medicinal chemistry as the physiology + pharmacology deep-dives, mining and electronics as targeted application phases.

---

## Prior coverage assumption

When you begin this curriculum, from the biology plan you'll already have:
- General chem foundations (OpenStax Atoms First 2e, done)
- Organic chem: mechanism-first Clayden through carbonyl/enol chemistry (Chs 1–2, 6, 8, 9–12, 14, 15, 17, 19, 20, 25, 26, 29, 30 approximately)
- Biochemistry: Lehninger cover-to-cover on metabolism (Chs 2–8, 10–23)
- Enzyme kinetics at introductory level (Lehninger Ch 6)

## Core textbook recommendations

Ranked by importance to your priorities.

### 1. McQuarrie & Simon — Physical Chemistry: A Molecular Approach
Statistical mechanics-first pedagogy. For a theoretical-physics-trained learner this is unambiguously the right P-chem text. Atkins will feel hand-wavy after real stat mech; Levine is more mathematical formalism than intuition. McQuarrie makes chemistry follow from what you already know.

### 2. Clayden, Greeves & Warren — Organic Chemistry (2nd ed)
Already yours. Revisited for the advanced chapters skipped in biology (retrosynthesis, aromatic mechanism deeper, pericyclic, radical, asymmetric).

### 3. Anslyn & Dougherty — Modern Physical Organic Chemistry
The lever for pharmacology. Uses MO theory heavily throughout; treats reactivity from a physical-reasoning standpoint. For a physicist doing drug design chemistry, this is where intuition sharpens dramatically. There isn't a great alternative at this level — Carroll's *Perspectives on Structure and Mechanism in Organic Chemistry* is second-best if you can't find A&D.

### 4. Miessler, Fischer & Tarr — Inorganic Chemistry (5th ed)
Group theory + MO diagrams from a physicist-friendly angle. Better fit for you than Housecroft & Sharpe; more analytical, less descriptive. Feeds coordination chem into the mining phase and MO-in-solids intuition into the electronics phase.

### 5. Harris — Quantitative Chemical Analysis (10th ed)
Analytical essentials only. Selected chapters — the goal is chemical intuition around equilibrium, buffers, titration, chromatography, spectroscopy interpretation, not becoming a working analytical chemist.

### 6. Fersht — Structure and Mechanism in Protein Science (revised 2017 reissue)
The enzyme mechanism deep-dive. Best rigorous treatment of enzyme mechanism, transition-state theory in enzymes, and drug-binding thermodynamics. The physiology/pharmacology bridge.

### 7. Patrick — An Introduction to Medicinal Chemistry (7th ed)
Accessible, well-loved primary text for medicinal chemistry. Excellent on structure-activity relationships, drug design strategies, ADMET, and case studies of major drug classes.

### 8. Silverman & Holladay — The Organic Chemistry of Drug Design and Drug Action (3rd ed)
Deeper follow-on to Patrick. Graduate-reference tone. Lead discovery, receptor theory, enzyme inhibitor design, prodrugs.

### 9. Free — Hydrometallurgy: Fundamentals and Applications (2013)
For the mining phase. Modern textbook format, integrates chemistry with process engineering. Habashi's *Textbook of Hydrometallurgy* is the classic reference but Free is the better learning text.

### 10. Smart & Moore — Solid State Chemistry: An Introduction (4th or 5th ed)
For electronics. Chemistry-flavored solid state (defects, non-stoichiometry, ionic conductors, oxide semiconductors) vs. Kittel's physics angle. Your physics side already covers band theory; this fills in the chemistry.

### Supplementary
- **Grossman — The Art of Writing Reasonable Organic Reaction Mechanisms**: problem book, invaluable for building mechanism-defense skill.
- **Bard & Faulkner — Electrochemical Methods**: use Chs 1–3 in Phase 1; save the rest as reference.
- **Testa & Krämer — The Biochemistry of Drug Metabolism** (vol 1): the drug metabolism source for Phase 4.
- **Rowland & Tozer — Clinical Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics**: only if you decide to add PK/PD as an extension.
- **Cramer — Essentials of Computational Chemistry**: only if you want a computational drug design extension.

## Phase 1 — Physical Chemistry (Weeks 1–8, ~55 hrs)

McQuarrie & Simon primary. Focused pass: stat thermodynamics, kinetics, electrochem foundations. Light on quantum chemistry (you have QM from physics; the chemical applications are the point).

### Week 1 (~7 hrs): Quantum chemistry, light
McQuarrie Chs 1–6 selected review, Ch 8 (variational method), Chs 9–10 (molecular quantum mechanics, MO theory basics). Skim what you know. Emphasis: how QM shapes chemical bonding intuition (LCAO, hybridization from first principles, orbital symmetry).

### Weeks 2–5 (~28 hrs): Statistical thermodynamics
McQuarrie stat mech chapters (Chs 17–22 approximately; numbering shifts by edition). Boltzmann distribution, partition functions for translational/rotational/vibrational/electronic modes. Ideal gases, real gases, phase equilibria, chemical equilibria from partition functions. **This is where your physics pays off — you'll move faster than a chemist, but the whole point is slowing down on the chemical interpretation.** Every "and now applied to a chemical system" section deserves the same attention a chemist would give the derivation.

### Weeks 6–7 (~14 hrs): Chemical kinetics
McQuarrie kinetics chapters (Chs 27–29 approximately). Rate laws, elementary reactions, mechanism deduction from kinetics, collision theory, transition state theory (Eyring equation), catalysis basics. Direct bridge to Fersht in Phase 4.

### Week 8 (~6 hrs): Electrochemistry foundations
McQuarrie's electrochem coverage + Bard & Faulkner Chs 1–3. Electrochemical potentials, Nernst equation, electrode kinetics (Butler-Volmer), mass transport (diffusion layer, convection, migration), Faradaic vs. non-Faradaic processes. Foundation for both the mining phase and general redox intuition.

---

## Phase 2 — Advanced Organic Chemistry (Weeks 9–15, ~55 hrs)

Clayden revisited for skipped chapters + Anslyn & Dougherty as physical organic complement. The mechanism-defense skill is consolidated here.

### Weeks 9–11 (~24 hrs): Advanced Clayden
Chapters skipped or skimmed in biology:
- Aromatic chemistry deeper — electrophilic/nucleophilic aromatic substitution, aromatic heterocycles (Chs 21, 22, 29, 30 revisited at depth)
- Retrosynthesis and synthetic strategy (Chs 28, 32)
- Determination of reaction mechanisms (Ch 39) — kinetic isotope effects, crossover experiments, Hammett plots
- Radical chemistry (Ch 39 and neighbors)
- Pericyclic reactions — Diels-Alder, sigmatropic, electrocyclic (Chs 34–37)
- Asymmetric synthesis (Ch 41)

### Weeks 12–14 (~24 hrs): Physical organic chemistry
Anslyn & Dougherty, Chs 1–10 selectively. MO theory applied to reactivity, kinetic isotope effects (rigorously), linear free energy relationships (Hammett, Taft), solvent effects, catalysis theory. **This is where physical chemistry and organic chemistry fuse — foundational for the medicinal chem phase.** Slow work: expect 4–5 hrs per chapter.

### Week 15 (~7 hrs): Mechanism problem book
Grossman, *The Art of Writing Reasonable Organic Reaction Mechanisms*. Work through as many problems as time allows — start with intermediate difficulty. This is where the mechanism-defense skill consolidates. Add unfamiliar mechanisms to the notebook.

## Phase 3 — Inorganic + Analytical Essentials (Weeks 16–20, ~35 hrs)

### Weeks 16–18 (~21 hrs): Inorganic chemistry
Miessler, Fischer & Tarr:
- Symmetry and group theory (Chs 3–4) — easy given your physics; important for spectroscopy
- Molecular orbitals applied to inorganic molecules (Ch 5)
- Coordination chemistry (Chs 9–10) — **foundational for mining, do rigorously**
- Ligand field theory (Ch 10) — d-orbital splitting, spectroscopy and magnetism of complexes
- Organometallic chemistry basics (Ch 13) — some catalysis relevance
- Descriptive main-group chemistry (Chs 6–8) — reference-level skim only

### Weeks 19–20 (~14 hrs): Analytical chemistry essentials
Harris:
- Chemical equilibrium in depth (Chs 6–8) — buffers, acid-base, complex equilibria, activity coefficients revisited
- Titrations (Chs 10–13) — chemical intuition around endpoint detection, indicators
- Chromatography principles (Chs 22–24) — HPLC especially, critical for pharmacology later
- Spectroscopic methods (Chs 17–20) — UV-vis, mass spec, NMR interpretation deeper than Clayden

Skip: sampling, statistics chapters (you have these), instrumentation details, wet-lab technique chapters.

## Phase 4 — Biochemistry Revisited & Enzyme Mechanism (Weeks 21–25, ~40 hrs)

Lehninger is already done. This phase goes deeper on enzyme mechanism (Fersht) and adds what Lehninger doesn't cover well: drug metabolism, cytochrome P450 chemistry, xenobiotic biotransformation.

### Weeks 21–23 (~24 hrs): Enzyme mechanism at depth
Fersht — *Structure and Mechanism in Protein Science*:
- Ch 2: Chemical catalysis — the physical organic view
- Ch 3: Kinetics of enzyme reactions — beyond Michaelis-Menten (pre-steady-state, single-turnover)
- Ch 4: Measurement of individual rate constants
- Ch 5: pH dependence, isotope effects in enzymes
- Ch 12: Transition state stabilization — quantitative treatment
- Chs 16–17: Case studies (serine proteases, lysozyme, etc.)

### Weeks 24–25 (~16 hrs): Drug metabolism & xenobiotic chemistry
Not in Lehninger. Sources:
- Testa & Krämer, *The Biochemistry of Drug Metabolism* (Wiley), vol 1 for redox reactions (Phase I metabolism)
- Selected chapters from Ortiz de Montellano (ed.), *Cytochrome P450: Structure, Mechanism, and Biochemistry* — mechanism-focused chapters especially
- 2–3 modern reviews on CYP450 mechanisms and induction from *Chemical Reviews* or *Drug Metabolism Reviews*

**This is where physiology and pharmacology fuse.** Every drug's metabolic fate lives in this chemistry.

## Phase 5 — Medicinal Chemistry & Drug Design (Weeks 26–30, ~35 hrs)

### Weeks 26–27 (~14 hrs): Patrick
*An Introduction to Medicinal Chemistry* cover-to-cover. Fast pass — accessible by this point. Focus: drug-target interactions, SAR, drug design strategies, ADMET properties, case studies of major drug classes (β-blockers, ACE inhibitors, HIV protease inhibitors, kinase inhibitors, statins).

### Weeks 28–29 (~14 hrs): Silverman
*The Organic Chemistry of Drug Design and Drug Action*. Deeper follow-on. Chapters on lead discovery, receptor theory, enzyme inhibitor design, prodrugs. Cross-reference back to Fersht for the mechanism side.

### Week 30 (~7 hrs): Primary literature capstone
5–7 papers spanning: Lipinski's rule of five (original 1997 paper + a modern reassessment), a classic SAR study, one CYP450 mechanism paper, one modern drug design case study, and one review on covalent inhibitors (where the physical organic chemistry from Phase 2 pays off directly). Specific paper picks best selected when you reach this point.

## Phase 6 — Extractive/Mining Chemistry (Weeks 31–32, ~15 hrs)

Targeted phase given priority 3.

Free — *Hydrometallurgy: Fundamentals and Applications*:
- Aqueous solution chemistry review — fast, you have this from Phases 1 and 3
- Leaching chemistry: acid systems, cyanide leaching, chloride systems
- Solvent extraction and ion exchange — coordination chemistry from Phase 3 pays off directly
- Electrowinning and electrorefining — electrochemistry from Phase 1 pays off directly
- Precipitation, cementation, crystallization
- Case studies: copper, gold, uranium extraction

Supplementary: Marsden & House, *The Chemistry of Gold Extraction* — one focused case study to consolidate, if interested.

---

## Phase 7 — Solid-State & Electronics Chemistry (Weeks 33–34, ~15 hrs)

Targeted phase given priority 4. Band theory assumed from your physics — focus on the chemistry.

Smart & Moore — *Solid State Chemistry: An Introduction*:
- Crystal structures and bonding in solids — light pass, you know most
- Defects and non-stoichiometry — critical for semiconductor doping chemistry
- Ionic conductors — batteries, solid electrolytes, fuel cells
- Electronic properties of oxides — mixed conductors, transparent conducting oxides
- Superconductors (if time)
- Preparative methods — CVD, MBE, sol-gel, molten salt synthesis; how electronic materials are actually made

If deep semiconductor chemistry becomes interesting, Sze's *Physics of Semiconductor Devices* is the natural follow-up (heavy but excellent).

## Workflow notes

- **Problem sets stay non-negotiable.** McQuarrie's for stat thermo, Clayden's for advanced organic, Grossman's for mechanism defense, Fersht's for enzyme kinetics, Miessler's for group theory / MO. If you cut anything, cut passive reading — never problem sets.
- **The physicist-reads-McQuarrie speed trap.** The single biggest failure mode in Phase 1 is going too fast because the math is familiar. The math isn't the point. The chemical translation is. Slow down at every "applied to a chemical system" moment; make yourself predict things like activity coefficients and equilibrium constants from partition functions before reading the answer.
- **Mechanism notebook continues.** Add to the biology curriculum notebook. Target: 60–80 mechanisms internalized by end of Phase 5 (metabolism + Phase 4 additions + medicinal chem targets + CYP450 archetypes).
- **Group theory pays off early.** The symmetry work in Phase 3 will feel natural given your physics but gets you fluent in spectroscopy papers. Don't skim character tables — work the reduction problems.
- **Papers track.** Unlike biology (papers concentrated in Phase 4), consider one paper every 2 weeks throughout — chemistry breadth benefits from constant primary literature exposure. Rolling list available on request.
- **Pace check at end of Phase 2.** If more than 2 weeks behind, cut Phases 6–7 first — they're lowest priority. Do not compress Phases 4–5. Also acceptable: trim Phase 3 analytical to 1 week instead of 2, or drop the pericyclic/asymmetric chapters of Phase 2.
- **Overlap consolidation.** Phases 1 (electrochem) → 3 (coordination chem) → 6 (mining) form a coherent inorganic/electrochem track. Phases 2 (physical organic) → 4 (enzyme mechanism) → 5 (medicinal chem) form the pharmacology track. Note the cross-connections as you go.
