# Geology: A Thorough Enrichment Curriculum

**Purpose:** intellectual pleasure. No credential goal, no career use, but done at real depth because that's how you enjoy it.
**Target:** confident geological literacy — read primary literature comfortably, identify rocks and structures in the field, understand Earth as a coupled system.
**Budget:** ~6 hrs/week over ~26 weeks (~155 hrs total).
**Flavor:** European examples (British/Irish, Polish, Alpine) where the textbook material allows; geochemistry thread leveraging the chemistry curriculum.
**Sequencing:** deferred — could run parallel-lite alongside biology (~3 hrs/week, longer duration), fit as a summer 2027 intensive between biology and chemistry, or sit after chemistry finishes in 2028. Notes at end.

## Textbook recommendations

Fewer than the previous curricula since geology is a more unified discipline at this level.

### 1. Marshak — Earth: Portrait of a Planet (6th ed, 2019)
The best-written introductory geology text. Genuinely enjoyable prose, superb illustrations, excellent conceptual clarity. Standout for a self-learner over Grotzinger & Jordan's *Understanding Earth* (also excellent but drier). Foundation for Phase 1.

### 2. Nesse — Introduction to Mineralogy (3rd ed, 2016)
Modern, well-structured mineralogy. Handles crystallography, optical mineralogy, and systematic mineralogy cleanly. Alternative: Klein & Philpotts' *Earth Materials* combines minerals + petrology in one volume — worth considering if you want a unified Phases 2+3 text (see adjustments).

### 3. Winter — Principles of Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology (2nd ed)
Standard graduate-level petrology, accessible once mineralogy is behind you. Selected chapters — you don't need the full treatment.

### 4. Boggs — Principles of Sedimentology and Stratigraphy (5th ed)
Standard for sedimentary geology. Feeds directly into the petroleum capstone. Selected chapters.

### 5. Fossen — Structural Geology (2nd ed, 2016)
The modern classic. Excellent figures, well-paced. If you buy one geology textbook where visualization matters, this is it. Alternative: van der Pluijm & Marshak's *Earth Structure* combines structural + plate tectonics in one book (see adjustments).

### 6. Kearey, Klepeis & Vine — Global Tectonics (3rd ed)
Plate tectonics rigorously done. Companion to Fossen. If time-constrained, van der Pluijm covers both structural and tectonics at slightly less depth.

### 7. Stanley & Luczaj — Earth System History (4th ed)
Narrative historical geology from Hadean to Anthropocene. Well-written, integrates paleoclimate, mass extinctions, and Earth system dynamics.

### 8. Woodcock & Strachan (eds.) — Geological History of Britain and Ireland (2nd ed, 2012)
The definitive text for British/Irish geology. Wiley-Blackwell, ~£50-70 — pricey but no substitute. Skim broadly, then read carefully. Perfect companion to your British/Irish natural history reading.

### 9. White — Geochemistry (2013)
The modern geochemistry text. Comprehensive: thermodynamics of geochemical systems, aqueous geochemistry, isotope geochemistry, cosmochemistry. Selected chapters — the geochem thread doesn't need cover-to-cover.

### 10. Bjørlykke (ed.) — Petroleum Geoscience: From Sedimentary Environments to Rock Physics (2nd ed, 2015)
Springer. European-flavored (Norwegian author, North Sea case studies) — the perfect petroleum text for your bias. Preferred over Selley's *Elements of Petroleum Geology* (more American/Middle Eastern focus).

### Narrative books, woven throughout
- **Richard Fortey — The Earth: An Intimate History** (2004): global geological tour, brilliant. Assign to Phase 1.
- **Richard Fortey — The Hidden Landscape**: British geology narrative. Assign to Phase 5.
- **John McPhee — Annals of the Former World** (Pulitzer 1999): American in focus but a masterwork; do not skip. Assign across Phases 4–5.
- **Marcia Bjornerud — Timefulness**: short, on geological deep time. Assign to Phase 5.
- **Robert Hazen — The Story of Earth**: mineral evolution angle, complements the geochemistry thread. Assign to Phase 6.
- **Robert Macfarlane — Underland**: poetic, British/European. Assign to Phase 8.

### Optional
- Boggs, *Petrology of Sedimentary Rocks* — deeper sed petrology if you like Phase 3
- McCann (ed.), *The Geology of Central Europe* (2 vols, Geological Society) — expensive reference for Polish/Alpine/German geology
- Prothero & Dott, *Evolution of the Earth* — alternative to Stanley
- Turcotte & Schubert, *Geodynamics* — if you want a real geophysics extension (math-heavy, right up your alley)

## Phase 1 — Physical geology foundations (Weeks 1–5, ~30 hrs)

Marshak cover-to-cover, in parallel with Fortey's *The Earth: An Intimate History*.

- Weeks 1–2: Ch 1–5 (introduction, minerals, igneous rocks, volcanism, sedimentary/metamorphic overview)
- Weeks 3–4: Ch 6–11 (plate tectonics, deformation, earthquakes, mountain building, geologic time)
- Week 5: Ch 12–17 (Earth history, atmosphere/climate, oceans, glaciers, deserts, rivers)

Marshak end-of-chapter problems: work the conceptual ones, skip the numerical drills. About 2–3 hrs per chapter total.

Fortey in parallel — one chapter per Marshak week, no coordination required. Contemplation reading; evenings.

## Phase 2 — Mineralogy (Weeks 6–8, ~18 hrs)

Nesse, selected:
- Week 6: Crystallography, crystal chemistry (Chs 1–5) — your chemistry background makes this straightforward
- Week 7: Optical mineralogy basics (Chs 6–8) — enough to understand what a petrographic microscope does; don't try to actually use one from a book
- Week 8: Systematic mineralogy — silicates especially (Chs 15–20 approximately)

**Goal:** identify hand-sample minerals with a hardness kit, hand lens, and streak plate. Buy or borrow all three — the tactile side is 80% of the pleasure here.

## Phase 3 — Petrology (Weeks 9–12, ~24 hrs)

### Weeks 9–10 (~12 hrs): Igneous & metamorphic
Winter, selected:
- Igneous: classification, magma generation, differentiation, volcanic and plutonic rock associations
- Metamorphic: metamorphic facies, key mineral assemblages, P-T-t paths
- Skip: detailed geochemical modeling (revisited in Phase 6), regional petrology detail

### Weeks 11–12 (~12 hrs): Sedimentary
Boggs, selected:
- Weathering and sediment production
- Depositional environments (fluvial, deltaic, marine, aeolian, glacial)
- Sedimentary rock classification and diagenesis
- Basic stratigraphy — lithostratigraphy, biostratigraphy, sequence stratigraphy intro
- Skip: analytical technique chapters

## Phase 4 — Structural geology & plate tectonics (Weeks 13–15, ~18 hrs)

### Weeks 13–14 (~12 hrs): Structural geology
Fossen, selected — enjoy the figures:
- Stress and strain fundamentals
- Brittle deformation, faults and fault systems
- Ductile deformation, folds, shear zones
- Structural analysis at various scales
- Optional: rheology chapter for the physics angle

### Week 15 (~6 hrs): Plate tectonics
Kearey chapters covering: plate motion kinematics, mid-ocean ridges, subduction zones, transform faults, continental collision, mantle convection basics. If Kearey feels excessive, van der Pluijm's tectonics section suffices.

Start McPhee's *Annals of the Former World* here — his basin-and-range and Appalachian chapters land beautifully against Fossen and Kearey. Read across Phases 4–5.

## Phase 5 — Historical geology & European geology (Weeks 16–19, ~24 hrs)

### Weeks 16–17 (~12 hrs): Global historical geology
Stanley & Luczaj — narrative Hadean-to-Anthropocene. Fast enjoyable read; take chronological notes.

In parallel: Bjornerud's *Timefulness* (short, evenings across these two weeks).

### Weeks 18–19 (~12 hrs): British/Irish + Polish/Alpine geology
Woodcock & Strachan — skim broadly, then read carefully:
- Precambrian basement & Caledonian orogeny (Britain, Ireland, Scandinavia)
- Variscan orogeny (relevant across Europe including Poland, Germany, Britain)
- Alpine orogeny (British Cenozoic + continental Alps)
- Quaternary glaciation of Britain, Ireland, Poland, Alps

For Polish geology specifically, the McCann *Geology of Central Europe* volumes are the deep reference — if you don't want to buy them, review papers from the *Geological Quarterly* (Polish Geological Institute) suffice. I can send a starter reading list if you want.

In parallel: Fortey's *The Hidden Landscape* — perfect timing.

## Phase 6 — Geochemistry thread (Weeks 20–22, ~18 hrs)

By now the chemistry curriculum has fully prepared you for this. White, selected:
- Thermodynamics of geochemical systems (leverages McQuarrie stat thermo directly)
- Aqueous geochemistry (leverages Harris equilibrium + Free hydrometallurgy — real cross-curriculum payoff)
- Trace element and isotope geochemistry
- Radiogenic isotope systems (Sm-Nd, Rb-Sr, U-Pb) — the physics of dating
- Stable isotope geochemistry (O, C, S) — climate and biogeochem
- Skip: cosmochemistry, organic geochemistry (previewed in petroleum phase)

In parallel: Hazen's *The Story of Earth* — mineral evolution and geochemistry as narrative. Excellent complement.

## Phase 7 — Petroleum geology capstone (Weeks 23–24, ~12 hrs)

Bjørlykke, selected — this is the integration payoff:
- Sedimentary basins and basin analysis (structural + sed cross-reference)
- Source rocks and organic geochemistry (geochem cross-reference) — kerogen types, maturation, oil vs. gas windows
- Petroleum migration and accumulation
- Reservoir rocks and rock properties
- Traps and seals (structural cross-reference)
- Case studies: North Sea, Norwegian continental shelf

**Notice how much prior material lights up here** — this is why petroleum geology makes such a satisfying integrator despite the modest scope allocation.

## Phase 8 — Field, narrative, and integration (Weeks 25–26, ~12 hrs)

Choose your own adventure:
- Macfarlane's *Underland* — the poetic capstone; European caves, catacombs, underground rivers
- Revisit any Phase's textbook chapters you skimmed and now want deeper
- Read primary literature on a topic that caught you — reviews from *Reviews of Geophysics*, *Earth-Science Reviews*, or *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*
- **Actually go do field geology** — Snowdonia (Wales), the Burren (Ireland), the Karkonosze (Poland/Czech border), or day trips from Frankfurt to the Rheinisches Schiefergebirge or the Odenwald. A geological map plus a hand lens transforms an ordinary hike.

## Workflow notes

- **Problems are lighter than the previous curricula.** Do the conceptual problems in Marshak, Fossen, Boggs; skip most of the numerical drills. The exception is Phase 6 (geochemistry) where the calculations are the point.
- **Buy a rock/mineral kit.** Even a cheap 20-mineral hand-sample kit + hardness testers dramatically improves Phase 2. Also worth: a hand lens (10× or 20×), a small streak plate, and a geological hammer if you'll field-trip. Total investment ~€50.
- **Geological maps are underrated.** The British Geological Survey (bgs.ac.uk) has free viewers for UK maps; the Polish Geological Institute (pgi.gov.pl) similar for Poland; Geoportal Deutschland covers Germany. Spend time with maps of areas you know.
- **Field trips are highly recommended and are how geology becomes real.** Frankfurt has excellent access to the Rhine graben (Cenozoic rifting), the Vogelsberg (Cenozoic volcanism), and the Taunus (Variscan). A day at a good exposure teaches more than a chapter. The Alps are within weekend reach.
- **Narrative books work best in parallel, not as breaks between phases.** Read them evenings/weekends while the textbook is happening in structured sessions. They compound with the textbook material.
- **Cross-curriculum harvest.** Your chemistry curriculum will make Phase 6 (geochemistry) unusually easy. Notice this and enjoy it. Your biology curriculum will help slightly in the paleontology sections of Phase 5.

## A note on editions & pricing

- Editions used above: Marshak 6th (2019); Nesse 3rd (2016); Winter 2nd (2014); Boggs 5th (2014); Fossen 2nd (2016); Kearey 3rd (2009); Stanley 4th (2014); Woodcock & Strachan 2nd (2012); White 2013; Bjørlykke 2nd (2015).
- Most standard textbooks are ~$80-150 new; used prints often $30-60. Woodcock & Strachan and the McCann volumes are the pricey outliers.
- Older editions are fully fine — geology moves slowly compared to biology.
- Marshak may have a newer edition by the time you start; check before buying, but the 6th is genuinely excellent.
