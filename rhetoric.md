# A Traditional Curriculum in Rhetoric

*For trivium completion — full classical method (theory + progymnasmata + imitatio + declamatio).*

---

## Preface

### Where this sits in the trivium

Rhetoric is the third stage of the trivium and presupposes the first two. Grammar gave you the *correctness* of expression; logic gave you the *validity* of argument; rhetoric gives you their *application* to a living audience under contingent circumstances. Aristotle is explicit (*Rhetoric* I.2) that rhetoric is the counterpart (*antistrophos*) of dialectic — it borrows logic's machinery but operates in the realm of the probable rather than the demonstrative, addressing whole human beings rather than disembodied propositions. Keep this hinge in mind: every classical author you will read assumes you can already think a syllogism and parse a sentence.

### The two tracks

Traditional rhetorical education ran two tracks in parallel from the start, never sequentially:

1. **Theoretical (the *technē*)** — reading and digesting the system: the genera, the canons, the pisteis, the figures, the *stasis* doctrine.
2. **Practical (the exercises)** — the *progymnasmata* (preliminary exercises), *imitatio* (imitation of model authors), and *declamatio* (full mock speeches).

A curriculum that reads Aristotle but never writes a *chreia*, or composes essays but never analyzes Demosthenes, is not classical rhetoric — it is half a thing. The full method is what produces the result.

### Pedagogical principles

- **Slow, with the pen in hand.** Read with a commonplace book. Copy out striking periods, figures, and arguments. Quintilian (X.3) makes the case that careful slow writing is the foundation of fluent speech.
- **Recursion over progression.** The progymnasmata were not done once. They were repeated with new subjects of increasing difficulty across years. Treat each exercise as a *form* you return to, not a box to tick.
- **Editions matter.** Where a Loeb is available, prefer it: facing-page text gives you the original to glance at even without language competence, and the Loebs are the standard scholarly reference for citation.
- **No silent reading at the capstone level.** Declamation was spoken aloud. Read your imitations and exercises aloud, with attention to rhythm (*numerus*). This is non-negotiable for the *elocutio* canon.

### How to use the phases

The five phases below are a *sequence* for first acquaintance, not a partition. Each phase establishes new material; later phases assume earlier material is now part of your working equipment. Expect to keep returning to Aristotle and Quintilian throughout — these are not books you finish, they are books you furnish a mind with.

Acquire these first; they sit on the desk for the duration.

## Phase I — Foundations: The Greek Theoretical Tradition

**Rationale.** You begin with the founding philosophical quarrel about rhetoric itself (Plato vs. the Sophists), then take the system from the man who first organized it (Aristotle), then meet the dissenting tradition (Isocrates) that shaped actual education for the next thousand years. The exercises begin immediately at their simplest level.

### Reading

1. Plato, *Gorgias* — Donald Zeyl trans. (Hackett, 1987) or Robin Waterfield (Oxford World's Classics). The prosecution case against rhetoric. Read closely; the rest of the tradition is in part a reply to this dialogue.
2. Plato, *Phaedrus* — Nehamas & Woodruff trans. (Hackett, 1995). Plato's qualified rehabilitation. The myth of Theuth and the critique of writing are essential.
3. Gorgias, *Encomium of Helen* and *Defense of Palamedes* — in *The Older Sophists*, ed. Rosamond Kent Sprague (Hackett, 2001). The display pieces against which Plato is reacting.
4. Isocrates, *Against the Sophists* and *Antidosis* — in *Isocrates I*, trans. David Mirhady & Yun Lee Too (U Texas Press, 2000). Isocrates is the *educational* alternative to both Plato and the Sophists, and the actual model for the rhetorical school.
5. Aristotle, *Rhetoric* — George A. Kennedy trans., *On Rhetoric: A Theory of Civic Discourse*, 2nd ed. (Oxford UP, 2007). Kennedy's introduction and notes are the best in English.
6. (Pseudo-Aristotle) *Rhetoric to Alexander* — trans. in *The Complete Works of Aristotle*, ed. Jonathan Barnes (Princeton UP, 1984), vol. 2. Read selectively; useful for showing what a more technical handbook looked like alongside Aristotle.

### What to extract from Aristotle (the systematic core)

You should be able, by the end of this phase, to recite from memory:

- The three genera: *symbouleutikon* (deliberative), *dikanikon* (judicial), *epideiktikon* (epideictic), and their characteristic times, ends, and topics.
- The three pisteis: *ēthos*, *pathos*, *logos*.
- The enthymeme and the example (*paradeigma*) as the rhetorical counterparts to syllogism and induction.
- The *koinoi topoi* (common topics) — particularly the more/less, possible/impossible, past/future fact, and degree.

### Exercises

Begin the progymnasmata at exercises 1–4. Use Kennedy's *Progymnasmata: Greek Textbooks of Prose Composition and Rhetoric* (SBL, 2003) as your sourcebook for all 14. For each exercise: read the relevant treatments in Theon, Hermogenes, Aphthonius, and Nicolaus (Kennedy gives all four); then compose at least two of your own.

- **Exercise 1 — Fable (*mythos*).** Compose three fables, each with explicit *epimythium* (moral). Vary length: a tight one, a medium one, an expanded one with characterization.
- **Exercise 2 — Narrative (*diēgēma*).** Compose narratives of a historical event in each of the four manners Theon describes: direct, indirect, by interrogation, by enumeration. The same content, four forms.
- **Exercise 3 — Chreia.** Take a saying or deed attributed to a known figure. Develop it according to Hermogenes' eight headings: praise of the speaker, paraphrase, cause, contrary, comparison, example, testimony of the ancients, epilogue.
- **Exercise 4 — Proverb (*gnōmē*).** As with the chreia, but for a maxim rather than an anecdote.

### Capstone

Write a *defense of rhetoric* against the charges in Plato's *Gorgias*, ~2000 words. Use Aristotle's framework. The point is not originality but command of the materials.

## Phase II — Roman Synthesis: Cicero and Quintilian

**Rationale.** The Greek system reaches its mature pedagogical form in Latin. The *Ad Herennium* is the schoolbook (and the first surviving complete Latin rhetoric); Cicero is the system embodied in a working orator; Quintilian is the comprehensive pedagogical synthesis of the whole tradition. This phase is the longest and the most important.

### Reading

1. *Rhetorica ad Herennium* — Harry Caplan trans., Loeb (Harvard UP, 1954). The compact handbook. Read in full. Caplan's notes are a course in themselves.
2. Cicero, *De Inventione* — H. M. Hubbell trans., Loeb (Harvard UP, 1949). Cicero's early treatise; useful as a counterpart to *Ad Herennium* (the two were taught together for centuries).
3. Cicero, *De Oratore* — James M. May & Jakob Wisse trans., *On the Ideal Orator* (Oxford UP, 2001). This is the central work. Read slowly.
4. Cicero, *Orator* and *Brutus* — in Loeb editions (Hubbell; Hendrickson). *Brutus* is also the closest thing antiquity has to a history of Roman oratory.
5. Cicero, selected speeches — Catilinarians, *Pro Milone*, *Pro Caelio*, the first and second Philippics. Use Loeb or the Oxford World's Classics translations. Read each with an analytical eye for arrangement (*dispositio*) and style (*elocutio*).
6. Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria* — Donald A. Russell trans., *The Orator's Education*, 5 vols., Loeb (Harvard UP, 2001). The magnum opus of Roman rhetorical education. *Do not skip any book.* Notable concentrations:
   - Book I — early education (the trivium *in nuce*)
   - Books III–VI — invention and arrangement
   - Books VIII–IX — style and the figures (the densest treatment in the tradition)
   - Book X — the reading list and the imitation method
   - Book XII — the orator as *vir bonus dicendi peritus*
7. Tacitus, *Dialogus de Oratoribus* — W. Peterson trans., revised M. Winterbottom, Loeb (Harvard UP, 1970). The elegant post-mortem on republican oratory and its causes.

### Exercises

- Continue progymnasmata exercises 5–10:
  - **5. Refutation (*anaskeuē*)** and **6. Confirmation (*kataskeuē*)** — take a narrative (e.g., a myth, a historical anecdote) and argue against, then for, its credibility, using the headings: obscure, incredible, impossible, inconsistent, unfitting, inexpedient.
  - **7. Commonplace (*koinos topos*)** — amplification on a generic theme (against tyranny, against the temple-robber, in praise of the law-giver). The first sustained exercise in *amplificatio*.
  - **8. Encomium (*enkōmion*)** — under standard headings: origin (race, homeland, ancestors, parents), upbringing (education, art, principles), deeds (of soul, of body, of fortune), comparison, epilogue. Compose three: one historical figure, one mythological, one impersonal (e.g., on the bee, on a virtue).
  - **9. Invective (*psogos*)** — the inverse structure of the encomium.
  - **10. Comparison (*synkrisis*)** — the encomium and invective fused. Compose at least two (e.g., Achilles vs. Hector; the active life vs. the contemplative).

- Begin formal **imitatio** of Cicero (see *The Imitatio Program* below). Take a paragraph of *Pro Caelio* or the First Catilinarian and put it through the four-step process: translate (here, render in your own English), paraphrase, vary, rival.

### Capstone

A *suasoria* of ~1500–2000 words: advise a historical figure at a decision-point (Quintilian's own example: "Cicero deliberates whether to burn his writings, since Antony promises him his life if he does"). Take it through all five canons explicitly — annotate your draft with *inventio* notes, *dispositio* outline, *elocutio* observations, *memoria* technique used, *pronuntiatio* directions for delivery.

## Phase III — Style, the Figures, and Hellenistic Refinement

**Rationale.** The Greek tradition produced a body of stylistic criticism in the Hellenistic and Roman-Greek periods that complements the Roman handbooks. This phase deepens the *elocutio* canon — the figures of speech, the levels of style, the question of the sublime — to a level that the broader treatises only sketch.

### Reading

1. Demetrius, *On Style* — Doreen C. Innes trans., Loeb (Harvard UP, 1995, in the same volume as Aristotle's *Poetics* and Longinus). The four-style doctrine (plain, grand, elegant, forcible).
2. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *On Literary Composition* and the critical essays on Demosthenes and Lysias — Stephen Usher trans., Loeb, 2 vols. (Harvard UP, 1974/1985). The most precise treatment of word-arrangement (*synthesis*) in antiquity.
3. (Pseudo-)Longinus, *On the Sublime* — D. A. Russell trans., Loeb (Harvard UP, 1995, revised). The treatise on greatness in style.
4. Hermogenes, *On Types of Style* — Cecil W. Wooten trans. (UNC Press, 1987). The Byzantine standard — seven ideas of style with sub-types. Dense; read alongside Wooten's introduction.
5. Quintilian, *Institutio* VIII–IX — *reread* now with these treatises in mind. The dialogue between Quintilian and the Greek tradition becomes audible only on the second pass.

### Companion: the figures of speech

This is the moment to take the figures seriously rather than as ornaments. Two ways in:

- The figures section of Lausberg (§§ 538–910) — encyclopedic; consult by need.
- Henry Peacham, *The Garden of Eloquence* (1593) — facsimile via Scolar Press, or online via EEBO (Early English Books Online). The Renaissance English exposition; each figure with definition, examples, and *use*. Excellent training in noticing figures in the wild.

### Exercises

- Complete the progymnasmata, exercises 11–14:
  - **11. Speech-in-character (*ēthopoeia*/*prosopopoeia*).** Compose the speech that a historical or mythological figure would deliver at a particular moment. ("What would Andromache say upon learning of Hector's death?") The classical subject par excellence; demands all three pisteis.
  - **12. Description (*ekphrasis*).** Three descriptions: of a person, of a place, of an event. Apply the criteria of clarity (*saphēneia*) and vividness (*enargeia*) drawn from Demetrius and Dionysius.
  - **13. Thesis.** Argue a general question (not "Should Socrates marry Xanthippe?" but "Should a wise man marry?"). The transition into philosophical rhetoric. Compose for and against.
  - **14. Introduction of a law (*legem ferre*).** Propose and defend a fictional statute. Treat as a deliberative speech with all the standard topics: justice, expediency, possibility, honor.

- Sustained **figures program.** Choose 20 figures from Peacham. For each: (a) write a card with definition; (b) collect three examples from your reading; (c) compose three of your own. Build to 60 figures over this phase.

- **Style imitation.** Take three Ciceronian sentences and three Demosthenic sentences. Imitate each in English at three lengths: shorter, equal, expanded. The point is to feel period structure from the inside.

### Capstone

An *ekphrasis* of substantial length (~2000 words) on a complex scene — a battle, a ceremony, a building — in which you deliberately deploy and label at least fifteen figures of speech. Annotate the margin with the figure name at each occurrence.

## Phase IV — Declamation and the Christian–Medieval Continuation

**Rationale.** Declamation was the capstone of ancient rhetorical training; the Christian and medieval centuries took the inherited apparatus and turned it to new ends (preaching, letter-writing, legal pleading). This phase makes you a declaimer and shows you the long transmission.

### Reading

1. Seneca the Elder, *Controversiae* and *Suasoriae* — Michael Winterbottom trans., 2 vols., Loeb (Harvard UP, 1974). Read in full. The single best source for what advanced classical declamation actually sounded like.
2. The Pseudo-Quintilianic *Minor Declamations* — D. R. Shackleton Bailey trans., Loeb (Harvard UP, 2006). The schoolmaster's declamations, with model arguments — invaluable as worked examples of *inventio*.
3. The Pseudo-Quintilianic *Major Declamations* — selections, Lewis Sussman trans. (Lang, 1987). The grand show pieces.
4. Augustine, *De Doctrina Christiana*, Book IV — R. P. H. Green trans. (Oxford UP, 1995). The Christian appropriation of Cicero — the three levels of style baptized.
5. Cassiodorus, *Institutiones* II.2 (on rhetoric); Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae* II — both available in modern English translations (Halporn/Vessey; Barney et al., Cambridge UP, 2006). The compressed transmission to the early Middle Ages.
6. Geoffrey of Vinsauf, *Poetria Nova* — Margaret Nims trans., revised Martin Camargo (PIMS, 2010). The medieval verse handbook on composition.
7. *The Rhetoric of Cicero in Its Medieval and Early Renaissance Commentary Tradition*, ed. Cox & Ward (Brill, 2006) — selected chapters, for the medieval *artes* (dictaminis, praedicandi, poetriae).
8. Robert of Basevorn, *Forma Praedicandi* — in *Three Medieval Rhetorical Arts*, ed. James J. Murphy (U California Press, 1971). The medieval art of preaching.

### Exercises

- **Compose four full declamations.**
  1. A *suasoria* on a historical theme (e.g., "Caesar deliberates whether to cross the Rubicon").
  2. A *controversia* — invent a stock scenario in the Senecan manner (the disinherited son, the pirate's daughter, the priestess who survived the brothel). Argue both sides in two separate declamations.
  3. A Christian sermon (~15–20 minutes spoken) on a scriptural text, organized after the *Forma Praedicandi*: theme, protheme, division, development of members, conclusion.
  4. A formal letter in the medieval *ars dictaminis* structure: *salutatio*, *exordium* (or *captatio benevolentiae*), *narratio*, *petitio*, *conclusio*.

- **Imitatio shift.** Switch your model author for this phase from Cicero to Augustine (for prose rhythm and Christian-classical fusion). Take a passage of *Confessions* X or *De Doctrina* IV through the four-step process.

- **Declaim aloud.** Each of the four compositions above is to be delivered aloud, in private, at full voice, ideally to a friend or recording. Time yourself. Mark a copy of the text for delivery (*pronuntiatio*) — pauses, emphases, gestures.

### Capstone

A *controversia* of ~3000 words, argued on both sides, with a brief methodological postface (~500 words) in which you reflect on how the same materials of *inventio* produced opposite conclusions. This is the classical capacity *in utramque partem dicere* — the trained mind's ability to argue either side.

## Phase V — Renaissance Synthesis and the Full Method

**Rationale.** The Renaissance recovered the ancient corpus, integrated it with the Christian middle ages, and produced both the most exuberant pedagogical handbooks (Erasmus) and the most influential reforms (Ramus). This phase consolidates the system and gives you the vernacular Anglophone branch (Wilson, Peacham, Puttenham).

### Reading

1. Erasmus, *De Copia* — Betty I. Knott trans., *Copia: Foundations of the Abundant Style*, in *Collected Works of Erasmus*, vol. 24 (U Toronto Press, 1978). Book I is the heart: 200 variations on a single sentence. Do them.
2. Erasmus, *De Conscribendis Epistolis* — Charles Fantazzi trans., in *CWE* vol. 25 (U Toronto Press, 1985). The Renaissance reinvention of letter-writing.
3. Erasmus, selected *Adagia* — *CWE* vols. 31–36 (selections). For the practice of commonplace.
4. Philip Melanchthon, *Elementa Rhetorices* — selections in English in Schneider's *Philip Melanchthon's Rhetorical Construal of Biblical Authority* (Lewiston, 1990) or the Latin (Stupperich edition).
5. Thomas Wilson, *The Art of Rhetoric* (1560) — ed. Peter E. Medine (Penn State UP, 1994). The first major English rhetoric.
6. Henry Peacham, *The Garden of Eloquence* (1593) — facsimile (Scolar Press) or EEBO. Reread in full now.
7. George Puttenham, *The Art of English Poesy* — eds. Frank Whigham & Wayne A. Rebhorn (Cornell UP, 2007). The vernacular synthesis of rhetoric and poetics in Elizabethan English.
8. Peter Ramus & Audomarus Talaeus — *Arguments in Rhetoric Against Quintilian*, trans. Carole Newlands (Northern Illinois UP, 1986). The Ramist reform — controversial, historically pivotal, worth knowing as the moment the classical canons were torn apart and reassigned between logic and rhetoric.
9. Walter J. Ong, *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue* (Harvard UP, 1958; reprint U Chicago Press, 2004). Read after the Ramus primary text. Ong's diagnosis of what the Ramist reform did to the tradition is essential context.

### Exercises

- **Erasmian copia.** Take ten sentences of your own. Produce 50 variations of each. (Erasmus did 200 on one; ten times fifty is a humane equivalent.)
- **Commonplace book.** If you have not been keeping one, start now. Organize by topic (*loci communes*). Enter striking sentences from your reading under their headings. By the end of this phase, aim for several hundred entries across 30+ heads.
- **Final imitatio.** Choose any one author from the whole curriculum — Demosthenes, Cicero, Quintilian, Augustine, Erasmus — and produce a sustained imitation: ~2000 words in their voice on a subject they did not treat.

### Capstone — the full classical capstone

Compose three orations of ~2500 words each, one in each of the three Aristotelian genera, on subjects of your choosing:

1. A **deliberative** speech (advise an audience on a future course of action).
2. A **judicial** speech (accuse or defend in a hypothetical case).
3. An **epideictic** speech (praise or blame a person, action, or thing).

Each must:
- Take an explicit position on all five canons (annotate in marginalia).
- Use all three pisteis demonstrably.
- Deploy at least 20 named figures (mark in margin).
- Be delivered aloud and timed.
- Be accompanied by a short (~500 word) self-criticism in the manner of Quintilian X — what you imitated, what you departed from, what failed.

This is the *vir bonus dicendi peritus* exercise.

## The Imitatio Program

The locus classicus is Quintilian, *Institutio* X.2. The Renaissance reformulation is Erasmus, *De Copia*. The four-step technique (drawn from both):

1. **Translate or render.** If from Latin or Greek, translate. If from English to English, transpose register (high to plain, plain to high). The point is to break the original's hold on phrasing.
2. **Paraphrase.** Restate in different words but the same arrangement.
3. **Vary.** Recast in a different arrangement — period reordered, subordination changed, figures substituted. Erasmus's 200 variations on *Tuae litterae me magnopere delectaverunt* (Your letter pleased me greatly) is the model.
4. **Rival (*aemulatio*).** Compose your own original passage on a fresh subject in the model's manner. This is the goal, not mere copying — Quintilian is firm: imitation that does not transcend its model is not yet imitation.

Recommended model authors, in order of introduction:

- Phase II: Cicero (the periodic Latin sentence in its grandest form).
- Phase III: Demosthenes (the controlled forcible style) and Tacitus (the compressed, abrupt Silver Latin counterpoint).
- Phase IV: Augustine (the classical-Christian fusion).
- Phase V: Erasmus (vernacular humanism) and any one English Renaissance prose author (Hooker, Donne's sermons, Browne).

Do not imitate more than one author at a time. Quintilian X.2.24: he who tries to follow many will catch none.

## The Declamation Program

Declamation has two species:

- **Suasoria** (deliberative): advise a historical or mythological figure at a decision point. The Senecan stock topics: glory vs. safety, immediate vs. eventual advantage, what the gods will reward, what posterity will say.
- **Controversia** (judicial): argue a side in a fictitious legal case, governed by a stock law and a lurid set of facts.

Sample suasoria prompts (Senecan and post-Senecan):

- The 300 Spartans deliberate whether to retreat from Thermopylae.
- Alexander deliberates whether to sail the outer Ocean.
- Cicero deliberates whether to beg Antony's pardon.
- Cato deliberates whether to kill himself after Thapsus.

Sample controversia setups (in the Senecan manner — always begin with the stock law, then the case):

- *Law:* The disinherited son shall not enter his father's house. *Case:* A son disinherited by his father returns secretly to nurse him through a fatal illness; the father, restored, leaves him everything; the brothers contest the will.
- *Law:* A priestess must be chaste and from a chaste family. *Case:* A virgin, captured by pirates, sold to a brothel, slew a soldier who attempted her, was acquitted, and seeks the priesthood.

These are not historical or moral questions. The point is the training of *inventio* and *dispositio* under constraint.

Declaim four full pieces (two suasoriae, two controversiae) by the end of Phase IV. In Phase V, declaim the three capstone orations.

## On Method: How to Read These Texts

A few discipline-specific reading habits worth fixing from the start.

- **Three passes.** First pass: read straight through for sense, without notes. Second pass: read with the pen, marking divisions, figures, key claims. Third pass: outline in a few hundred words. Most of the primary texts in this curriculum repay all three.
- **Outline as the ancients did.** When you outline a speech, separate explicitly: *exordium*, *narratio*, *partitio*, *confirmatio*, *refutatio*, *peroratio*. When the text deviates, ask why.
- **The figure-hunt.** As soon as you have Lanham or Peacham at hand, no page of primary text is read without spotting and naming the figures. This becomes automatic by Phase III.
- **Aloud, again.** Aristotle is dense and Quintilian is long; both are easier read aloud in stretches of half an hour. Cicero's speeches require it. Augustine's prose rhythm (the *cursus*) can only be felt aloud.
- **Commonplace from day one.** A physical notebook, organized by topic. Entries copied verbatim with citation. Quintilian (II.7), Erasmus, and the entire humanist tradition cannot be wrong on this point.

## A Note on Editions and Languages

You will read everything in English translation; the curriculum does not assume Latin or Greek. Where Loeb editions are recommended, the facing-page original is a benefit even without language competence — you can spot a *kairos* or a *concinnitas* even on cursory acquaintance with the original word.

If at any point you decide to add a classical language, Latin is the natural choice: it unlocks Cicero, Quintilian, *Ad Herennium*, Seneca, Augustine, Erasmus, and the Renaissance handbooks in one stroke. Wheelock's *Latin* (7th ed.) and then the LLPSI series (Ørberg, *Familia Romana* and *Roma Aeterna*) are the standard self-study route; *Ad Herennium* is the conventional first sustained text. This is optional and outside the curriculum proper.

## Closing Remarks

The traditional curriculum's ambition is not a body of knowledge but a *habitus* — a trained disposition of mind and tongue. You will know you have completed it not when you have read the books but when you cannot read a leading article, hear a speech, or write a memo without noticing the figures, judging the arrangement, and feeling the *kairos* of the moment. Quintilian's promise (XII.1) — that the orator is *vir bonus dicendi peritus*, a good man skilled in speaking — was that the technical skill and the moral character ripen together. The classical method holds you to both.
