# Logic and the Entry into Aristotle, Plato, and the Platonist Tradition

**A revised 18-month curriculum, with a longer continuation into the Platonist tradition.**

**Target:** Read Aristotle and Plato seriously, and the Platonist tradition (Plotinus through to modern revivals) with proper grounding in both ancient and modern logic. Aristotelian logic gets primary technical attention; modern logic is compressed to its essential core (foundations through Gödel).

**Logic emphasis:** Lean toward Aristotelian + medieval logic in the core. Modern logic carried up through soundness/completeness/Gödel within Months 1–18. **Track C** (modal, non-classical, specialisation, philosophy of mathematics) restores the advanced modern logic depth, positioned as a parallel continuation alongside the Platonist tradition.

**Greek:** Vocabulary-level only. Running thread throughout, using F. E. Peters' *Greek Philosophical Terms* as the working reference. Loeb editions used for parallel Greek-English access to key passages.

**Assumptions about you (unchanged):**
- Mathematical maturity from theoretical physics; rigorous proofs are familiar.
- Programming/CS familiarity; Gödel-numbering and decidability arguments are conceptually less alien than to most philosophy students.
- Polish reading proficiency — this gives direct access to the Lvov–Warsaw school's work on Aristotelian logic (Łukasiewicz, Bocheński, Klima as a continuator), which is unusually well-placed to bridge ancient and modern formal logic. Polish-language options are flagged where relevant.

**Format conventions:**
- *Primary text* = spine of the phase. Read carefully, do exercises where applicable.
- *Companion* = read alongside the primary text for alternative treatment or commentary.
- *Supplementary* = consult selectively.
- *Optional* = if time and curiosity allow.
- Page counts approximate; reading rates calibrated to ~5–10 pp/hour with exercises, slower for dense proofs or primary ancient texts.

---

## The Three Tracks

The curriculum runs on three tracks:

**Track A — Modern Logic.** Core: Phases 1, 3, 5 (within Months 1–18). Continuation: **Track C** (Phases C1–C4, ~6 months after the core, or interleaved with the Platonist continuation).

**Track B — Aristotelian / Medieval Logic and Ancient Philosophy.** Core: Phases 2, 4, 6, 7 (within Months 1–18). Continuation: Phases 9–13 (~14 months, the Platonist tradition through to modern revivals).

**Phase 8** is the bridge between traditions and closes the 18-month core.

The 18-month core interleaves Tracks A and B. Aristotle's *Organon* (Phase 2) follows a modern foundations phase because Łukasiewicz's reconstruction of the syllogistic — your principal companion on Aristotle's logic — requires modern formal vocabulary. Plato (Phase 6) comes after the medieval logic phase so that you read Plato with a working sense of how Platonist metaphysics and Aristotelian logic became entangled in the medieval reception.

After the core, the two continuations (Track C and the Platonist tradition) are independent and can be pursued in either order, in series, or interleaved. Specific interleaving recommendations are at the end of Track C.

## Phase 1 — Foundations of Modern Classical Logic (Months 1–2)

**Goal:** Fluency with the syntax and semantics of propositional and first-order logic. Enough to read Łukasiewicz's formal reconstruction of Aristotle in Phase 2.

### Primary text

- **Halbach, Volker. *The Logic Manual.* Oxford University Press, 2010. ~200 pp.**
  - Tight, mathematically respectable, used at Oxford. Companion exercises booklet free online: [logicmanual.philosophy.ox.ac.uk](http://logicmanual.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/). Do them.
  - **Coverage required:** chapters on propositional logic, predicate logic with identity, natural deduction.
  - **Skip for now:** metalogical proofs (return in Phase 3).
  - **Pace:** 6–7 weeks at 5–6 hrs/week.

### Supplementary

- Sider, Theodore. *Logic for Philosophy.* OUP, 2010. Chapters 1–4 as parallel reading — Sider's philosophical motivation (why first-order, what validity means) is excellent.

### Greek thread begins

- **Peters, F. E. *Greek Philosophical Terms: A Historical Lexicon.* NYU Press, 1967. ~234 pp.**
  - Your working reference for Greek philosophical vocabulary. Read through the introduction and the entries for the ~30 most basic terms (*ousia, eidos, hyle, morphe, energeia, dynamis, telos, logos, nous, psyche, sophia, episteme, doxa, dialektike, arche, kosmos, physis, kinesis, ergon, hexis, aretē, agathon, kalon, alētheia, on, mē on, genesis, ousia, hypokeimenon, hypostasis*) over these two months. ~30 min/week.
  - This continues at low intensity throughout the curriculum. You're building a *working vocabulary*, not learning Greek.

### Bonus connection

- Compositionality of formal semantics ↔ a question Aristotle and the Stoics already understood when they distinguished *legomena kata symplokēn* (things said in combination) from terms said in isolation (*Categories* 1a16, *De Interpretatione* ch. 1). You'll see it formalised again in Phase 2.

## Phase 2 — Aristotle's *Organon* and the Syllogistic (Months 3–5)

**Goal:** Read the *Organon* with both ancient and modern eyes. Understand the categorical syllogistic, its modal extension, the theory of demonstration, and Aristotle's account of definition. This is where Aristotelian logic gets the serious treatment the previous draft of the curriculum denied it.

### Primary texts (Aristotle himself)

- **Aristotle. *Categories* and *De Interpretatione*.** Translated by J. L. Ackrill. Clarendon Aristotle Series, OUP, 1963. ~158 pp incl. commentary.
  - Ackrill's commentary is dense but unmatched. Slow reading.
- **Aristotle. *Prior Analytics* Book I.** Translated by Gisela Striker. Clarendon Aristotle Series, OUP, 2009. ~268 pp incl. commentary.
  - The book that founded formal logic. Striker's commentary handles the modal syllogistic — the hardest part — better than any predecessor.
- **Aristotle. *Posterior Analytics*.** Translated by Jonathan Barnes. 2nd ed., Clarendon Aristotle Series, OUP, 1993. ~302 pp incl. commentary.
  - Aristotle's theory of scientific knowledge and demonstration. Reading order matters: this is where the syllogistic *does work* metaphysically.
- **Aristotle. *Topics* (selections, Books I and VIII).** Translated by Robin Smith, *Topics I and VIII*, Clarendon Aristotle Series, OUP, 1997.
  - The art of dialectical argument. Bridge to Plato (Phase 6).

### Modern companion

- **Łukasiewicz, Jan. *Aristotle's Syllogistic from the Standpoint of Modern Formal Logic.* 2nd ed., OUP, 1957. ~222 pp.**
  - The foundational modern reconstruction of Aristotle's syllogistic as an axiomatic system. Reading this *alongside* Striker's translation of *Prior Analytics* lets you see Aristotle from two angles at once. Polish original: *Sylogistyka Arystotelesa z punktu widzenia współczesnej logiki formalnej* (PWN, 1957).
  - Łukasiewicz makes specific claims (the syllogistic is a propositional theory of terms, not a predicate-calculus fragment) that Corcoran and others contested — read with critical attention.

### Companions / secondary

- **Smith, Robin. "Logic." In *The Cambridge Companion to Aristotle*, edited by Jonathan Barnes, CUP, 1995, pp. 27–65.**
  - The clearest short overview. Read at the start of the phase as orientation.
- Corcoran, John. "Aristotle's Natural Deduction System." In *Ancient Logic and Its Modern Interpretations*, ed. Corcoran, Reidel, 1974.
  - The alternative reading: Aristotle's syllogistic is a natural deduction system, not Łukasiewicz's axiomatic one. Read after Łukasiewicz to see the debate.
- Lear, Jonathan. *Aristotle and Logical Theory.* Cambridge, 1980. ~135 pp.
  - Slim and philosophical. Excellent on the relationship between the syllogistic and the theory of demonstration.

### Stanford Encyclopedia entries

Mandatory background; read each before the corresponding Aristotelian text:
- "Aristotle's Logic"
- "The Square of Opposition"
- "Aristotle on Non-Contradiction"
- "Medieval Theories of the Syllogism" (forward reference for Phase 4)

### Greek thread

Add terms specific to Aristotle's logic: *protasis, syllogismos, horos, schema, mesos, akros, apodeixis, epagōgē, archē, aitia, dialektikē, sophistikē, endoxon, peri tinos*. Use Loeb's *Prior Analytics* (Hugh Tredennick, Loeb 1938) for parallel Greek-English access to key passages.

### Pace

13 weeks at 6 hrs/week. Slower on Striker's *Prior Analytics* (especially the modal syllogistic in chapters 8–22 — this is genuinely hard), faster on *Topics*.

### Bonus connections

- Łukasiewicz axiomatised the syllogistic using a propositional logic over *terms* (variables ranging over predicates). This is structurally close to what modern type theory does when it treats predicates as first-class. You'll recognise the move.
- Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* on first principles being known *per se* and grounding demonstration is the historical ancestor of Hilbert's programme — the question of whether mathematics rests on self-evident axioms. The Frege/Hilbert/Gödel sequence in Phase 5 will land differently after reading the *Posterior Analytics*.

## Phase 3 — Modern Metalogic, Compressed (Months 6–7)

**Goal:** Prove soundness and completeness for first-order logic. Understand compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem at a working level. Enough machinery to make Gödel's theorems meaningful in Phase 5. Less depth than the previous draft of this curriculum, since model theory for its own sake is not your target.

### Primary text

- **van Dalen, Dirk. *Logic and Structure.* 5th ed., Springer, 2013. ~263 pp.**
  - Read chapters 1–4. Skip chapters 5–6 (intuitionistic logic and normalisation — would be central if you were doing the previous curriculum, optional here).
  - **Pace:** 7–8 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

### Set-theory primer if needed

- Goldrei, Derek. *Classic Set Theory: For Guided Independent Study.* Chapman & Hall/CRC, 1996. Selected chapters on cardinality, well-ordering, and ordinals (~3 weeks). Skip if you already have this from physics.

### Companion (gentler alternative if van Dalen is too compressed)

- Hunter, Geoffrey. *Metalogic: An Introduction to the Metatheory of Standard First-Order Logic.* University of California Press, 1971. ~289 pp.

### Bonus connection

- The compactness theorem (every finitely satisfiable set is satisfiable) has the same flavour as topological compactness from analysis — not coincidence; the Stone space of complete theories is genuinely compact in the topological sense.

## Phase 4 — Medieval Logic (Months 8–9)

**Goal:** Engage with the great elaboration of Aristotelian logic that the modern formal tradition ignored for centuries and is now recovering. This is technically demanding — supposition theory, the theory of consequences, treatments of insolubles (semantic paradoxes), and modal logic — and intellectually beautiful.

### Primary text (modern overview)

- **Dutilh Novaes, Catarina, and Stephen Read, eds. *The Cambridge Companion to Medieval Logic.* Cambridge University Press, 2016. ~370 pp.**
  - The standard modern reference. Each chapter is by a specialist. Read in order; the chapters on supposition theory, consequences, and obligations are particularly important.
  - **Pace:** 5 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

### Companion (free, important)

- **Spade, Paul Vincent. *Thoughts, Words and Things: An Introduction to Late Medieval Logic and Semantic Theory.* 2nd ed., 2002. ~351 pp.**
  - Free PDF from the author at [pvspade.com/Logic/](https://pvspade.com/Logic/). Spade is exceptionally clear. Recommended over the Cambridge Companion if you want a *single* author's perspective rather than an edited collection.

### History (the canonical reference)

- **Bocheński, Józef Maria. *A History of Formal Logic.* Translated by Ivo Thomas. University of Notre Dame Press, 1961. ~567 pp.**
  - Originally Polish (*Formale Logik*, in German first, but Bocheński was Polish-Swiss and central to the Polish school). Read the medieval sections (Part III) selectively as reference. This stays on your shelf for life.

### Selected primary sources

- William of Ockham. *Ockham's Theory of Terms: Part I of the Summa Logicae.* Translated by Michael J. Loux. University of Notre Dame Press, 1974. ~221 pp. Read selected chapters.
- John Buridan. *Summulae de Dialectica.* Translated and edited by Gyula Klima. Yale University Press, 2001. ~1024 pp. — vast; read Treatise 4 (on supposition) and Treatise 8 (on demonstration). Klima is Hungarian and continues the central-European tradition of careful work on Buridan.
- Peter of Spain. *Tractatus*, called afterwards *Summule Logicales*. Selected sections in Norman Kretzmann and Eleonore Stump (eds.), *The Cambridge Translations of Medieval Philosophical Texts, Vol. 1: Logic and the Philosophy of Language*, CUP, 1988.

### Stanford Encyclopedia entries (read before primary sources)

- "Medieval Theories of: Supposition" / "Consequence" / "Insolubles" / "Modality"
- "John Buridan"
- "William of Ockham"

### Bonus connection

- Medieval supposition theory anticipates a great deal of modern formal semantics — quantifier scope, the distinction between *suppositio personalis* and *suppositio simplex* maps roughly onto extensional vs intensional readings. Several of the medievals were doing recognisable semantic theory.
- The medieval treatment of *insolubilia* (semantic paradoxes, especially the Liar) anticipates Tarski and modern truth theory. Buridan's solutions in particular are worth knowing before you meet Tarski.

## Phase 5 — Gödel and Incompleteness (Months 10–11)

**Goal:** Understand Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems with their proofs, and what they do and do not say about mathematics, mind, and Platonism. This last is not incidental — Gödel was himself a mathematical Platonist, and the philosophical implications of his theorems are contested precisely along Platonist/anti-Platonist lines.

### Primary text

- **Smith, Peter. *An Introduction to Gödel's Theorems.* 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2013. ~388 pp.**
  - The canonical work for philosophers who want real proofs, not popularisations.
  - **Pace:** 8 weeks at 6 hrs/week. Slow down on the diagonalisation lemma and the second theorem.

### Companion

- Boolos, George S., John P. Burgess, and Richard C. Jeffrey. *Computability and Logic.* 5th ed., CUP, 2007. Computability chapters (1–8) in parallel with Smith.

### Essential supplement (anti-bullshit vaccine)

- **Franzén, Torkel. *Gödel's Theorem: An Incomplete Guide to Its Use and Abuse.* A K Peters, 2005. ~172 pp.**
  - Read after Smith. Inoculates against Lucas, Penrose, the Hofstadter-imitators, and the various postmodern misuses you will inevitably encounter when reading philosophy of mathematics or critical theory.

### Bonus connection to your philosophy targets

- Gödel's own Platonism is laid out in his "What Is Cantor's Continuum Problem?" (1947, supplementary section in 1964 reprint). After reading Smith, read this 20-page paper — it's a Platonist defence of mathematical realism by the man who proved the incompleteness theorems. You'll return to it when you read modern Platonism in Phase 13.

## Phase 6 — Plato (Months 12–14)

**Goal:** Read Plato as a philosopher whose problems you can articulate, not as a cultural monument. Cover the dialogues most important for the metaphysical and epistemological traditions you'll follow downstream.

### Translations

- **Cooper, John M., ed. *Plato: Complete Works.* Hackett, 1997.**
  - The single-volume English standard. Different translators per dialogue, all reputable. This is your reading edition.
- For specific dialogues with serious commentary, see individual entries below.

### Dialogues in reading order

The reading order is pedagogical, not chronological:

1. **Meno** (~30 pp) — On knowledge, recollection, virtue. Short, programmatic. *Cambridge Companion to Plato*, ch. on Meno first.
2. **Phaedo** (~70 pp) — On the soul, Forms, the argument for immortality. Translation + commentary: Gallop, *Plato: Phaedo* (Clarendon Plato Series, OUP, 1975).
3. **Republic** (~300 pp) — Read in full but with focus on Books V–VII (the metaphysical and epistemological core: divided line, sun, cave, theory of Forms). Commentary: Annas, *An Introduction to Plato's Republic*, OUP, 1981.
4. **Theaetetus** (~85 pp) — On knowledge. Commentary: Burnyeat, *The Theaetetus of Plato*, Hackett, 1990. This is one of the great pieces of philosophical commentary; it's worth the time.
5. **Sophist** (~75 pp) — On being, non-being, falsehood, the late Plato's revision of the theory of Forms. Commentary: Cornford, *Plato's Theory of Knowledge*, Routledge, 1935 (covers both Theaetetus and Sophist).
6. **Parmenides** (~70 pp) — Plato's most difficult dialogue. Self-critique of the Forms. Commentary: Cornford, *Plato and Parmenides*, Routledge, 1939; more recent: Gill and Ryan, *Plato: Parmenides*, Hackett, 1996.
7. **Timaeus** (~85 pp) — Cosmology, the Demiurge. The single most influential Platonic text in the later tradition (especially for Phases 9–11). Commentary: Cornford, *Plato's Cosmology*, Routledge, 1937.

### Secondary

- **Kraut, Richard, ed. *The Cambridge Companion to Plato.* CUP, 1992. ~564 pp.**
  - Read the relevant chapters as you go through each dialogue.

### Optional

- Vlastos, Gregory. *Platonic Studies.* Princeton, 1973. Selected essays.
- Sayre, Kenneth. *Plato's Late Ontology.* Princeton, 1983 — for serious work on the Parmenides and Sophist if you fall in love with the late dialogues.

### Greek thread

Read key passages in parallel with Loeb editions (W. R. M. Lamb and others, 1925–1935). The *Republic* and *Theaetetus* Loeb volumes are particularly useful. Continue building vocabulary; the *Sophist* introduces *koinōnia, methexis, summeixis* (modes of participation between Forms), which are critical for later Platonism.

### Pace

12 weeks at 6 hrs/week. Slow on Parmenides and Sophist; faster on Meno and parts of Republic.

## Phase 7 — Aristotle's *Metaphysics* and the Plato–Aristotle Relation (Months 15–16)

**Goal:** Read Aristotle's metaphysics with your work on Plato fresh, and see how Aristotle's critique of the theory of Forms shaped everything downstream. This completes the reading of Aristotle proper.

### Primary text

- **Aristotle. *Metaphysics.* Translated by W. D. Ross. The Complete Works of Aristotle, ed. Barnes, Princeton, 1984. Vol. 2.**
  - Or the *Metaphysics* in the Loeb Classical Library (Tredennick), useful for parallel Greek.
  - **Required reading:** Books I (Α), IV (Γ), VII (Ζ), VIII (Η), IX (Θ), XII (Λ).
  - Book I sets up the critique of Plato. Books VII–IX are the heart of the substance doctrine. Book XII contains the Prime Mover and the theological core that the later Platonist tradition absorbed and modified.

### Companions

- **Ross, W. D. *Aristotle's Metaphysics.* 2 vols, OUP, 1924.**
  - The canonical commentary; on the shelf for reference.
- **Frede, Michael, and Günther Patzig. *Aristoteles' "Metaphysik Z": Text, Übersetzung und Kommentar.* C. H. Beck, 1988.** (German.)
  - The standard modern commentary on Book Zeta. If your German is up to it; otherwise, see Frede's *Essays in Ancient Philosophy* (Minnesota, 1987) for chapters on Zeta in English.
- **Halper, Edward. *One and Many in Aristotle's Metaphysics: The Central Books.* Parmenides Publishing, 2005.**
  - A book-length commentary on Z, H, Θ. Excellent.

### Secondary on the Plato–Aristotle relation

- Cherniss, Harold. *Aristotle's Criticism of Plato and the Academy.* Johns Hopkins, 1944. Now dated but foundational; you'll find later scholars constantly arguing with it.
- Fine, Gail. *On Ideas: Aristotle's Criticism of Plato's Theory of Forms.* Clarendon, 1993.

### Stanford Encyclopedia entries

- "Aristotle's Metaphysics"
- "Substance"
- "Aristotle on Causality"

### Pace

8 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

## Phase 8 — Bridge: Philosophical Logic and the Modern Reception (Months 17–18)

**Goal:** Step back, integrate. Read about logic itself as a philosophical subject, and engage with the modern philosophers who returned to Aristotle and Plato through the back door of formal logic.

### Philosophy of logic

- **Quine, W. V. O. *Philosophy of Logic.* 2nd ed., Harvard, 1986. ~109 pp.**
- **Burgess, John P. *Philosophical Logic.* Princeton, 2009. ~153 pp.**

### Frege, Husserl, and the analytic-continental origin

Since you asked for both traditions:

- **Dummett, Michael. *Origins of Analytical Philosophy.* Harvard, 1993. ~210 pp.**
  - Dummett's argument that analytic and phenomenological philosophy share a common Frege-and-Husserl origin in anti-psychologism about logic. This is the linchpin for anyone wanting both traditions.
- **Husserl, Edmund. *Logical Investigations,* Volume 1: Prolegomena to Pure Logic.** Trans. Findlay, Routledge, 2001. ~250 pp.
  - Husserl arguing on logical grounds against psychologism — same fight Frege was having. Read as a logician would.

### Optional

- **Sommers, Fred. *The Logic of Natural Language.* Clarendon, 1982.**
  - Sommers' "term logic" is a serious modern attempt to revive an Aristotelian-style logic over terms rather than predicates. Controversial; intellectually serious. Read if you want to see what a contemporary defence of broadly Aristotelian logic looks like.
- **Anscombe, G. E. M., and P. T. Geach. *Three Philosophers: Aristotle, Aquinas, Frege.* Blackwell, 1961.**
  - Three short philosophical essays. The fact that this book exists at all — treating Aristotle, Aquinas, and Frege as commensurable — captures the integration you've been working toward.

### Pace

8 weeks at 5 hrs/week.

# End of Core Curriculum (~18 months)

At this point you've covered: modern logic up through Gödel; Aristotle's *Organon*, *Metaphysics*, and the medieval tradition that elaborated his logic; the Platonic dialogues central to the metaphysical tradition; and the philosophy-of-logic bridge into both analytic and continental philosophy.

You now have two parallel continuations: **Track C** (advanced modern logic, ~6 months) and the **Platonist tradition** (Phases 9–13, ~14 months). They are independent. You can pursue either, both in series, or interleave them. Many readers discover during the continuations that one direction pulls harder and reallocate accordingly — that's healthy.

You should also re-evaluate against alternative directions you might prefer: Aristotle's *Ethics* and *Politics*; later medieval Aristotelianism (Aquinas, Scotus); or the analytic philosophy proper that the logic in Phases 1–8 was preparing you for (Kripke, Lewis, Dummett's mature work, Quine, Williamson).

The Platonist continuation phases below are dated "Months 19–32" on the assumption that you do it immediately after the core. If you do Track C first, add ~6 months to all of those numbers.

# Track C — Advanced Modern Logic (Continuation, ~5–6 months)

**Goal:** Recover the modern logic depth the Aristotelian pivot displaced from the core. Modal and non-classical logic, plus one specialisation direction, plus philosophy of mathematics. Brings the modern logic side up to the "comprehensive" level you originally asked for, and connects back to the Platonist tradition through mathematical Platonism specifically.

**Positioning default:** all of Track C in series after Phase 8 and before Phase 9. Alternative interleavings at the end of this track.

## Phase C1 — Modal Logic (~2 months)

**Goal:** Working command of propositional modal logic, Kripke semantics, completeness for standard systems, and quantified modal logic. Essential for analytic metaphysics, philosophy of language, and a serious adult engagement with Aristotle's modal syllogistic.

### Primary text

- **Garson, James W. *Modal Logic for Philosophers.* 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2013. ~492 pp.**
  - The most thorough modal-logic text for philosophers. Cover the propositional modal core (K, T, S4, S5), Kripke semantics, soundness/completeness proofs, and quantified modal logic. Skim the chapters on specific applications.
  - **Pace:** 8–10 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

### Alternative (tighter)

- Sider, Theodore. *Logic for Philosophy.* OUP, 2010. Chapters 6–10 (~120 pp of relevant material).
  - Tighter and more opinionated. Natural if you've been using Sider as a parallel reference since Phase 1.

### Optional depth

- Fitting, Melvin, and Richard L. Mendelsohn. *First-Order Modal Logic.* Kluwer, 1998. ~292 pp. — Deeper quantified modal logic.

### Bonus connections

- Kripke frames are the same structures used in temporal/epistemic logic for formal verification (model checking, LTL/CTL specifications). If you've seen these in CS, the modal-logic core is structurally familiar.
- Possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals (Lewis, Stalnaker) is the philosophical home of the same machinery used in causal inference — Pearl's causal models are intentionally counterfactual. Worth flagging given your fraud-prevention work; the philosophical literature and the causal-ML literature are surprisingly disconnected.
- Aristotle's modal syllogistic (Phase 2) — you'll now have the apparatus to read Patzig, Hintikka, and Patterson on how to formalise it. Striker's commentary in Phase 2 already gestured at these debates.

## Phase C2 — Non-Classical Logics (~1.5 months)

**Goal:** Working familiarity with the major non-classical logics — intuitionistic, relevance, paraconsistent, many-valued, fuzzy, conditional — in a unified tableau framework.

### Primary text

- **Priest, Graham. *An Introduction to Non-Classical Logic: From If to Is.* 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2008. ~613 pp.**
  - Read Part I (propositional, ~300 pp) thoroughly. Sample Part II (first-order extensions) selectively.
  - **Pace:** 6 weeks at 5 hrs/week.

### Bonus connections

- Intuitionistic logic ↔ constructive proof ↔ type theory ↔ proof assistants (Coq, Agda, Lean). The Curry–Howard correspondence comes alive here, and is directly relevant to anyone with a programming background.
- Paraconsistent logic ↔ medieval treatments of insolubles (Phase 4, Buridan in particular). Priest is one of the contemporary champions of paraconsistency; reading him after the medievals shows the depth of the historical thread.

## Phase C3 — Specialisation: Model Theory or Proof Theory (~1.5 months, pick one)

**Goal:** Pick one direction for graduate-level depth. Both are beautiful; neither is necessary unless you intend to specialise. Skip this phase entirely if you'd rather get to philosophy of mathematics faster.

### Option A — Model Theory

- **Marker, David. *Model Theory: An Introduction.* Graduate Texts in Mathematics 217, Springer, 2002. ~342 pp.**
  - Graduate-level model theory: types, saturation, stability, o-minimality. Mathematically demanding; this is mathematical logic in a maths-department sense, not philosophy-of-logic.
  - **Pace:** 8 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

### Option B — Proof Theory

- **Troelstra, Anne, and Helmut Schwichtenberg. *Basic Proof Theory.* 2nd ed., CUP, 2000. ~430 pp.**
  - Sequent calculus, natural deduction, cut-elimination, normalisation, ordinal analysis. The Polish/Gentzen tradition continued.
  - **Pace:** 8 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

### Companion (for the Proof Theory route)

- Negri, Sara, and Jan von Plato. *Structural Proof Theory.* CUP, 2001. ~257 pp. — More accessible on the structural side. Read this first if you want a gentler entry, then return to Troelstra/Schwichtenberg.

## Phase C4 — Philosophy of Mathematics (~1 month)

**Goal:** Engage with the philosophical questions about mathematics — what mathematical objects are, what mathematical knowledge consists in, how the foundational programmes (logicism, formalism, intuitionism, set-theoretic Platonism) interact. This connects directly back to Phase 13 of the Platonist continuation (Gödel, mathematical realism, contemporary mathematical Platonism), and is the natural point of contact between Track C and the Platonist tradition.

### Primary text

- **Shapiro, Stewart. *Thinking About Mathematics: The Philosophy of Mathematics.* OUP, 2000. ~309 pp.**
  - The standard accessible overview.
  - **Pace:** 4 weeks at 6 hrs/week.

### Companion

- **Benacerraf, Paul, and Hilary Putnam, eds. *Philosophy of Mathematics: Selected Readings.* 2nd ed., CUP, 1983.**
  - The canonical anthology. Read selectively; at minimum: Benacerraf's "What Numbers Could Not Be" and "Mathematical Truth," Putnam's "What Is Mathematical Truth?", Hilbert's "On the Infinite," Brouwer's "Consciousness, Philosophy, and Mathematics," Gödel's "What Is Cantor's Continuum Problem?" (re-read after Phase 5).

### Optional

- Maddy, Penelope. *Naturalism in Mathematics.* Clarendon, 1997. — A modern naturalist/realist position.
- Linnebo, Øystein. *Philosophy of Mathematics.* Princeton, 2017. — More recent overview.

## Interleaving Track C with the Platonist continuation

If you don't want Track C in a single block:

- **Phase C1 (Modal logic)** is best done either right after Phase 5 (Gödel) — pushing the rest of the core back by 2 months — or right after Phase 8. The former gives you modal apparatus before reading Plato's *Sophist* and *Parmenides*, both of which have moves around possibility and necessity. The latter keeps the core 18 months intact, which is what the default ordering assumes.
- **Phase C2 (Non-classical)** has a strong thematic connection with Phase 4 (medieval logic). Cleanest is to do it right after C1.
- **Phase C3 (Specialisation)** is self-contained; do whenever appetite is highest, or skip if neither model theory nor proof theory pulls you.
- **Phase C4 (Philosophy of mathematics)** pairs naturally with Phase 13 (modern Platonist revivals). You can do them together as a single closing sub-curriculum on mathematical Platonism — a fitting end to the whole project.

**Most efficient default:** all of Track C in series after Phase 8, then the Platonist continuation. Total: ~24 months for core + Track C, then ~14 months of Platonist continuation = ~38 months if you do everything.

# Track B Continuation — The Platonist Tradition (~14 months)

## Phase 9 — Hellenistic Schools and Middle Platonism (Months 19–20)

**Goal:** Get the bridge from Plato to Plotinus right. The 600 years between them are usually skipped; without them, Plotinus looks unprovoked.

### Texts

- **Long, A. A., and D. N. Sedley. *The Hellenistic Philosophers.* 2 vols, CUP, 1987.**
  - The standard primary-text anthology with commentary. Read Vol. 1 selectively — especially the Stoic logic sections (which interact interestingly with Aristotle's syllogistic).
- **Dillon, John. *The Middle Platonists, 80 B.C. to A.D. 220.* 2nd ed., Cornell, 1996.**
  - The canonical work on the philosophers who handed Plato to Plotinus.

### Stanford Encyclopedia

- "Middle Platonism"
- "Plutarch"
- "Stoicism"
- "Ancient Logic" (read especially on Stoic logic — the rival ancient tradition to Aristotle's, which the Polish school later vindicated as proto-propositional logic)

## Phase 10 — Plotinus and Neoplatonism (Months 21–24)

**Goal:** Read the *Enneads* with care. Plotinus is the most important philosopher between Aristotle and Augustine, and the source of nearly everything downstream in Christian, Islamic, and Jewish philosophy.

### Primary text

- **Plotinus. *The Enneads.* Translated by A. H. Armstrong. 7 vols, Loeb Classical Library, Harvard, 1966–1988.**
  - Standard scholarly translation with facing Greek. Read selectively:
    - Ennead I.6 (On Beauty)
    - Ennead V.1 (On the Three Primary Hypostases)
    - Ennead VI.9 (On the Good or the One)
    - Ennead V.3, V.4, V.5 (the One and Intellect)
    - Ennead III.7 (On Eternity and Time)
    - Ennead IV.3–4 (On the Soul)
    - Ennead VI.7 (How the Multiplicity of the Forms Came into Being)
- Alternative for reading: Plotinus, *The Enneads*, ed. Gerson, trans. Boys-Stones et al., CUP, 2018 — single volume, modern translation, lacks Greek but more readable.

### Companions

- **Hadot, Pierre. *Plotinus, or the Simplicity of Vision.* Trans. Chase, University of Chicago, 1993. ~143 pp.**
  - Short, philosophical, beautiful. The single best entry into Plotinus.
- **Gerson, Lloyd. *Plotinus.* Routledge, 1994.**
  - More analytical.

### After Plotinus

- Porphyry. *Isagoge* (the introduction to Aristotle's *Categories* that became the medieval logic-textbook starting point). Translated by Jonathan Barnes, Clarendon, 2003.
- Proclus. *The Elements of Theology.* Trans. E. R. Dodds, OUP, 1933. The most rigorously argued Neoplatonist work — set out as a series of theorems with proofs, in Euclidean style. Will interest you formally.

## Phase 11 — Christian Platonism (Months 25–28)

**Goal:** Augustine through Boethius. The transmission of Platonism into Western Christianity, and the philosophical foundations of medieval thought.

### Texts

- **Augustine.** Read in this order:
  - *Confessions.* Translated by Henry Chadwick, OUP World's Classics, 1991. ~328 pp.
  - *On Christian Doctrine.* Trans. D. W. Robertson, Pearson, 1958.
  - *On the Trinity.* Selections from Books VIII–XV (the philosophical core), trans. Edmund Hill, New City Press, 1991.
  - *City of God,* Books VIII (on Platonism) and XI (on creation). Trans. Henry Bettenson, Penguin, 2003.
- **Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite.** *The Complete Works.* Trans. Colm Luibheid, Paulist Press, 1987. Read *The Divine Names* and *The Mystical Theology*.
- **Boethius.** 
  - *The Consolation of Philosophy.* Trans. Victor Watts, Penguin Classics, 1999.
  - *On the Trinity* and the other *opuscula sacra* in *Theological Tractates*, Loeb edition (Stewart, Rand, Tester), 1973.

### Companion

- **Stock, Brian. *Augustine the Reader.* Harvard, 1996.**
- **Marenbon, John. *Boethius.* OUP, 2003.** Boethius is critical — he was the channel through which most of Aristotle's logic (via his translations of the *Organon*) and Neoplatonist metaphysics reached the Latin West.

## Phase 13 — Modern Platonist Revivals (Months 31–32)

**Goal:** See how Platonism survived and was reinvented after Kant, especially in 20th-century analytic philosophy of mathematics (where it never really left) and in moral philosophy (where it returned).

### Texts

- **Gerson, Lloyd P. *From Plato to Platonism.* Cornell University Press, 2013.**
  - Gerson argues that Platonism is a coherent philosophical position with identifiable commitments, not just a historical label. Excellent integrative reading at this point.
- **Murdoch, Iris. *The Sovereignty of Good.* Routledge, 1970. ~106 pp.**
  - The most influential 20th-century moral Platonism.
- **Gödel, Kurt. "What Is Cantor's Continuum Problem?" (1947, revised 1964).** In *Collected Works,* Vol. II, OUP, 1990. Re-read in light of all the above.
- **Putnam, Hilary, and Paul Benacerraf, eds. *Philosophy of Mathematics: Selected Readings.* 2nd ed., CUP, 1983.** Read the Platonism/realism sections.
- **Williams, Rowan. *The Edge of Words: God and the Habits of Language.* Bloomsbury, 2014.** Contemporary theological Platonism, intellectually serious.

## Sequencing Rationale

- **Modern foundations (1) before Aristotle's *Organon* (2):** so Łukasiewicz's formal reconstruction is readable.
- **Aristotle's *Organon* (2) before modern metalogic (3):** Phase 2 is the long historical/textual phase; Phase 3 is shorter and technical. Putting them in this order means you don't lose Aristotelian-reading momentum to metalogic.
- **Modern metalogic (3) and medieval logic (4) before Gödel (5):** because medieval theories of insolubles are the historical ancestor of modern truth-theory paradoxes, and you can appreciate this when you reach Gödel.
- **Logic phases (1–5) before Plato (6):** the syllogistic and modern logic together let you read Plato's *Sophist* and *Parmenides* without being mystified by the formal structure of the argument moves.
- **Plato (6) before Aristotle's *Metaphysics* (7):** so Aristotle's critique of Plato lands properly.
- **Bridge phase (8) at the end of the core:** lets you read Quine, Burgess, Dummett, and Husserl with both ancient and modern logic fresh.
- **Track C (advanced modern logic) as parallel continuation:** modular. The modal logic in C1 has the strongest case for earlier insertion (right after Gödel, or before Plato's late dialogues), but is not essential for either; defer to taste. Philosophy of mathematics in C4 pairs naturally with Phase 13, and many readers will want to do those together regardless of when they do C1–C3.
- **Platonist tradition (9–13) as continuation:** modular; you can drop out at any phase and use the time for other directions.

## Honest Caveats

- This curriculum is dense even at 6 hrs/week. If your real availability is closer to 4 hrs/week, expect ~24 months for the core rather than 18.
- The Platonist continuation (Phases 9–13) overlaps with what most philosophy graduate programs would treat as a multi-year specialisation. Treat it as a sustained reading project, not a sprint.
- I've biased the secondary literature toward older but canonical commentaries (Cornford, Ross, Cherniss, Dillon, Bocheński) because they remain reference works the field still uses. Where contemporary scholarship has decisively replaced them (Striker on the *Prior Analytics*, Dutilh Novaes on medieval logic), I've used the new sources.
- You should expect at some point — probably around Phase 6 or 7 — to discover that some sub-area pulls you more than the curriculum allows. That's the point. The curriculum gives you the ground to make that judgment with knowledge, not before.
