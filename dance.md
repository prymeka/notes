# A Curriculum in Dance History

*Western-focused (including Latin America), but not exclusively so. A concert/theatrical-dance spine (court dance → ballet → modern → contemporary), braided with social, popular, and folk dance where they illuminate the story. Balanced method — guided watching, stylistic-analytic understanding, and cultural context — with a parallel primary-source track and a light embodied practice thread.*

---

## Preface

### What this subject is, and the problem at its heart

Dance history has a difficulty no other art history faces so acutely: **its primary object disappears the moment it is made.** Before film, a dance existed only in the moment of performance, in the bodies of those who did it, and in whatever indirect traces survived — a notation system, a treatise, a painting, a costume, a description by someone who watched. There is no dance equivalent of the score you can read or the recording you can replay for anything before roughly 1900. This shapes everything: dance history is unusually dependent on **reconstruction, inference, and the study of traces**, and unusually vulnerable to the biases of who could write, who was painted, and whose dancing was considered worth recording.

Hold this from day one. When a book tells you "the minuet was danced *thus*," it is reconstructing from notation and treatises, not replaying a video. The confidence of the claim should track the richness of the surviving evidence — and you should ask, always, *what did not survive, and whose dancing was it?*

### Three caveats to hold throughout

1. **The written record is thin, uneven, and socially skewed.** We know far more about the court dances of literate European elites (who commissioned treatises and paintings) than about the popular, rural, and enslaved peoples' dances happening in the same centuries — even though the latter were often the *source* of the former. The archive's silences are not neutral.

2. **Appropriation and erasure are structural in this history, not incidental.** A recurring, well-documented pattern — especially in the Western/Latin American story you've asked for — is that African-diasporic and Afro-Latin dance forms were the generative engine of "Western" social and popular dance (the tango, the danzón, jazz dance, and nearly all twentieth-century popular dance), while credit, respectability, and commercial reward flowed to European or white adapters. Naming this is history, not editorializing; you'll meet the scholarship that documents it.

3. **"Latin dance" is a marketing category, not an ethnographic fact.** What ballroom studios sell as "Latin" (a standardized salsa, a competition rumba, a cha-cha-chá) is a codified, often North-Americanized product distinct from the living Cuban, Brazilian, Argentine, Dominican, and other traditions it derives from. Keep the studio product and the vernacular source distinct, exactly as you keep the ballroom "Latin" rumba separate from Afro-Cuban *rumba*.

### The four tracks

Run them together within each phase:

1. **Narrative & Style** — read the history; understand *how technique and choreographic vocabulary evolved* (why the turnout, why pointe work, why Graham's contraction, why the breaking of the fourth wall).
2. **Guided Watching** (the substitute for a "listening" track) — watch the dances. For pre-film eras this means watching modern *reconstructions* (with the caveat that a reconstruction is an argument, not a recording); for everything since ~1900, the real thing. Dance history read without watching is choreography with the movement deleted.
3. **Cultural Context** — dance in its social, political, economic, gendered, and racial world: the court as a political instrument, the ballet as spectacle and as marriage-market, the ballroom as a site of respectability panic, the club as a subculture.
4. **Primary Sources** — the parallel documents track: dance treatises and manuals, **notation systems** (a fascinating thread in their own right), choreographers' and dancers' own writings, iconography (paintings, prints, photographs), and criticism.

### The embodied thread (light, as you specified)

A **light** practice component runs throughout: try the *basic* step or body-principle of a few key dances, because dance is the one art you cannot fully understand from the outside — feeling *why* a waltz's rise-and-fall or a tango's weight-shift or a Graham contraction works teaches what no reading can. This is not a technique course; it is a handful of "try this yourself" moments per phase, done for insight, not proficiency. Where a dance carries genuine physical risk for a beginner (pointe work, aerial, deep floor work), the instruction is *don't* — watch and read instead, and if you ever pursue it, do so with a qualified teacher.

### Bridges to your existing curricula

This ties directly to the work you've been building:
- It is the **sibling of your music history curriculum** — dance and music are inseparable, and the two histories run in parallel and constantly intersect (the Baroque suite *is* a sequence of stylized dances; ballet is music made visible; the tango is a dance *and* a musical genre). Cross-references to that curriculum are flagged throughout.
- It connects to your **prosody** work through a shared deep structure: **rhythm, metre, phrase, and the patterning of time**. A dance phrase and a poetic line are both organizations of stress and duration; the minuet and the iambic line are cousins.
- It connects to your **rhetoric** work: Baroque and Enlightenment dance theory (Noverre's *ballet d'action*) explicitly borrowed the classical rhetorical project of *moving the passions* and *telling through gesture* — dance as a persuasive, expressive language.

### Watching & source infrastructure

- **Watching:** YouTube covers an enormous amount (company channels, archival uploads, reconstructions); many major companies (Paris Opera, Bolshoi, NYCB, Royal Ballet) have official channels and cinema/streaming programs. **Marquee TV** is a paid dance/arts streaming service; some university libraries offer **Dance in Video** (Alexander Street). [Largely free via YouTube]
- **Sources:** Many historical treatises (Arbeau, Feuillet, Rameau-the-dancing-master, Blasis, Noverre) are public-domain and free online; the **New York Public Library Jerome Robbins Dance Division** is the great archive. [Many free]

- **Selma Jeanne Cohen et al. (eds.), *International Encyclopedia of Dance*, 6 vols. (Oxford UP, 1998).** The reference of record — look up any figure, form, or term. [Reference]

**Primary-source anthologies (your parallel track).**

**Method / dance studies.**

## Phase 0 — Orientation: how to study a vanishing art

**Goal.** Acquire the meta-tools before the narrative: why dance history is a history of *traces*; how to read the four kinds of evidence (notation, treatise, image, description); the appropriation/erasure and "Latin category" caveats; and how to "read" a piece of choreography critically.

**Prerequisites.** None. (Your music history curriculum is a strong asset but not required.)

**Narrative & Method.** Jonas, *Dancing*, Introduction and ch. 1; skim Foster's *Reading Dancing* introduction (how choreography carries meaning); read the editorial apparatus of Cohen's *Dance as a Theatre Art*.

**Cultural Context.** The politics of the archive — who gets recorded and who doesn't (the caveats above, made concrete).

**Guided Watching.** Watch the opening episode of the *Dancing* series; then watch three *very* different short clips (a classical ballet variation, a social-dance clip, a folk/ritual dance) simply to calibrate your eye to how different "dance" can be.

**Primary Sources.** Look at one image, one notation excerpt, and one written description of the *same* kind of dance, and note what each medium captures and loses — a direct exercise in the "traces" problem.

**Exercises.**
- *Method*: Write a page on why dance history depends more on reconstruction and inference than music or art history does.
- *Embodied (light)*: Stand up and try to *describe in words* a simple movement you just made (a step and a turn), then try to *draw* it. Feel firsthand why notating dance is hard.

**Minimum viable path.** Jonas Intro + ch. 1 + the *Dancing* opening episode + the "same dance, three media" source exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~2 weeks.

## Phase 1 — Ancient and pre-modern foundations

**Goal.** Dance in antiquity and the medieval world, and the crucial recognition that dance is one of humanity's oldest and most universal practices — ritual, communal, and expressive long before it was theatrical. A deliberately brief, humbling frame before the Western theatrical story begins.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas, the early/global chapters. Topics: dance in ancient Greece (the *choros*; dance in tragedy and religious festival — a tie to your classical work); Roman *pantomimus*; medieval Europe (the carole/round dance; the danse macabre; the Church's deep ambivalence toward dancing); and a glance at the antiquity of dance worldwide, to resist a purely European origin story.

**Guided Watching.** Reconstructions and iconography-based interpretations (necessarily speculative — flag this); the *Dancing* series' relevant segments.

**Cultural Context.** Dance as ritual and social glue; religious sanction and suspicion (a theme that recurs for centuries — the "respectability panic" begins here).

**Primary Sources.** Greek and Roman references to dance (Plato, Lucian's *On the Dance*, in translation — Lucian is a gem) [public domain]; medieval church condemnations and the iconography of the danse macabre.

**Exercises.**
- *Narrative*: Explain why the Church's ambivalence toward dance mattered for what survived in the record.
- *Cross-connection*: Relate the Greek *choros* to the role of the chorus in the tragedy you met in your classical/rhetoric reading.

**Minimum viable path.** Jonas's early chapters + Lucian's *On the Dance* (excerpt).

**Time estimate.** ~2 weeks.

## Phase 2 — Renaissance court dance and the birth of ballet (c. 1450–1650)

**Goal.** The stately social dances of the European courts, their codification in the first dance treatises, and the emergence of ballet from courtly spectacle — culminating in the French court and the *ballet de cour*. Ballet begins as a *social and political* practice, not a stage art.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 1.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas and Anderson on early ballet. Topics: the Italian *balli* and the first dancing masters (Domenico da Piacenza; Guglielmo Ebreo); the basse danse and pavane/galliard; Catherine de' Medici and the *Ballet Comique de la Reine* (1581, often called the first ballet); the *ballet de cour* at the French court.

**Guided Watching.** Reconstructions of Renaissance court dances (pavane, galliard) — several good ones exist; watch for the erect carriage, the intricate footwork, the social geometry.

**Cultural Context.** Dance as a *political instrument* — the court dance displayed hierarchy, control, and the ruler's power; dancing well was a courtier's obligation (tie to Castiglione's *sprezzatura*, which you may know from your rhetoric/menswear reading).

**Primary Sources.** **Thoinot Arbeau, *Orchésographie* (1589)** — the great, delightful primary source for Renaissance social dance, in dialogue form, with step-by-step instructions and even rhythmic notation. Free and readable. [Public domain] Also the early Italian treatises.

**Exercises.**
- *Embodied (light)*: Using Arbeau, try the basic step-pattern of a **pavane** (a slow processional) — genuinely doable from the treatise, and a direct experience of reading dance from a primary source.
- *Cross-connection (music)*: The pavane and galliard are also musical forms — connect to the dance movements in your music history curriculum's Renaissance/Baroque phases.

**Minimum viable path.** Anderson's early-ballet section + Arbeau (browse + the pavane) + one court-dance reconstruction.

**Time estimate.** ~3 weeks.

## Phase 3 — The Baroque: the professional stage and the *belle danse* (c. 1650–1750)

**Goal.** Ballet's move from the court to the professional stage under Louis XIV; the founding of the first ballet academy; the emergence of a codified technique and the first great notation system; and the deep entanglement of Baroque dance with Baroque music.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 2.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas/Anderson. Topics: **Louis XIV** (the "Sun King," himself a dancer — his role in *Le Ballet de la Nuit*) and the founding of the **Académie Royale de Danse (1661)**; Pierre Beauchamp (credited with codifying the five positions of the feet — the foundation of ballet technique to this day); the professionalization of dancers and the appearance of women on the professional stage; the *belle danse* / French noble style.

**Guided Watching.** The excellent modern reconstructions of Baroque dance (the work of ensembles like the New York Baroque Dance Company); watch for the ornate footwork, the *port de bras*, the relationship to Baroque music's phrasing.

**Cultural Context.** Absolutism and spectacle; dance as royal propaganda; the birth of the *professional*, and what that did to the *amateur* courtier-dancer.

**Primary Sources.** **Raoul-Auger Feuillet, *Chorégraphie* (1700)** — the first widely used dance *notation* system (Beauchamp–Feuillet notation), which let dances be published and circulated across Europe; a landmark in the "writing down dance" thread. **Pierre Rameau, *Le Maître à danser* (1725)** — the detailed manual of the noble style. [Both public domain]

**Exercises.**
- *Primary source/notation*: Look at a page of Beauchamp–Feuillet notation and decode a simple path — experience an early solution to the disappearing-object problem.
- *Cross-connection (music)*: Take a Baroque suite from your music history curriculum (allemande, courante, sarabande, gigue) and learn that **each movement is a stylized dance** with its own character and metre — the clearest possible dance/music link.

**Minimum viable path.** Anderson's Baroque section + Feuillet (look) + Pierre Rameau (browse) + the suite-as-dances connection.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 4 — The Enlightenment reform: the *ballet d'action* (c. 1750–1800)

**Goal.** The pivotal argument that ballet should *express and narrate*, not merely display steps and ornament — the birth of the dramatic, storytelling ballet. This is the phase with the richest tie to your rhetoric work.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 3.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas/Anderson. Topics: the reform of ballet toward drama; **Jean-Georges Noverre** and the *ballet d'action* (expressive, dramatic ballet); the loosening of restrictive costumes to allow real movement (Marie Sallé); the beginnings of ballet as a serious expressive art rather than court ornament.

**Guided Watching.** Reconstructions are scarce (few survive), so this phase leans on reading and iconography; watch what reconstructions exist and later ballets that inherited the *ballet d'action* ideal.

**Cultural Context.** Enlightenment aesthetics (expression, nature, feeling); the parallel reform happening in opera (Gluck — a direct tie to your music history curriculum's Classical phase).

**Primary Sources.** **Jean-Georges Noverre, *Lettres sur la danse et sur les ballets* (1760)** — a foundational document of Western dance, arguing for expressive, dramatic ballet. Read the excerpts in Cohen's anthology; it explicitly invokes the classical project of moving the passions. [Public domain]

**Exercises.**
- *Rhetoric tie (do this one)*: Read Noverre and connect his argument — that dance should *move the passions* and *tell*, through expressive gesture — to the classical rhetorical goals (*movere*; *actio*/delivery) from your rhetoric curriculum. Noverre is essentially applying rhetoric to the body.
- *Narrative*: State what the *ballet d'action* rejected and what it introduced.

**Minimum viable path.** Anderson's reform section + Noverre's *Letters* (excerpts) + the rhetoric cross-connection.

**Time estimate.** ~3 weeks.

## Phase 5 — Romantic ballet (c. 1830–1870)

**Goal.** The first great flowering of ballet as we picture it: the ethereal ballerina, pointe work, the white tulle, the supernatural themes — and the social forces (and gender politics) behind the "cult of the ballerina."

**Prerequisites.** Phase 4.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas/Anderson. Topics: the rise of **pointe work** and the mystique of the ballerina; **Marie Taglioni** and *La Sylphide* (1832); **Carlotta Grisi** and **Jules Perrot/Jean Coralli's *Giselle* (1841)** — the supreme surviving Romantic ballet; the *ballet blanc*; the shift of ballet's center toward Paris and then, crucially, toward Russia and Denmark (August Bournonville in Copenhagen, whose style survives intact).

**Guided Watching.** *Giselle* (a full production — the touchstone) and *La Sylphide* (ideally a Bournonville-tradition production); watch how pointe work is used to signify the ethereal and otherworldly.

**Cultural Context.** Romanticism (the supernatural, the unattainable ideal, the doomed love); the darker social reality — the *corps de ballet* dancers, often working-class women, and the exploitative "abonnés" culture of the Paris Opéra (captured, and complicated, in Degas's paintings); the near-disappearance of the male dancer from the Western stage.

**Primary Sources.** **Théophile Gautier's dance criticism** (Gautier co-created *Giselle* and wrote vividly about the Romantic ballerina — a superb primary source) [public domain]; Degas's ballet iconography, read as evidence of the era's social reality.

**Exercises.**
- *Cultural*: Use Gautier and Degas together to describe both the *idealization* and the *exploitation* of the Romantic ballerina.
- *Cross-connection (music)*: Connect Romantic ballet to the Romantic music of your music history curriculum's Phase 6.

**Minimum viable path.** Anderson's Romantic section + *Giselle* (full) + Gautier's criticism.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 6 — Classical ballet and the Russian apogee (c. 1870–1910)

**Goal.** The codification of the grand classical style and the creation of the enduring full-length masterpieces — the "classics" every company still dances — centered in Imperial Russia. *(Per your request, this is the classical-ballet apogee treated as a phase of the Western theatrical tradition; it is not a Russian-national-dance section.)*

**Prerequisites.** Phase 5.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas/Anderson. Topics: **Marius Petipa** and the grand classical ballet in St. Petersburg; the Petipa–**Tchaikovsky** collaborations — *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890), *Swan Lake* (definitive 1895 revival with Lev Ivanov), *The Nutcracker* (1892); the structure of the classical *pas de deux* and the grand full-evening ballet; the codification of the *danse d'école* (the academic vocabulary you can still see in any ballet class).

**Guided Watching.** *The Sleeping Beauty* and *Swan Lake* (full productions — the summit of the classical style); watch the architecture of a classical *pas de deux* (entrée, adagio, variations, coda).

**Cultural Context.** Imperial patronage and spectacle; ballet as a state-supported grand art; the paradox that the "Russian" classics were made largely by a Frenchman (Petipa) to the codified French-Italian academic technique — a *transnational* Western art, not a national one.

**Primary Sources.** **Carlo Blasis's technical treatises** (*The Code of Terpsichore*, 1828 — the codification of academic ballet technique that underlies the whole classical style) [public domain]; Petipa's production notes and memoirs; contemporary balletomane criticism.

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Diagram the structure of a classical *pas de deux* and relate it to musical form (a tie to your music theory/history work).
- *Cross-connection*: Connect the Tchaikovsky ballet scores to your music history curriculum.

**Minimum viable path.** Anderson's classical section + *Swan Lake* (full) + Blasis (browse) + the *pas de deux* structure exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 7 — The modernist revolution I: Ballets Russes and the shattering of convention (c. 1909–1929)

**Goal.** The explosion that dragged ballet into the twentieth century: Diaghilev's Ballets Russes, the collision of dance with modernist music and art, and the scandals that redefined what ballet could be.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 6.

**Narrative & Style.** Jonas; Reynolds & McCormick, *No Fixed Points*, opening. Topics: **Sergei Diaghilev** and the **Ballets Russes** (Paris, from 1909) as a *Gesamtkunstwerk* uniting choreography, cutting-edge music, and avant-garde design; **Michel Fokine's** reforms; **Vaslav Nijinsky** as dancer and as radical choreographer (*L'Après-midi d'un faune*, 1912; *The Rite of Spring*, 1913 — the riot, a direct tie to your music history curriculum's Phase 7); the collaborations with **Stravinsky**, Picasso, and others; **Bronislava Nijinska**; the return and reinvention of the male dancer.

**Guided Watching.** Reconstructions of *L'Après-midi d'un faune* and the Joffrey Ballet's celebrated reconstruction of the original *Rite of Spring*; watch how radically these broke with the classical line (the two-dimensional friezes, the turned-in feet, the stamping).

**Cultural Context.** Modernism across the arts; Paris as the avant-garde capital; the *Rite* riot as a modernist origin-myth (approach the tidy "riot" story with some skepticism — see Evidence note).

**Primary Sources.** Fokine's manifesto (his "five principles" letter to *The Times*); Nijinsky's diary; contemporary reviews of the *Rite* premiere (read critically against the myth). In Cohen's anthology.

**Exercises.**
- *Cross-connection (music)*: Pair this phase directly with your music history curriculum's modernist-rupture phase — the *Rite of Spring* is the same event in both histories.
- *Style*: Explain how Nijinsky's choreography rejected the classical vocabulary of Phase 6.

**Minimum viable path.** *No Fixed Points* opening + the Joffrey *Rite* reconstruction + Fokine's principles.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 8 — The modernist revolution II: the birth of modern dance (c. 1900–1950)

**Goal.** The parallel and even more radical break: the American and German pioneers who rejected ballet *entirely* and built new movement languages from the ground up. This is arguably the most important phase for understanding twentieth-century dance.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0's method; runs parallel to Phase 7 (they overlap in time).

**Narrative & Style.** Reynolds & McCormick, *No Fixed Points*; Jonas. Topics: the barefoot revolt — **Isadora Duncan** (natural movement, Greek-inspired, anti-ballet) and **Loïe Fuller** (light, fabric, spectacle); **Ruth St. Denis** and **Ted Shawn** (Denishawn, the training ground); **Martha Graham** (the codified modern technique — *contraction and release*; the psychological, mythic work — *Appalachian Spring*, 1944); **Doris Humphrey** (*fall and recovery*); **José Limón**; and, in Germany, **Ausdruckstanz** (expressive dance) — **Mary Wigman** and **Rudolf Laban** (who also created **Labanotation**, the major modern dance-notation system — the "writing dance" thread again).

**Guided Watching.** Duncan-style reconstructions; Fuller's *Serpentine Dance* (early film exists!); Graham's *Appalachian Spring* and *Lamentation*; Wigman's *Hexentanz*. Watch how each pioneer built a *different* relationship between the body and gravity.

**Cultural Context.** Modernism, feminism, and the female body's self-authorship (these pioneers were overwhelmingly women creating their own art on their own terms); the body as expressive of the *inner* and *psychological*; and the troubling entanglement of German *Ausdruckstanz* with the Nazi period (handled squarely, per the music-curriculum precedent).

**Primary Sources.** **Isadora Duncan, *The Art of the Dance*** and her memoir *My Life*; **Martha Graham, *Blood Memory* (memoir)** and her notebooks; Wigman's and Laban's writings; John Martin's pioneering dance criticism. In Cohen's anthology.

**Exercises.**
- *Embodied (light)*: Try a Graham **contraction and release** (seated, gentle — the initiation of movement from the pelvis/breath) and, separately, Humphrey's **fall and recovery** principle. Feeling these teaches the core insight of modern dance better than any reading.
- *Style*: Contrast Duncan's and Graham's answers to "what should dance be, if not ballet?"

**Minimum viable path.** *No Fixed Points* (modern-dance chapters) + the Fuller/Graham/Wigman watching + Duncan and Graham's writings + the contraction/release exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 9 — Social and popular dance I: the Atlantic and Latin American roots

**Goal.** Give social and popular dance its own sustained treatment, centered on the Afro-diasporic and Latin American forms that are the generative engine of modern Western social dance — and the honest account of their roots and their appropriation. This is the heart of the "Latin America included" part of your request.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0's method.

**Narrative & Style.** Malnig (ed.), *Ballroom, Boogie, Shimmy Sham, Shake*; Thompson, *Tango*; Daniel on Caribbean/Atlantic diaspora dance. Topics: the African roots and the Middle Passage; enslaved peoples' dance in the Americas (and its suppression and survival); the **Latin American forms** — Argentine **tango** (from the Buenos Aires margins, African and immigrant roots, to global respectability); Cuban **danzón, son, mambo, cha-cha-chá**, and Afro-Cuban **rumba** (distinct from the ballroom "rumba"); Brazilian **samba** (and its Afro-Brazilian roots; the *roda*); the Dominican **merengue** and **bachata**; later, **salsa** (a New York, largely Puerto Rican/Cuban, diaspora synthesis); the nineteenth-century European social dances that these met and transformed (the **waltz** — itself once scandalous for its closed embrace — the polka).

**Guided Watching.** Authentic Argentine tango (social, not competition); Afro-Cuban rumba (guaguancó); Brazilian samba (both *samba no pé* and samba de gafieira); a Vienna-style waltz; contrast each vernacular form with its later ballroom-standardized version.

**Cultural Context.** Race, class, and the "respectability panic" that greeted each Black-rooted dance in turn; the pattern by which "dangerous" vernacular dances were tamed, whitened, and commercialized into ballroom form; nation-building and dance (tango as Argentine identity; samba as Brazilian).

**Primary Sources.** Early dance-manual attempts to codify these forms; period newspaper reactions (the moral panics); Thompson's art-historical argument for the African aesthetic roots; oral histories.

**Exercises.**
- *Embodied (light)*: Try the basic weight-shift of a **box step** (waltz) and the basic step of a **salsa** or **merengue** (the simplest entry points) — enough to feel the difference between a rise-and-fall European frame and a grounded, hip-driven Afro-diasporic one.
- *Calibration (do this one)*: Take one form (e.g. tango or rumba) and distinguish the *vernacular source* from the *ballroom/competition product* — and trace who got credit and who got erased.
- *Cross-connection (music)*: The tango, samba, danzón, and salsa are musical genres too — braid this with your music history curriculum.

**Minimum viable path.** Malnig (selectively) + Thompson (selectively) + the tango/rumba/samba/waltz watching + the vernacular-vs-ballroom calibration + one basic step.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 10 — Social and popular dance II: jazz dance, the ballroom, and the twentieth-century floor

**Goal.** The North American story: the African-American vernacular jazz-dance tradition that underlies virtually all twentieth-century popular dance, the swing era, the codification (and appropriation) of ballroom, and Broadway/theatrical jazz.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 9.

**Narrative & Style.** Malnig; and **Jacqui Malone, *Steppin' on the Blues: The Visible Rhythms of African American Dance* (U. of Illinois Press)** — the key text on the African-American vernacular tradition. Topics: minstrelsy and its long shadow (the theft and caricature of Black dance); the vernacular jazz tradition (the cakewalk, the Charleston, the Lindy Hop and **the Savoy Ballroom**, tap dance — the Nicholas Brothers, Bill "Bojangles" Robinson); the swing era; the standardization of **ballroom** (the "English style," the competition circuit, the International/American distinction); Broadway and theatrical jazz dance (**Jack Cole**, the "father of theatrical jazz"; later **Bob Fosse**, **Jerome Robbins** — the bridge to Phase 11).

**Guided Watching.** Archival Lindy Hop (Whitey's Lindy Hoppers in *Hellzapoppin'*, 1941 — astonishing); the Nicholas Brothers (*Stormy Weather*); a Fosse number (*Sweet Charity* or *Chicago*); competition ballroom for contrast.

**Cultural Context.** The Savoy as a rare integrated space; the systematic pattern of Black origination and white commercial capture (again — now in dance's clearest twentieth-century form); social dance as youth culture and resistance.

**Primary Sources.** Malone's documentation; dancers' oral histories (the Lindy Hoppers; tap masters); early ballroom codification manuals (Imperial Society of Teachers of Dancing).

**Exercises.**
- *Embodied (light)*: Try the basic **Charleston** step and a basic **Lindy** rock-step/triple-step (the most accessible swing entry) — feel the syncopation and the grounded bounce.
- *Style*: Trace one lineage (e.g. cakewalk → Charleston → Lindy → later social dance) and note what carried through.

**Minimum viable path.** Malone (selectively) + the *Hellzapoppin'* Lindy + Nicholas Brothers + a Fosse number + one basic step.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 11 — Mid-century theatrical dance: Balanchine, the American ballet, and Broadway (c. 1930–1970)

**Goal.** The mid-century consolidation of a distinctively American theatrical dance: Balanchine's neoclassical revolution, the maturing of American ballet, and the golden age of the Broadway dance musical.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 7–8 (ballet and modern threads converge here); Phase 10 (Broadway jazz).

**Narrative & Style.** *No Fixed Points*. Topics: **George Balanchine** (from the Ballets Russes to America; the founding of New York City Ballet; **neoclassicism** — plotless, music-driven, speed and attack, "see the music, hear the dance"; *Apollo*, *Agon*, *Serenade*); **Antony Tudor** (the "psychological" ballet); **Jerome Robbins** (bridging ballet and Broadway — *Fancy Free*, *West Side Story*); Agnes de Mille (*Rodeo*; *Oklahoma!*'s dream ballet, which changed the musical); the integration of dance into musical-theatre storytelling; **Bob Fosse's** distinctive style.

**Guided Watching.** Balanchine's *Agon* and *Serenade* (the neoclassical language); *West Side Story* (Robbins); a de Mille dream ballet; watch how Balanchine's plotless, musical abstraction differs from both the story-ballet and modern dance.

**Cultural Context.** The Americanization of ballet; the Cold War cultural politics of ballet (the US and USSR both weaponizing it — approach as documented cultural history); the Broadway musical as a mass art.

**Primary Sources.** **Balanchine's own statements** (on music and dance; "ballet is woman" — a much-debated claim worth examining critically); Robbins's and de Mille's writings; Lincoln Kirstein's essays. In Cohen's anthology.

**Exercises.**
- *Cross-connection (music)*: Balanchine's collaborations with Stravinsky (*Agon*, *Apollo*) are a peak of the dance/music relationship — braid directly with your music curricula.
- *Style*: Explain "neoclassicism" — what it kept from classical ballet and what it stripped away.

**Minimum viable path.** *No Fixed Points* (Balanchine chapters) + *Agon* + *West Side Story* + Balanchine's statements.

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 12 — The postmodern break and after (c. 1960–1990)

**Goal.** The radical questioning of what dance even *is*: the Judson Dance Theater's rejection of virtuosity and spectacle, the rise of postmodern and experimental dance, and the major choreographic voices who redefined the art.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 8 (modern dance) and Phase 11.

**Narrative & Style.** *No Fixed Points*; Sally Banes, ***Terpsichore in Sneakers: Post-Modern Dance* (Wesleyan UP)** — the key text. Topics: **Merce Cunningham** (the pivotal figure — chance procedures; the separation of dance from music, made with **John Cage**; movement as pure event — a direct tie to your music history curriculum's Phase 10); the **Judson Dance Theater** (1960s downtown New York — pedestrian movement, "everyday" bodies, the *No Manifesto* — **Yvonne Rainer**, Steve Paxton, Trisha Brown, David Gordon); **contact improvisation** (Paxton); Pina **Bausch** and *Tanztheater* (the German dance-theater synthesis of movement, emotion, and theater); Alvin **Ailey** (*Revelations*, 1960 — the landmark rooting of concert dance in African-American experience); the beginnings of "crossover" between ballet and modern.

**Guided Watching.** Cunningham (any work — note the deliberate dance/music independence); Rainer's *Trio A* (the anti-virtuosic manifesto in movement); Bausch's *Café Müller* or *The Rite of Spring*; Ailey's *Revelations* (essential).

**Cultural Context.** 1960s avant-garde and anti-establishment values; the questioning of virtuosity, narrative, the proscenium, and "the masterpiece"; identity and concert dance (Ailey).

**Primary Sources.** **Yvonne Rainer's "No Manifesto"**; Cunningham's and Cage's writings on chance and independence; Banes's criticism. In Copeland & Cohen, *What Is Dance?*

**Exercises.**
- *Style*: Explain the Judson project — what it rejected (virtuosity, spectacle, the trained body) and why that was radical.
- *Cross-connection (music)*: The Cunningham–Cage collaboration embodies the same aesthetic as your music history curriculum's post-1945 phase — pair them.

**Minimum viable path.** Banes, *Terpsichore in Sneakers* (selectively) + Cunningham + Rainer's *Trio A* + Ailey's *Revelations* + the "No Manifesto."

**Time estimate.** ~4 weeks.

## Phase 13 — Contemporary dance and street/club forms (c. 1980–present)

**Goal.** The pluralist present: contemporary concert dance's global eclecticism, and the parallel rise of vernacular street and club dance (hip-hop and its relatives) into a global — and now concert and commercial — force.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 12 (concert thread); Phases 9–10 (social/vernacular thread).

**Narrative & Style.** *No Fixed Points* (close); Malnig and hip-hop-dance scholarship. Topics — *concert*: William Forsythe (deconstructing ballet); Mark Morris (musicality and wit); Jiří Kylián; Ohad Naharin and **Gaga**; Akram Khan; the globalization and eclecticism of contemporary dance; the blurring of ballet/modern/contemporary. Topics — *street/club*: the birth of **hip-hop dance** (1970s Bronx — **breaking/b-boying**, the same cultural moment as hip-hop music in your music curriculum) and the West Coast **funk styles** (**popping** and **locking**); **house** and club dance; **voguing** and the **ballroom** scene (Harlem's Black and Latino LGBTQ+ culture — *Paris Is Burning*); the movement of these forms into the commercial mainstream (music video, competition, concert stage) and the appropriation questions that follow them.

**Guided Watching.** Forsythe (*In the Middle, Somewhat Elevated*); a Naharin/Gaga work; foundational breaking, popping, and locking (archival and contemporary); voguing (*Paris Is Burning*); a contemporary crossover work. 

**Cultural Context.** Globalization and cultural hybridity; street dance as youth culture, community, and competition; the commercialization-and-appropriation cycle in its newest form; identity, race, and queerness in the ballroom/vogue scene.

**Primary Sources.** Choreographers' contemporary writings and interviews; hip-hop-dance oral histories and documentaries (*Paris Is Burning*; breaking documentaries); dance criticism of the present.

**Exercises.**
- *Embodied (light)*: Try the basic principles of **popping** (the muscle contraction) or a basic breaking **top-rock** — the most accessible entries; feel the difference between a codified concert vocabulary and a battle-born street form. (Avoid power moves/floor work without instruction.)
- *Synthesis*: Trace how a street form (breaking or voguing) moved from a specific community to a global commercial stage — who gained, who was erased.

**Minimum viable path.** *No Fixed Points* (close) + Forsythe + foundational breaking/popping/locking + *Paris Is Burning* + one basic street-dance principle.

**Time estimate.** ~5 weeks.

## Phase 14 — Capstone: cross-cutting themes and synthesis

**Goal.** Pull the braided strands together through themes that cut across the whole history, and complete a synthesis project.

**Prerequisites.** All prior phases (or the MVP spine).

**Cross-cutting themes to trace.**
- **The disappearing object and the technologies of preservation** — treatise → notation (Feuillet → Labanotation) → film → video → motion capture; each changed what could be preserved, transmitted, and studied.
- **The vernacular-to-theatrical (and Black-to-mainstream) pipeline** — again and again, the generative energy comes from popular, often Afro-diasporic, vernacular forms, and is refined, codified, whitened, and commercialized "upward." This is perhaps *the* master pattern of Western dance history.
- **The pendulum of the body's relationship to gravity, virtuosity, and meaning** — ballet's aspiration upward vs modern dance's grounding; virtuosity vs the pedestrian; narrative vs abstraction. The art keeps re-litigating these.
- **Dance and music as one braided history** — the through-line to your music curriculum, from the Baroque suite to the tango to Cunningham–Cage.

**Capstone project (pick one).**
- Trace a single thread across the whole span — one *form* (the *pas de deux*; the tango; the solo), one *body-principle* (turnout; groundedness; syncopation), or one *cross-cultural exchange* — integrating narrative, watching, context, and at least two primary sources.
- Or: an in-depth study of one choreographer, company, or vernacular tradition, with a source-grounded argument.

**Guided Watching.** A final integrative viewing: pick one work from the theatrical spine and one from the social/vernacular strand and watch each closely enough to "read" it in the terms of the whole curriculum.

**Time estimate.** ~2–3 weeks.

## Evidence quality — what is settled, contested, and reconstructed

Per your usual preference, an explicit calibration of the claims you will meet:

- **The disappearing object** *(the master epistemic problem).* For everything before film (~1900), we have *traces* — notation, treatises, images, descriptions — not the dance. Any pre-modern reconstruction is an *argument* built on incomplete evidence, and its confidence should track the evidence's richness. Treat "the Baroque minuet was danced thus" the way you treat a scansion under the foot convention in prosody: a defensible reconstruction, not a recording.
- **The archive's social skew** *(a real, structural bias).* The record over-represents literate European elites (who commissioned the treatises and paintings) and under-represents popular, rural, colonized, and enslaved peoples' dance — even when the latter was the *source*. What survives is not a neutral sample of what was danced.
- **The Black-origins / appropriation pattern in popular dance** *(well-documented, not editorial).* From minstrelsy through the Charleston, Lindy, tango, samba, and hip-hop, the recurring capture of Afro-diasporic forms by white/commercial adapters is a matter of record; naming it is history. (Malone; Thompson; Malnig.)
- **"Latin dance" as a category** *(a marketing construct).* The ballroom/competition "Latin" dances are codified, often North-Americanized products distinct from the living Cuban, Argentine, Brazilian, and Dominican traditions they derive from. Keep source and product separate.
- **First/founder claims** *(usually tidy legend).* "The first ballet" (the *Ballet Comique*, 1581), Beauchamp "inventing" the five positions, "the father of X" labels — these compress gradual, collective processes into single origin points. Useful shorthand; treat with the same skepticism as musical origin-myths.
- **The *Rite of Spring* riot** *(a modernist origin-myth, partly embellished).* The 1913 "riot" is real but has been dramatized and simplified in the retelling; the sources conflict on how much was outrage at the choreography vs the music vs pre-existing factions. A good case study in how a founding story hardens.
- **Reconstruction and "authenticity" in early-dance revival** *(genuinely contested).* Modern "historically informed" reconstructions of Renaissance and Baroque dance are scholarly interpretations, not neutral recoveries — and they vary. Likewise the "authentic" folk or social dance a revival presents is often shaped by the revivers. Both are debated.
- **Notation never captures everything** *(methodological humility).* Every notation system (Feuillet, Labanotation, Benesh) records some dimensions of movement and loses others (dynamics, quality, intention). A notated dance is a reduction, as a score is of a performance — more so.

## Beyond this curriculum

Natural continuations, none required:

- **Dance studies and theory in depth** — Susan Leigh Foster's body of work; André Lepecki, *Exhausting Dance*; the "choreographing history" and performance-studies turn; dance and gender/queer theory (Ramsay Burt, *The Male Dancer*).
- **Non-Western classical and ritual dance in depth** — the traditions this curriculum only glanced at, studied on their own terms: Indian classical dance (Bharatanatyam, Kathak), Japanese Noh and Kabuki, West African and Afro-Caribbean ritual dance, Indonesian dance — the natural complement, mirroring your music curriculum's Phase 11.
- **A single form or figure to specialist depth** — take any one phase (the tango; Balanchine; the Ballets Russes; hip-hop dance) to monograph-level study.
- **Dance notation and preservation** — learn to *read* Labanotation (Ann Hutchinson Guest's manuals); the history and technology of recording movement, up to motion capture — a thread that would appeal to your technical side.
- **The convergence point** — dance-and-music as a single subject (choreomusicology): the art song, opera, and the ballet score are where this curriculum, your **music history**, your **music theory**, and even your **prosody** (shared rhythm and phrase) all meet. A focused study of the ballet score, or of a single choreographer–composer partnership (Petipa–Tchaikovsky; Balanchine–Stravinsky; Cunningham–Cage), would braid them all.
