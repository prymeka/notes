# Biology & Metabolism: A Graduate-Level Self-Study Curriculum

**Target:** read primary literature fluently on metabolism, do problem sets, defend mechanisms.
**Budget:** ~7+ hrs/week over ~32 weeks (~240 hrs total).
**Strategy:** strategic coverage, not cut depth. Skim where your physics already covers the material; go deep on biochemistry (the spine); be targeted in physiology; capstone with rigorous nutrition science.

For comparison: a pre-med/biochem student covering this material takes roughly 4–5 semesters with lectures and exams. The schedule below has slack but is not generous. The single biggest failure mode of solo study at this depth is skipping problems — don't.

## Core textbook recommendations

Ranked by importance to your stated goals.

### 1. Lehninger — Principles of Biochemistry (Nelson & Cox, 8th ed)
The biochemistry spine. Best pedagogical balance of rigor and intuition specifically for metabolism. Strong end-of-chapter problems.
*Why over Berg/Stryer or Voet:* widely considered the best for metabolism; mechanism intuition is excellent without being shallow; matches your "applied/intuitive with solid theoretical grounding" preference.

### 2. Clayden, Greeves & Warren — Organic Chemistry (2nd ed, OUP)
Mechanism-first throughout, intuitive without being shallow.
*Why over OpenStax/Bruice/Wade:* it teaches you to push electrons and predict mechanisms, which is the right mental model for biochemistry. Aligns with how you think.

### 3. Boron & Boulpaep — Medical Physiology (3rd ed)
The most quantitative and cellular/molecular of the major physiology textbooks.
*Why over Guyton & Hall:* Boron is more rigorous and more physics-flavored. Better fit for your background. Guyton is clinically excellent but more memorization-heavy.

### 4. Stipanuk & Caudill — Biochemical, Physiological, and Molecular Aspects of Human Nutrition (4th ed)
The capstone where biochemistry + physiology + nutrition fuse.
*Why over Gropper et al.:* more rigorous, more biochemistry-heavy. Integrative metabolism (Randle cycle, fuel selection, fed/fasted, insulin resistance) is exactly its core territory.

### Optional supplementary
- **Berg, Tymoczko & Stryer — Biochemistry** (9th ed) as a second voice on hard Lehninger chapters. More concise.
- **Alberts et al. — Molecular Biology of the Cell** (7th ed), specifically the chapters on mitochondria, membrane transport, and cellular energetics, if you want deeper cell biology under your bioenergetics.

## Phase 1 — Chemistry Foundations (Weeks 1–10, ~75 hrs)

### Weeks 1–3 (~20 hrs): General chemistry essentials
**OpenStax Chemistry: Atoms First 2e**, selective. Skim aggressively where it overlaps with your physics.

Focus: atomic/molecular structure (light), bonding & molecular geometry, intermolecular forces, solutions, thermodynamics, chemical kinetics, equilibrium, acids & bases, electrochemistry/redox.
Skip: nuclear chemistry, descriptive inorganic, materials chemistry.

**Goal:** comfort with pKa, buffer chemistry, redox half-reactions, ΔG/ΔH/ΔS in chemical context, rate laws and rate constants. These are biochemistry's vocabulary.

### Weeks 4–10 (~55 hrs): Organic chemistry for biochemistry
**Clayden**, focused chapter selection. Do problems — Clayden's problems are excellent and the whole point of the book.

Approximate chapter sequence (Clayden 2nd ed; numbers may shift slightly):
- Structure & bonding, conformations (Ch 1–2, light)
- **Acidity, basicity, pKa, equilibrium control of reactions (Ch 8)** — critical for biochem
- **Stereochemistry (Ch 14, 16)** — critical; enzymes are chiral
- Nucleophilic substitution & elimination (Ch 15, 17, 19) — for enzyme mechanism intuition
- Carbonyl additions: aldehydes, ketones (Ch 6, 9–11)
- Carboxylic acid derivatives, esters, amides (Ch 10, 12) — peptide bonds, lipid esters
- **Enol/enolate chemistry, aldol & Claisen condensations (Ch 20, 25, 26)** — these literally *are* biochem reactions (aldolase, citrate synthase, fatty acid synthesis)
- Pericyclic & radical chemistry (Ch 34–37, light) — for advanced cofactor reactions
- Heterocycles & nucleotide-relevant chemistry (Ch 29–30)
- **Skip:** synthesis strategy chapters, advanced spectroscopy beyond NMR basics, organometallics

**Goal:** when you see a biochem reaction arrow, you can write a plausible mechanism. Push electrons. You're not learning retrosynthesis.

## Phase 2 — Biochemistry (Weeks 11–22, ~95 hrs)

**Lehninger** as primary, **Berg/Stryer** as second voice on hard chapters. Do at least 50% of end-of-chapter problems.

### Weeks 11–13 (~24 hrs): Foundations
Lehninger Parts I & II:
- Ch 2: Water, weak acids/bases
- Ch 3–5: Amino acids, peptides, protein structure & function (hemoglobin/myoglobin previews O₂ transport)
- **Ch 6: Enzymes — kinetics, mechanism, regulation. Deep pass; foundational.**
- Ch 7: Carbohydrates
- Ch 8: Nucleotides
- Ch 10: Lipids
- Ch 11–12: Biological membranes, transport, biosignaling

### Weeks 14–21 (~60 hrs): Metabolism — the core
**The heart of the curriculum. Take your time.** Lehninger Part III:

- **Ch 13: Bioenergetics & biochemical reaction types** — ΔG′°, coupled reactions, ATP, redox carriers
- **Ch 14: Glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway**
- **Ch 15: Metabolic regulation principles + glucose & glycogen metabolism** — glycogenesis and glycogenolysis live here
- **Ch 16: Citric acid cycle** — Krebs proper
- **Ch 17: Fatty acid catabolism** — β-oxidation
- Ch 18: Amino acid oxidation & urea cycle
- **Ch 19: Oxidative phosphorylation & the electron transport chain** — central to mitochondrial respiration, with O₂ as terminal electron acceptor. **Spend extra time here.**
- Ch 20: Photosynthesis — light pass; useful for ETC analogy and CO₂ fixation chemistry, but plants aren't your focus
- **Ch 21: Lipid biosynthesis** — DNL lives here. Read carefully.
- Ch 22: Amino acid & nucleotide biosynthesis
- **Ch 23: Hormonal regulation & integration of metabolism** — Randle cycle territory; this is where everything connects. **Spend extra time here too.**

### Week 22 (~10 hrs): Consolidation
Re-read Ch 23. Work integrative problems.
**First primary literature paper:** Randle PJ, Garland PB, Hales CN, Newsholme EA. *The glucose fatty-acid cycle.* Lancet 1963;281(7285):785–9. Then one modern review on the glucose–fatty acid cycle / fuel selection (I can send a list when you reach this point).

## Phase 3 — Physiology (Weeks 23–28, ~48 hrs)

**Boron & Boulpaep — Medical Physiology**, selective chapters.

Sequence:
- **Cellular physiology** (membranes, ion channels, transport, signaling — selected from Part II). Many concepts overlap with Lehninger; use Boron for the integrative cell view.
- **Respiratory physiology** (~5 chapters in the respiratory section). Gas exchange, O₂ and CO₂ transport in blood, Bohr and Haldane effects, ventilation, V/Q matching. **This is where your O₂/CO₂ question lives at the organism level.**
- **GI physiology**: digestion, absorption, the gut–liver axis
- **Endocrine physiology**: pancreatic islets (insulin, glucagon), adrenal cortex (cortisol), thyroid, growth hormone. Critical for metabolic regulation.
- **Energy metabolism / integrated metabolism chapter** (Boron has one)

Skip or skim: most of cardiovascular (light pass on coronary metabolism only), most of renal (skim acid–base regulation & glucose handling), reproductive, most neurophysiology beyond CNS–metabolic axes.

## Phase 4 — Integrative Metabolism & Nutrition (Weeks 29–32, ~32 hrs)

**Stipanuk & Caudill** as primary. This is where biochemistry + physiology + nutrition fuse into one picture. Read in this order:

- Macronutrient digestion, absorption, transport (cells → blood → tissues)
- Postabsorptive state, postprandial state, transitions between them
- Hormonal control of substrate utilization
- Carbohydrate metabolism integration
- **Lipid metabolism integration — DNL revisited at the organism level**
- Protein metabolism integration
- **Fuel selection and substrate competition (Randle cycle), insulin resistance mechanisms**
- Energy expenditure, basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis
- Micronutrients in metabolism — B vitamins as cofactors closes the loop with the cofactor chemistry from Lehninger

In parallel, **4–6 modern review papers** from *Cell Metabolism*, *Nature Reviews Endocrinology*, *Diabetes*, *Annual Review of Nutrition*. I'll suggest specific reviews once you reach Phase 4 (the field moves and the best current reviews depend on what's published when you get there).

## Workflow notes

- **Problem sets are non-negotiable.** You said you want to defend mechanisms. Do at least 50% of end-of-chapter problems in Lehninger metabolism chapters and Clayden carbonyl/enol chapters. Skipping problems is the single biggest failure mode of self-study at this depth.
- **Active recall after every chapter:** write the major reactions/pathways from memory before checking. For metabolism this is essential — you cannot "recognize" your way to grad-level rigor.
- **Mechanism notebook:** keep a notebook (paper or digital) of enzyme mechanisms with arrows pushed. By Phase 4 you should have 30–40 mechanisms internalized.
- **Pace check at end of Phase 2:** if you're more than 2 weeks behind, cut Phase 3 scope (drop the GI deep-dive, keep respiratory + endocrine) rather than rushing biochemistry. Biochemistry is the foundation everything else rests on.
- **Don't read Lehninger linearly cover-to-cover** during the metabolism section. Read each pathway, work the problems, then loop back to the integration chapter (Ch 23) repeatedly as your reference point.
