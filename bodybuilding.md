# The Science of Muscle, Strength & Adaptation: A Phased Self-Education Curriculum for the Quantitative Learner

## TL;DR
- This is a multi-year, primary-source-driven curriculum organized into six sequential Phases (A–F) plus two parallel cross-cutting tracks (Research Literacy and a Living-Literature track), moving molecules → cells → whole-body systems → training science → integration, anchored on ~8 rigorous textbooks and ~40 seminal papers/meta-analyses.
- The intellectual spine is four textbooks — MacIntosh/Gardiner/McComas (muscle biophysics), Sharples/Wackerhage/Morton (molecular exercise physiology), Schoenfeld (hypertrophy science), and Zatsiorsky/Kraemer/Fry (strength) — scaffolded by Powers & Howley for whole-body physiology, with a dedicated calibration layer separating settled consensus from live debates.
- Contested questions (muscle damage as a mechanism, metabolic stress, volume ceilings, periodization superiority, protein timing, interference magnitude, proximity-to-failure) are treated as first-class objects of study, and research-appraisal skills (effect sizes, CIs, small-sample pitfalls, the magnitude-based-inference scandal) run in parallel from Day 1.

## Key Findings (Orientation for the Learner)

**The field is empirically shallow relative to its confidence.** Exercise science is dominated by small samples, short durations (6–12 weeks), and surrogate endpoints. A systematic review of 232 sports-science papers by Lohse and colleagues (2020) found a median sample size of just 10 per group (IQR 8–15) for multi-group studies and 14 (IQR 10–24) for single-group studies, with only 15% reporting an a priori sample-size calculation. Effect sizes are small and heterogeneous. This means the *research-literacy track is not optional garnish — it is load-bearing*. You cannot correctly interpret the volume or periodization literature without first internalizing effect sizes, confidence intervals, and the reasons the field's own statistics have been challenged.

**Mechanism and application are only loosely coupled.** The molecular story (mTORC1, mechanotransduction) is elegant but does not cleanly predict training outcomes; acute signaling and MPS responses frequently fail to predict chronic hypertrophy. Treat the molecular phases as foundational understanding, not as a direct optimization target. This gap is itself one of the most important meta-lessons.

**A small number of researchers and labs generate a large fraction of the signal.** Brad Schoenfeld (Lehman/CUNY), Stuart Phillips (McMaster), Henning Wackerhage/Adam Sharples (molecular), the Kentucky group (McCarthy, Peterson, Murach — satellite cells), Zatsiorsky/Kraemer (strength theory), Digby Sale (neural), Andrew Vigotsky (biomechanics/EMG critique), Kristin Sainani (statistics), Louise Burke (nutrition). Knowing whose work you are reading — and their priors — is part of literacy.

## Curriculum Architecture & Dependency Map

**Six core phases (largely sequential):**
- **Phase A — Molecular & Cellular Foundations** (biochemistry → muscle ultrastructure → EC coupling → bioenergetics)
- **Phase B — Systems & Integrative Physiology** (neuromuscular, endocrine, cardiorespiratory, whole-body bioenergetics)
- **Phase C — Molecular Exercise Physiology of Adaptation** (mechanotransduction, mTORC1, MPS/MPB, satellite cells, ribosome biogenesis, mechanism debates)
- **Phase D — Hypertrophy Training Science** (volume, load, frequency, failure, ROM, tempo, rest)
- **Phase E — Strength, Power & Periodization** (neural adaptations, RFD, periodization models, specificity)
- **Phase F — Integration: Concurrent Training, Nutrition, Recovery** (interference effect, sports nutrition, sleep/recovery, tendon/injury)

**Two parallel tracks (run continuously from the start):**
- **Track R — Research Literacy** (start in Phase A, deepen through Phase D)
- **Track L — Living Literature** (ongoing: MASS, journal tables of contents, position stands)

**Dependency logic:**
```
A (molecules/cells) ──► B (systems) ──► C (molecular adaptation)
                                          │
                                          ▼
                        ┌──────────────► D (hypertrophy) ──┐
                        │                                   ├──► F (integration)
                        └──────────────► E (strength/power)─┘
Track R (research literacy) ═══ runs parallel A→D, prerequisite for critically reading D & E ═══
Track L (living literature) ═══ runs parallel throughout ═══
```
- A is a hard prerequisite for C. B is a hard prerequisite for the endocrine/cardio parts of C and F.
- D and E can run **in parallel** once C is complete (they share the neuromuscular and molecular base but diverge in outcome focus). Many learners will interleave them.
- F requires D and E (concurrent training presupposes you understand both hypertrophy and endurance adaptation; the interference effect is literally the collision of Phase C's mTOR story with Phase B/F's AMPK–PGC-1α story).
- Track R should be ~4–6 weeks *ahead* of the point where you first read primary RCTs critically (i.e., begin it during Phase A/B so it is mature by Phase D).

## PHASE A — Molecular & Cellular Foundations
**Time estimate:** 8–12 weeks (assuming ~6–8 h/week). Compressible for someone with Lehninger-level biochemistry already.

**Learning objectives:**
- Reconstruct skeletal muscle ultrastructure from first principles: sarcomere, thick/thin filaments, titin, the regulatory proteins (troponin/tropomyosin), and the structural proteins (dystrophin, costameres) that will matter for mechanotransduction later.
- Derive the sliding-filament and cross-bridge cycle mechanistically (Huxley), including the biochemical states of the actomyosin ATPase and the thermodynamics of force generation.
- Explain excitation–contraction coupling at molecular resolution: the action potential, T-tubule/DHPR–RyR mechanical coupling, Ca²⁺ transient, and relaxation via SERCA.
- Master the three bioenergetic systems (ATP-PCr, glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation) as coupled fluxes with defined rate/capacity trade-offs, connecting directly to your existing biochemistry.
- Understand the fiber-type continuum (MHC isoforms I/IIa/IIx) at the level of protein isoforms, not just "slow/fast."

**PRIMARY readings:**
- MacIntosh, Brian R.; Gardiner, Phillip F.; McComas, Alan J. *Skeletal Muscle: Form and Function.* 2nd ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2006. — **The spine of Phase A.** The most rigorous single-volume treatment of muscle biophysics accessible to a non-specialist. Combines anatomy, biophysics, and chemistry with clinical applications. (Note: 2nd ed. is 2006; it is dated on molecular signaling — Phase C's texts supersede it there — but remains authoritative on structure, EC coupling, and mechanics.)
- Nelson, David L.; Cox, Michael M. *Lehninger Principles of Biochemistry.* 8th ed. New York: W.H. Freeman/Macmillan, 2021. — You already read at this level; use the bioenergetics, glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and metabolic-integration chapters as your **reference substrate** rather than reading cover-to-cover.

**SUPPLEMENTARY readings:**
- Huxley, A.F. "Muscle structure and theories of contraction." *Progress in Biophysics and Biophysical Chemistry* 7 (1957): 255–318. — The seminal sliding-filament mechanics paper; read for the modeling approach as much as the content (a physicist will appreciate the rate-constant formalism).
- Widmaier, Eric P.; Raff, Hershel; Strang, Kevin T. *Vander's Human Physiology: The Mechanisms of Body Function.* 16th ed. New York: McGraw-Hill, 2022. — General physiology backstop (cell membranes, ion channels) if any prerequisite feels thin. *(Secondary/textbook — scaffolding only.)*

**Exercises / build-reproduce-analyze:**
- **Derive** the length–tension relationship from filament-overlap geometry and confirm it against the classic Gordon–Huxley–Julian curve. (Quantitative; suits your background.)
- **Model** the cross-bridge cycle as a simple state machine / kinetic scheme (2-state Huxley) and simulate force vs. velocity; compare to the Hill equation.
- **Diagram** the full EC-coupling chain from motor-neuron AP to Ca²⁺ release to force, annotating each step with time constants.

**Cross-domain connections:** The bioenergetics you formalize here (ATP-PCr, glycolytic flux, oxphos) is the *same* machinery whose signaling role reappears in Phase C (AMPK as an energy sensor) and Phase F (the interference effect and PGC-1α-driven mitochondrial biogenesis). Costameres and dystrophin (structure, here) become mechanotransduction candidates in Phase C.

## PHASE B — Systems & Integrative Physiology
**Time estimate:** 8–10 weeks.

**Learning objectives:**
- Motor units and the size principle (Henneman): orderly recruitment, rate coding, and how the nervous system grades force — the substrate for both hypertrophy (fiber recruitment) and strength (neural drive).
- The training-relevant endocrine system: testosterone, GH/IGF-1 axis, cortisol, insulin — synthesis, receptors, and *realistic* physiological roles (setting up the Phase C debunking of the "hormone hypothesis" of hypertrophy).
- Cardiorespiratory physiology and whole-body bioenergetics: VO₂max, lactate threshold, cardiac output, and the determinants of endurance performance.
- Integrated substrate metabolism during exercise across intensities.

**PRIMARY readings:**
- Powers, Scott K.; Howley, Edward T.; Quindry, John C. *Exercise Physiology: Theory and Application to Fitness and Performance.* 11th or 12th ed. New York: McGraw-Hill, 2020/2024. — **The spine of Phase B.** The most widely used, well-organized exercise-physiology text; strong on cardiorespiratory and bioenergetic integration. Use the most current edition available.
- Enoka, Roger M. *Neuromechanics of Human Movement.* 5th ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2015. — **The rigorous choice for motor units, recruitment, and rate coding.** More mechanically precise than the general exercise-phys texts on the neuromuscular material.

**SUPPLEMENTARY readings:**
- McArdle, William D.; Katch, Frank I.; Katch, Victor L. *Exercise Physiology: Nutrition, Energy, and Human Performance.* 8th ed. Philadelphia: Wolters Kluwer/Lippincott Williams & Wilkins, 2015. — Alternative/complement to Powers & Howley; **stronger on metabolism and energy**, heavier and more encyclopedic. Use as a reference, not a linear read.
- Kenney, W. Larry; Wilmore, Jack H.; Costill, David L. *Physiology of Sport and Exercise.* 8th ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2021. — The third major exercise-phys text; more sport-performance-oriented. Pick *one* of these three as primary (Powers) and use the others to triangulate confusing topics.
- Henneman, Elwood; Somjen, George; Carpenter, David O. "Functional significance of cell size in spinal motoneurons." *Journal of Neurophysiology* 28 (1965): 560–580. — The original size-principle paper.

**Exercises:**
- **Analyze** a published VO₂/work-rate dataset: identify ventilatory/lactate thresholds and reason about the underlying physiology.
- **Write a 1-page critical brief** on why serum testosterone/GH elevations post-exercise are a weak causal lever for hypertrophy — you will test this claim against primary sources in Phase C.

**Cross-domain connections:** The size principle here is the neural half of the "high-load vs low-load" hypertrophy debate in Phase D (do you need heavy loads to recruit high-threshold motor units, or does proximity to failure recruit them anyway?). The endocrine material sets up one of the most important *overhyped* claims you will dismantle in Phase C.

## PHASE C — Molecular Exercise Physiology of Adaptation
**Time estimate:** 10–14 weeks. The intellectual heart of the "why."

**Learning objectives:**
- Trace the hypertrophy signaling cascade: mechanical tension → mechanotransduction (candidate sensors) → mTORC1 → p70S6K / 4E-BP1 → translation.
- Distinguish muscle protein synthesis (MPS) from breakdown (MPB); understand net protein balance, the deuterium-oxide and stable-isotope methods, and why acute MPS ≠ chronic hypertrophy.
- Understand satellite cells, myonuclear accretion, the myonuclear domain (and its contested rigidity), and ribosome biogenesis (translational capacity vs efficiency).
- Critically evaluate the three proposed hypertrophy mechanisms — mechanical tension (primary), metabolic stress, muscle damage — and the current state of each debate.
- Understand the molecular basis of endurance adaptation (AMPK → PGC-1α → mitochondrial biogenesis) as the necessary setup for the interference effect in Phase F.

**PRIMARY readings (sequenced):**
1. Sharples, Adam P.; Wackerhage, Henning; Morton, James P. (eds.). *Molecular Exercise Physiology: An Introduction.* 2nd ed. Abingdon: Routledge, 2022. — **The spine of Phase C.** The only student-facing textbook dedicated to this material; covers signaling, genetics/epigenetics, ribosome biogenesis, and the molecular basis of both resistance and endurance adaptation. Has a companion website with self-test questions (free).
2. Wackerhage, Henning; Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Hamilton, D. Lee; Lehti, Maarit; Hulmi, Juha J. "Stimuli and sensors that initiate skeletal muscle hypertrophy following resistance exercise." *Journal of Applied Physiology* 126, no. 1 (2019): 30–43. doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.00685.2018. — **Best single review of the "what initiates hypertrophy" problem.** Defines the stimulus/sensor framework; states plainly that mTORC1-mediated MPS is "the key, but not sole" mechanism and that the initiating stimuli "remain elusive."
3. Goodman, Craig A. "Role of mTORC1 in mechanically induced increases in translation and skeletal muscle mass." *Journal of Applied Physiology* 127, no. 2 (2019): 581–590. — Deep dive on the mechanotransduction→mTORC1 link.
4. Roberts, Michael D.; McCarthy, John J.; Hornberger, Troy A.; et al. "Mechanisms of mechanical overload-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy: current understanding and future directions." *Physiological Reviews* 103, no. 4 (2023). doi:10.1152/physrev.00039.2022. — **The current authoritative synthesis.** Read last in this block; it integrates and updates everything above.

**PRIMARY readings — the mechanism debates:**
5. Damas, Felipe; Phillips, Stuart M.; Libardi, Cleiton A.; Vechin, Felipe C.; Lixandrão, Manoel E.; Jannig, Paulo R.; et al. "Resistance training-induced changes in integrated myofibrillar protein synthesis are related to hypertrophy only after attenuation of muscle damage." *The Journal of Physiology* 594, no. 18 (2016): 5209–5222. doi:10.1113/JP272472. — **The empirical linchpin of the muscle-damage debate.** Shows early post-exercise MPS is directed at repairing damage and is *uncorrelated* with hypertrophy; only after the repeated-bout effect attenuates damage does MPS correlate with growth. Conclusion: "muscle hypertrophy is the result of accumulated intermittent increases in MyoPS mainly after a progressive attenuation of muscle damage."
6. Schoenfeld, Brad J. "Does exercise-induced muscle damage play a role in skeletal muscle hypertrophy?" *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* 26, no. 5 (2012): 1441–1453. doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e31824f207e. — The paper that framed the question; concludes a causal link is "yet to be established."
7. Petrella, John K.; Kim, Jeong-su; Mayhew, David L.; Cross, James M.; Bamman, Marcas M. "Potent myofiber hypertrophy during resistance training in humans is associated with satellite cell-mediated myonuclear addition: a cluster analysis." *Journal of Applied Physiology* 104, no. 6 (2008): 1736–1742. doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.01215.2007. — Human associative evidence linking myonuclear addition to hypertrophy magnitude (extreme vs modest vs non-responders after 16 weeks).
8. Murach, Kevin A.; Englund, Davis A.; Dupont-Versteegden, Esther E.; McCarthy, John J.; Peterson, Charlotte A. "Myonuclear Domain Flexibility Challenges Rigid Assumptions on Satellite Cell Contribution to Skeletal Muscle Fiber Hypertrophy." *Frontiers in Physiology* 9 (2018): 635. doi:10.3389/fphys.2018.00635. — The nuanced counterpoint: satellite cells are required in growing muscle but mature muscle retains flexibility; "recent studies… challenge this axiom." (See also Murach et al., *Skeletal Muscle* 7, no. 1 (2017): 14, doi:10.1186/s13395-017-0132-z, for the mouse depletion evidence.)

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Schoenfeld, Brad J. *Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy.* 3rd ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2020/2021. — Its early chapters (mechanisms, endocrine) can be read here, but the book truly belongs to Phase D; its mechanism chapter is a good practitioner-level synthesis to read *before* the primary papers.
- Atherton, Philip J.; Babraj, John; Smith, Kenneth; Singh, Jaipaul; Rennie, Michael J.; Wackerhage, Henning. "Selective activation of AMPK-PGC-1α or PKB-TSC2-mTOR signaling can explain specific adaptive responses to endurance or resistance training-like electrical muscle stimulation." *FASEB Journal* 19, no. 7 (2005): 786–788. — The classic "AMPK–mTOR switch" paper; foundational for the interference effect.

**Exercises:**
- **Build a signaling diagram** (mechanical stimulus → sensors → mTORC1 → translational output) and annotate every edge with the strength of evidence (established / plausible / speculative). This *is* the calibration exercise.
- **Write a referee report** on the Damas 2016 paper as if for *J Physiol*: what would you require to accept "damage is not a mechanism"?
- **Reconcile** Petrella (2008) and Murach (2018): design the experiment that would adjudicate the myonuclear-domain question.

**Cross-domain connections:** The AMPK/mTOR antagonism you learn here is the *mechanistic core* of the interference effect (Phase F). The "acute MPS ≠ chronic hypertrophy" lesson is a direct application of Track R (surrogate endpoints). The hormone material from Phase B gets stress-tested: the primary literature shows post-exercise systemic hormone spikes do not drive hypertrophy — a flagship *overhyped* claim.

## PHASE D — Hypertrophy Training Science
**Time estimate:** 10–12 weeks. Runs parallel with / before Phase E.

**Learning objectives:**
- Quantify the dose–response for training volume; understand weekly-sets metrics and their limits.
- Evaluate load (high vs low) and the role of proximity to failure / effective reps.
- Assess frequency, exercise selection, range of motion, tempo, and rest intervals against the RCT/meta-analytic evidence.
- Distinguish what is settled, contested, and overhyped in each variable.

**PRIMARY readings (sequenced):**
1. Schoenfeld, Brad J. *Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy.* 3rd ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2020/2021. — **The spine of Phase D.** The single most comprehensive text on hypertrophy; 1,000+ references. Read cover to cover here.
2. Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Ogborn, Dan; Krieger, James W. "Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Journal of Sports Sciences* 35, no. 11 (2017): 1073–1082. doi:10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197. — The landmark volume meta-analysis (34 treatment groups from 15 studies, P = 0.002): each additional weekly set was associated with an effect-size increase of 0.023, corresponding to a **0.37% greater gain in muscle size per added weekly set** — a graded dose-response.
3. Pelland, Joshua C.; Robinson, Zac P.; Remmert, Jacob F.; et al. "The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains." *Sports Medicine* (2025). doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. — **The current state of the art on volume.** Extends Schoenfeld 2017 with non-linear modeling, estimating a **0.24% increase in hypertrophy (95% CrI: 0.15, 0.33) per additional set at the average fractional weekly volume of 12.25 sets** — i.e., real but diminishing returns, and a direct empirical handle on the "volume ceiling" question.
4. Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Grgic, Jozo; Ogborn, Dan; Krieger, James W. "Strength and Hypertrophy Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis." *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* 31, no. 12 (2017): 3508–3523. doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200. — Hypertrophy is similar across loads *when sets are taken near failure*; 1RM strength significantly favors high loads. The empirical basis for decoupling "load for strength" from "load for size."
5. Refalo, Martin C.; Helms, Eric R.; Trexler, Eric T.; Hamilton, D. Lee; Fyfe, Jackson J. "Influence of Resistance Training Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis." *Sports Medicine* 53, no. 3 (2023): 649–665. doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y. — Set failure vs non-failure ES = 0.19 (95% CI 0.00–0.37, p = 0.045); momentary failure vs non-failure ES = 0.12 (95% CI −0.13–0.37, ns). Evidence for a *non-linear* proximity-to-failure→hypertrophy relationship.
6. Robinson, Zac P.; Pelland, Joshua C.; Remmert, Jacob F.; Refalo, Martin C.; Jukic, Ivan; Steele, James; Zourdos, Michael C. "Exploring the Dose–Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy: A Series of Meta-Regressions." *Sports Medicine* 54, no. 9 (2024): 2209–2231. doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2. — Proximity to failure meaningfully influences hypertrophy (more growth as sets terminate closer to failure) but has a negligible relationship with strength. Authors caution the analysis is exploratory.

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Grgic, Jozo; Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Orazem, John; Sabol, Filip. "Effects of resistance training performed to repetition failure or non-failure on muscular strength and hypertrophy: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Journal of Sport and Health Science* 11, no. 2 (2022): 202–211. doi:10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007. — **Open access.** Failure not required (strength ES −0.09, hypertrophy ES 0.22, both ns overall); a small hypertrophy edge for failure in trained individuals (ES 0.15, 95% CI 0.03–0.26).
- Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Grgic, Jozo; Van Every, Derrick W.; Plotkin, Daniel L. "Loading Recommendations for Muscle Strength, Hypertrophy, and Local Endurance: A Re-Examination of the Repetition Continuum." *Sports* 9, no. 2 (2021): 32. — **Open access**; excellent synthesis of the load/rep continuum.

**Exercises:**
- **Reproduce a meta-regression:** extract the data from Schoenfeld 2017's forest plot and re-fit the weekly-sets model yourself; then compare your slope and CI to Pelland 2025's non-linear fit. (This is the flagship quantitative project — it fuses Track R with Phase D.)
- **Build a "settled/contested/overhyped" ledger** for each training variable, with the effect size and CI attached to each claim.

**Cross-domain connections:** Every claim here is a test case for Track R (small n, surrogate endpoints — muscle thickness by ultrasound vs biopsy). The high-load/low-load debate reaches back to the size principle (Phase B) and forward to specificity (Phase E).

## PHASE E — Strength, Power & Periodization
**Time estimate:** 8–10 weeks. Parallel with Phase D.

**Learning objectives:**
- Partition strength gains into neural vs morphological contributions across the training timeline.
- Understand rate of force development (early vs late RFD), motor-unit firing adaptations, and the specificity principle.
- Evaluate periodization theory (linear, undulating, block) against the meta-analytic evidence — and confront how weak the "one model is superior" claims actually are.
- Understand the strength–hypertrophy relationship and the science underlying powerlifting and weightlifting.

**PRIMARY readings (sequenced):**
1. Zatsiorsky, Vladimir M.; Kraemer, William J.; Fry, Andrew C. *Science and Practice of Strength Training.* 3rd ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2021. — **The spine of Phase E.** The classic theoretical treatment (specificity, the maximum-effort/dynamic-effort/repeated-effort framework, timing of training effects).
2. Haff, G. Gregory; Triplett, N. Travis (eds.), for the NSCA. *Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning.* 4th ed. Champaign, IL: Human Kinetics, 2016. — **The comprehensive reference** for program design, testing, and the physiological bases; read selectively (bioenergetics/adaptation chapters overlap Phase B; use the program-design and periodization chapters here).
3. Sale, Digby G. "Neural adaptation to resistance training." *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 20, no. 5 Suppl (1988): S135–S145. — The seminal neural-adaptations paper.
4. Duchateau, Jacques; Semmler, John G.; Enoka, Roger M. "Training adaptations in the behavior of human motor units." *Journal of Applied Physiology* 101, no. 6 (2006): 1766–1775. — Motor-unit-level adaptations; pairs with Enoka (Phase B).
5. Folland, Jonathan P.; Williams, Anthony G. "The adaptations to strength training: morphological and neurological contributions to increased strength." *Sports Medicine* 37, no. 2 (2007): 145–168. — **Best review of the neural-vs-morphological partition.**

**PRIMARY readings — periodization (read as a debate):**
6. Williams, Tyler D.; Tolusso, Danilo V.; Fedewa, Michael V.; Esco, Michael R. "Comparison of Periodized and Non-Periodized Resistance Training on Maximal Strength: A Meta-Analysis." *Sports Medicine* 47, no. 10 (2017): 2083–2100. doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y. — Periodized > non-periodized for strength (ES ~0.43); undulating showed an edge, but note the design confounds the authors themselves flag.
7. Grgic, Jozo; Mikulic, Pavle; Podnar, Hrvoje; Pedisic, Zeljko. "Effects of linear and daily undulating periodized resistance training programs on measures of muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *PeerJ* 5 (2017): e3695. — **Open access**; finds no clear superiority of undulating over linear for hypertrophy.

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Suchomel, Timothy J.; Nimphius, Sophia; Bellon, Christopher R.; Stone, Michael H. "The Importance of Muscular Strength: Training Considerations." *Sports Medicine* 48, no. 4 (2018): 765–785. — Strength as a foundational quality.
- Cormie, Prue; McGuigan, Michael R.; Newton, Robert U. "Developing maximal neuromuscular power (Parts 1 & 2)." *Sports Medicine* 41, no. 1 & 2 (2011). — The power-development reviews.

**Exercises:**
- **Time-course model:** plot the hypothesized neural vs hypertrophic contributions to strength over 24 weeks and defend the crossover point from the primary literature.
- **Adversarial reading:** take Williams 2017's pro-periodization conclusion and write the strongest evidence-based rebuttal (design confounds, non-equated volume, training status).

**Cross-domain connections:** Neural adaptations connect back to the size principle and rate coding (Phase B). Specificity (Zatsiorsky) frames the interference effect (Phase F). The periodization debate is a Track R showcase: "superiority" claims resting on small, short, volume-unequated studies.

## PHASE F — Integration: Concurrent Training, Nutrition, Recovery
**Time estimate:** 12–16 weeks (three sub-modules). Requires D and E.

### F1 — Concurrent Training & the Interference Effect
**Objectives:** Understand endurance adaptation molecularly (AMPK→PGC-1α→mitochondrial biogenesis), the molecular interference hypothesis (AMPK inhibits mTORC1 via TSC2/raptor), and what the *whole-body* evidence actually shows about interference magnitude.

**PRIMARY:**
- Coffey, Vernon G.; Hawley, John A. "Concurrent exercise training: do opposites distract?" *Journal of Physiology* 595, no. 9 (2017): 2883–2896. doi:10.1113/JP272270. — **The definitive molecular review.**
- Fyfe, Jackson J.; Bishop, David J.; Stepto, Nigel K. "Interference between Concurrent Resistance and Endurance Exercise: Molecular Bases and the Role of Individual Training Variables." *Sports Medicine* 44, no. 6 (2014): 743–762. — Molecular bases + programming variables.
- Wilson, Jacob M.; Marin, Pedro J.; Rhea, Matthew R.; Wilson, Stephanie M.C.; Loenneke, Jeremy P.; Anderson, Jody C. "Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises." *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* 26, no. 8 (2012): 2293–2307. — The landmark meta-analysis (21 studies, 422 effect sizes). Interference is real but **modality-dependent and outcome-specific**: concurrent training with *running* — but not cycling — produced significant decrements in both strength and hypertrophy, and the effect was largest for power (mean power ES fell from 0.91 in strength-only to 0.55 in concurrent groups), while hypertrophy was comparatively spared overall.

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Baar, Keith. "Using molecular biology to maximize concurrent training." *Sports Medicine* 44, Suppl 2 (2014): S117–S125. — Programming from a molecular-signaling standpoint.

### F2 — Sports Nutrition & Supplementation
**Objectives:** Energy balance; protein requirements and MPS (leucine threshold, per-meal dose, distribution, the "anabolic window"); carbohydrate/fat for performance; hydration; evidence-graded supplements.

**PRIMARY:**
- Burke, Louise; Deakin, Vicki; Minehan, Michelle (eds.). *Clinical Sports Nutrition.* 6th ed. Sydney: McGraw-Hill Education, 2021. — **The spine of F2.** The most rigorous sports-nutrition reference.
- Morton, Robert W.; Murphy, Kevin T.; McKellar, Sean R.; Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Henselmans, Menno; Helms, Eric; Aragon, Alan A.; Devries, Michaela C.; Banfield, Laura; Krieger, James W.; Phillips, Stuart M. "A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults." *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 52, no. 6 (2018): 376–384. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608. — **The key protein meta-analysis** (49 studies, 1,863 participants). Breakpoint analysis found that **beyond ~1.6 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.0–2.2) of total daily protein, supplementation yielded no further hypertrophy** — the field's anchor value for protein requirements.
- Jäger, Ralf; Kerksick, Chad M.; Campbell, Bill I.; et al. "International Society of Sports Nutrition Position Stand: protein and exercise." *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* 14 (2017): 20. — **Open access.** Per-meal 0.4–0.55 g/kg across 3–5 meals; the "anabolic window" substantially relaxed (the heightened MPS response lasts ~12–24 h post-exercise).
- Kreider, Richard B.; Kalman, Douglas S.; Antonio, Jose; Ziegenfuss, Tim N.; Wildman, Robert; Collins, Rick; Candow, Darren G.; Kleiner, Susan M.; Almada, Anthony L.; Lopez, Hector L. "International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine." *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* 14 (2017): 18. — **Open access.** Creatine monohydrate = "the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available" for high-intensity capacity and lean mass; no superior alternative form (HCl, ethyl ester, kre-alkalyn).

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Maughan, Ronald J.; Burke, Louise M.; Dvorak, Jiri; et al. "IOC consensus statement: dietary supplements and the high-performance athlete." *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 52, no. 7 (2018): 439–455. — **Open access.** The evidence-graded supplement review; creatine, caffeine, beta-alanine, nitrate, and bicarbonate carry the strongest support.
- Wilkinson, Kiera; Koscien, Christopher P.; Monteyne, Alistair J.; Wall, Benjamin T.; Stephens, Francis B. "Association of postprandial postexercise muscle protein synthesis rates with dietary leucine: A systematic review." *Physiological Reports* 11, no. 15 (2023): e15775. doi:10.14814/phy2.15775. — **Open access.** Critically examines (and partly complicates) the leucine-threshold hypothesis: leucine dose predicted MPS in older but not younger adults.

### F3 — Recovery, Sleep, Injury & Connective Tissue
**Objectives:** Recovery physiology; sleep's role in adaptation and hormones; overtraining/overreaching; DOMS; tendon/connective-tissue mechanobiology and load management; evidence-based injury prevention.

**PRIMARY:**
- Lamon, Séverine; Morabito, Aikaterina; Arentson-Lantz, Emily; et al. "The effect of acute sleep deprivation on skeletal muscle protein synthesis and the hormonal environment." *Physiological Reports* 9, no. 1 (2021): e14660. doi:10.14814/phy2.14660. — **Open access.** In a 13-participant randomized crossover, one night of total sleep deprivation reduced muscle protein synthesis by 18% (0.072 → 0.059 %·h⁻¹, p = .040), increased plasma cortisol by 21% (p = .030), and decreased plasma testosterone by 24% (p = .029).
- Kreher, Jeffrey B.; Schwartz, Jennifer B. "Overtraining syndrome: a practical guide." *Sports Health* 4, no. 2 (2012): 128–138. — Overreaching vs overtraining framework.
- Bohm, Sebastian; Mersmann, Falk; Arampatzis, Adamantios. "Human tendon adaptation in response to mechanical loading: a systematic review and meta-analysis of exercise intervention studies on healthy adults." *Sports Medicine – Open* 1 (2015): 7. — **Open access.** The tendon load-adaptation reference.

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Schoenfeld, Brad J.; Contreras, Bret. "Is Postexercise Muscle Soreness a Valid Indicator of Muscular Adaptations?" *Strength and Conditioning Journal* 35, no. 5 (2013): 16–21. — DOMS ≠ hypertrophy.
- Roberts, Llion A.; Raastad, Truls; Markworth, James F.; et al. "Post-exercise cold water immersion attenuates acute anabolic signalling and long-term adaptations in muscle to strength training." *Journal of Physiology* 593, no. 18 (2015): 4285–4301. — The cold-water-immersion caveat (may blunt hypertrophy).

**Exercises (Phase F):**
- **Design a concurrent-training microcycle** that minimizes molecular interference (session separation, modality choice) and justify each decision from Coffey & Hawley 2017 and Baar 2014.
- **Quantitative nutrition model:** compute a day's protein distribution to maximize threshold "crossings" and critique the model's assumptions using Wilkinson 2023.
- **Supplement evidence grid:** build an ergogenic-aid table graded by evidence quality (IOC 2018), explicitly separating creatine/caffeine (strong) from the overhyped (BCAAs, most "test boosters," ketones).

**Cross-domain connections:** F1 is the literal integration of Phase C (mTOR) and Phase B/F (AMPK–PGC-1α). F2's leucine material is downstream of Phase C's MPS mechanics. F3's sleep/hormone findings re-engage Phase B's endocrine module — and again warn against the surrogate-endpoint trap (a single-night MPS study is not a chronic-hypertrophy result).

## TRACK R — Research Literacy (parallel, continuous)
**Time estimate:** ~3–4 h/week, beginning in Phase A; a concentrated 3-week "boot camp" is recommended before Phase D.

**Learning objectives:**
- Read and appraise RCTs and meta-analyses; understand study design, randomization, blinding, and volume-equating.
- Master effect sizes (Cohen's d, standardized mean difference), confidence intervals, and their interpretation over p-values.
- Understand the field's specific pathologies: small samples, short durations, surrogate endpoints (acute MPS, EMG amplitude, muscle thickness), individual response vs measurement error, and publication/analytical bias.
- Understand *why the field's own statistics were challenged* — the magnitude-based inference (MBI) episode — as a case study in methodological self-correction.

**PRIMARY readings:**
- Sainani, Kristin L. "The Problem with 'Magnitude-based Inference.'" *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 50, no. 10 (2018): 2166–2176. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000001645. — **The paper that ended MBI's credibility.**
- Sainani, Kristin L.; Lohse, Keith R.; Jones, Peter R.; Vickers, Andrew. "Magnitude-based Inference is not Bayesian and is not a valid method of inference." *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* 29, no. 9 (2019): 1428–1436. — The rebuttal-of-rebuttals.
- Lohse, Keith R.; Sainani, Kristin L.; Taylor, J. Andrew; Butson, Michael L.; Knight, Emma J.; Vickers, Andrew J. "Systematic review of the use of 'magnitude-based inference' in sports science and medicine." *PLOS ONE* 15, no. 6 (2020): e0235318. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0235318. — **Open access.** Documents the scale of the problem: across 232 papers, median sample size was 10 per group (IQR 8–15), only 15% did a priori power calculations, and typical MBI settings inflate Type I error rates well above nominal — a quantitative anatomy of the field's core weaknesses.
- Vigotsky, Andrew D.; Halperin, Israel; Lehman, Gregory J.; Trajano, Gabriel S.; Vieira, Thiago M. "Interpreting Signal Amplitudes in Surface Electromyography Studies in Sport and Rehabilitation Sciences." *Frontiers in Physiology* 8 (2018): 985. — **Open access.** Why EMG amplitude is a treacherous surrogate — directly relevant to the high/low-load debate.

**SUPPLEMENTARY:**
- Button, Katherine S.; Ioannidis, John P.A.; Mokrysz, Claire; et al. "Power failure: why small sample size undermines the reliability of neuroscience." *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 14 (2013): 365–376. — General but foundational on power and the winner's curse.
- Caldwell, Aaron R.; Vigotsky, Andrew D.; et al. "Moving Sport and Exercise Science Toward Open Science." *Journal of Sports Sciences* / reproducibility pieces. — For the meta-issues of the field.

**Exercises:**
- **Re-analyze** any one dataset from a Phase D primary paper computing effect sizes and CIs by hand.
- **Audit** three papers you've read for the pathologies above; write a one-paragraph verdict on each ("what would change my mind").

**Cross-domain connection:** This track is the epistemic prerequisite for *every* training-science claim in D, E, and F. Do not read the meta-analyses as oracle; read them as small, noisy estimates.

## MASTER CALIBRATION SECTION — Settled / Contested / Overhyped

**SETTLED (high confidence):**
- Progressive resistance training causes hypertrophy; mechanical tension is the primary driver; mTORC1-mediated MPS is central (necessary, not sufficient).
- Total weekly volume is a primary dose variable with a graded but diminishing dose-response (Schoenfeld 2017: 0.37%/set; Pelland 2025: ~0.24%/set at ~12 sets).
- Hypertrophy is achievable across a wide load range *if sets approach failure*; maximal strength favors heavier loads (specificity) (Schoenfeld 2017 low/high-load).
- Total daily protein (~1.6 g/kg/day, plateauing) is the dominant nutritional lever; creatine monohydrate is the best-evidenced ergogenic supplement (Morton 2018; Kreider 2017).
- Neural adaptations dominate early strength gains; hypertrophy contributes more later (Sale 1988; Folland & Williams 2007).
- Sleep loss acutely impairs MPS and shifts hormones catabolically (Lamon 2021).

**CONTESTED (genuinely open):**
- **Muscle damage as a mechanism** — likely *not* a driver; probably reparative (Damas 2016), but not fully closed.
- **Metabolic stress** — plausible contributor (BFR data) but mechanism and independence from tension unclear (Wackerhage 2019).
- **Optimal volume ceiling / whether "more is always better"** — Schoenfeld 2017 (linear) vs newer non-linear/diminishing-returns modeling (Pelland 2025) and dissent (Buckner et al.).
- **Proximity to failure** — hypertrophy improves nearer failure (non-linearly); failure not required and may not help strength (Refalo 2023; Robinson 2024; Grgic 2022).
- **Periodization superiority** — periodized > non-periodized for strength is reasonably supported; *which* model (linear vs undulating vs block) is superior is not (Williams 2017; Grgic 2017).
- **Myonuclear domain rigidity / satellite-cell necessity** — required in growing muscle; flexible in mature muscle (Petrella 2008 vs Murach 2018).
- **Interference effect magnitude** — real but modality- and dose-dependent; hypertrophy largely spared, power most affected (Wilson 2012; Coffey & Hawley 2017).

**OVERHYPED / OVERSOLD:**
- The **post-exercise "anabolic window"** (protein timing) as narrow/critical — largely relaxed; the elevated-MPS window is ~12–24 h (Jäger 2017 ISSN).
- **Transient post-exercise hormone spikes** (testosterone/GH) as hypertrophy drivers — weak/negligible causal role.
- **DOMS as a proxy for growth** — invalid (Schoenfeld & Contreras 2013).
- **Exotic creatine forms, BCAAs (with adequate protein), most "testosterone boosters," and ketone esters** for hypertrophy — weak or absent evidence (IOC 2018).
- **Magnitude-based inference-driven conclusions** in older sport-science papers — statistically unsound; discount accordingly (Sainani 2018; Lohse 2020).

## MVP / TIME-COMPRESSED PATH (~4–6 months at 6 h/week)
For when pace must be adjusted, read the *spines* plus a curated primary-paper set, and treat calibration + Track R as non-negotiable:
1. **Foundations (compressed):** MacIntosh et al. — chapters on ultrastructure, cross-bridge, EC coupling only; Powers & Howley — motor units, bioenergetics, endocrine, cardiorespiratory chapters only. (~6 weeks)
2. **Mechanism core:** Wackerhage 2019 + Roberts 2023 (*Physiol Rev*) + Damas 2016. Skim the Sharples/Wackerhage/Morton text. (~3 weeks)
3. **Training science:** Schoenfeld *Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy* (whole book) + Schoenfeld 2017 (volume) + Schoenfeld 2017 (loads) + Refalo 2023. (~5 weeks)
4. **Strength:** Zatsiorsky/Kraemer/Fry (skim) + Folland & Williams 2007. (~2 weeks)
5. **Integration:** Coffey & Hawley 2017 + Morton 2018 + Kreider 2017 + Lamon 2021. (~2 weeks)
6. **Research literacy (do in parallel from week 1):** Sainani 2018 + Lohse 2020 + Vigotsky 2018.
7. **Living literature:** Subscribe to MASS; read Stronger by Science's master list.

The MVP deliberately front-loads reviews and meta-analyses over textbooks and preserves the calibration section in full — because knowing *what is uncertain* is the highest-value, most transferable output.

## Recommendations (How to Actually Run This)
- **Sequence:** Do A→B→C strictly in order (they are cumulative). Then run D and E in parallel (interleave weekly). Do F last. Keep Track R running from week 1 and complete its "boot camp" before starting D.
- **Cadence:** Budget ~18–30 months at 6–8 h/week for the full program; ~4–6 months for the MVP. Re-read the Master Calibration section at the end of each phase and update your own "settled/contested/overhyped" ledger — this is the single most valuable recurring exercise.
- **Do the quantitative projects.** For your background, the highest-yield activities are: (1) reproducing the volume meta-regression from raw forest-plot data and comparing your slope/CI against both Schoenfeld 2017 (0.37%/set) and Pelland 2025 (0.24%/set at ~12 sets); (2) simulating the cross-bridge cycle; (3) auditing papers for the field's methodological pathologies. These convert passive reading into durable, transferable skill.
- **Benchmarks that should change your plan:** If you cannot correctly interpret a confidence interval or effect size by the end of Phase B, *stop and finish Track R before proceeding* — the rest of the curriculum will mislead you otherwise. If a newer meta-analysis (post-2025) overturns a "settled" item, promote it to "contested" and trace the primary studies.
- **Source hygiene:** Prefer the open-access primaries (flagged above) and use MASS/Stronger by Science as *navigation aids* to the primary literature, never as endpoints.

## Caveats
- **Editions drift.** Confirm the latest edition at time of purchase: Schoenfeld's *Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy* now has a 3rd edition (1st 2016; 2nd 2020/2021; 3rd most recent); Powers & Howley is at the 11th/12th edition; Kenney/Wilmore/Costill 8th; McArdle 8th; NSCA *Essentials* 4th (a 5th may appear). MacIntosh et al. 2nd ed. (2006) is the most recent and is dated on molecular signaling — rely on the Phase C texts there.
- **Some seminal papers sit behind paywalls;** I have flagged open-access items explicitly. Author-deposited PDFs and preprints (SportRxiv) often exist for paywalled work.
- **The literature moves.** Volume dose-response and proximity-to-failure are actively evolving (2024–2025 meta-analyses cited); treat any single meta-analysis as a noisy estimate, not a verdict. The Robinson 2024 proximity-to-failure paper is explicitly self-described as exploratory.
- **This is a science curriculum, not medical or training advice.** It optimizes for understanding, not for a personal program.
- The empirical Damas 2016 *J Physiol* paper (not its narrative companion review) carries the muscle-damage thread in the reading list, chosen for being the strongest primary evidence rather than a secondary synthesis.
