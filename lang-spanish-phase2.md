## How to use this series

### The arc of Phase 2

Phase 1 rebuilt the **indicative** and added the **imperative**. Phase 2 is the steepest and most important climb in the whole plan: the **subjunctive mood**, plus the two structures that depend on it — **conditional sentences** and **reported speech**. By the end you read genuine B2 prose: you parse subjunctives on sight, follow hypotheticals, and track who-said-what across shifting tenses. The seven modules map onto the weeks:

| Module | Grammar | Curriculum weeks |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | Present subjunctive — forms & the logic | 17–18 |
| 10 | Present subjunctive — the trigger system (nominal clauses) | 19–23 |
| 11 | Subjunctive in adverbial clauses + real conditionals | 25–28 |
| 12 | Imperfect subjunctive + hypothetical conditionals | 29–31 |
| 13 | Perfect subjunctive + relative clauses | 33–34 |
| 14 | Reported speech (*estilo indirecto*) | 35–36 |
| 15 | Reading-critical review: *gustar*-verbs & pronominals; *ser* vs *estar* | 37–38 |

Weeks 24, 32, 39, and 40 are consolidation and self-assessment; they appear under **Checkpoints**.

### The one idea that ties Phase 2 together: mood, not tense

Everything in Phase 1 was about **tense** (when) and **aspect** (how an event is viewed). The subjunctive is neither. It is a **mood** — a grammatical marking of the speaker's **stance toward the proposition**. The indicative asserts or reports something as real (*sé que viene*, "I know he's coming"). The subjunctive marks a proposition that is **not being asserted as fact** — it is instead wished, feared, doubted, sought, hypothesised, or made to depend on someone's will (*quiero que venga*, "I want him to come" — his coming is desired, not asserted). This single idea — **the subjunctive signals non-assertion / irrealis** — organises every rule below. For a reader it is a gift: a subjunctive verb is a flag that the clause's content is *not* being affirmed as true, so the mood itself tells you the epistemic or attitudinal status of what you're reading.

The subjunctive shows up in exactly **three kinds of subordinate clause**, and the whole phase is a tour of them:

1. **Nominal clauses** (Module 10) — a *que*-clause functioning as the object of a verb of will, emotion, doubt, or influence: *espero que…, dudo que…, te pido que…*
2. **Adverbial clauses** (Module 11) — clauses of purpose, unrealised time, hypothetical concession: *para que…, cuando + future, aunque + hypothetical*
3. **Adjectival / relative clauses** (Module 13) — modifying an antecedent that is indefinite or non-existent: *busco a alguien que hable…, no hay nadie que sepa…*

A caution that makes this hard: **English has almost no subjunctive left** (only vestiges like *if I **were** you*, *I suggest he **be** present*, *God **save** the Queen*), so your native intuition gives you very little to lean on. You are building this mood essentially from scratch — which is why Module 9 (the forms) and the trigger drilling in Module 10 deserve real, unhurried effort.

### How this builds on Phase 1 and feeds Phase 3

The payoff hooks planted earlier all come due now, and new ones are planted for later:

- The **present subjunctive** (Module 9) is built on the Phase 1 **present *yo*-form** (Module 1) and you have **already produced** its forms as the negative/*usted*/*nosotros* commands of Module 8 — so this opens with *recognition*, not from zero.

- **Reported speech** (Module 14) is the capstone that exercises the **entire** tense/mood system at once — present↔imperfect, preterite↔pluperfect, future↔conditional, both subjunctives.
- **Feeding Phase 3:** the imperfect subjunctive here is the base for the *pluscuamperfecto de subjuntivo* (*hubiera hablado*), the *condicional compuesto*, and Type-3 counterfactual conditionals (Weeks 46–49).

# Module 9 — El presente de subjuntivo: formas y lógica
### *(Weeks 17–18)*

## 9.1 The idea: you have already met this

Do not treat the subjunctive forms as new. In Module 8 you produced *no hables*, *hable usted*, *hablemos*, *venga*, *sea*, *vaya* — those **are** present-subjunctive forms. This module names them, systematises them, and shows they are generated by one rule from something you already own: the Phase 1 *yo*-form.

## 9.2 Formation: the *yo*-form and the "opposite vowel"

Take the **first-person-singular present indicative**, drop the **-o**, and add the **opposite** thematic vowel: *-ar* verbs take **-e** endings, *-er/-ir* verbs take **-a** endings.

| | **-ar** (hablar) | **-er** (comer) | **-ir** (vivir) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hable | coma | viva |
| tú | hables | comas | vivas |
| él/ella/usted | hable | coma | viva |
| nosotros/as | hablemos | comamos | vivamos |
| vosotros/as | habléis | comáis | viváis |
| ellos/ellas/ustedes | hablen | coman | vivan |

Two consequences of building on the *yo*-form. First, the **1sg and 3sg are identical** (*hable*, *coma*) — as in the imperfect, you fix the person from context. Second, and more powerful: **every irregular *yo*-form carries through the entire subjunctive**. Because the base is *tengo, hago, conozco, pongo, digo, oigo, vengo, salgo, veo*, you get:

- *tener* → **tenga**, tengas, tenga, tengamos, tengáis, tengan
- *hacer* → **haga**… · *poner* → **ponga**… · *decir* → **diga**… · *conocer* → **conozca**… · *salir* → **salga**… · *oír* → **oiga**… · *venir* → **venga**… · *ver* → **vea**, veas, vea, veamos, veáis, vean

There is no separate list of "irregular subjunctives" for these — they fall out of the *yo*-form you already drilled.

## 9.3 Stem-changing verbs

**-ar and -er stem-changers** follow the same "boot" as the present indicative — change in all but *nosotros/vosotros*: *pensar* → **piense**, pienses, piense, pensemos, penséis, **piensen**; *poder* → **pueda**, puedas, pueda, podamos, podáis, **puedan**.

**-ir stem-changers add a twist**: they show the boot change **and** a further vowel-raising (**e→i, o→u**) in the *nosotros/vosotros* forms — the very raising you met in the preterite third person and the gerund:

- *pedir* (e→i) → pida, pidas, pida, **pidamos, pidáis**, pidan
- *sentir* → sienta, sientas, sienta, **sintamos, sintáis**, sientan
- *dormir* → duerma, duermas, duerma, **durmamos, durmáis**, duerman

So *pidamos, sintáis, durmamos* — note the raised vowel where the present indicative had none (*pedimos, sentís, dormimos*).

## 9.4 Spelling changes

To keep the sound before the new vowel: **-car → -que** (*buscar → busque*), **-gar → -gue** (*llegar → llegue*), **-zar → -ce** (*empezar → **empiece***, stacking with the e→ie change), **-ger/-gir → -ja** (*coger → coja*, *dirigir → dirija*), **-guir → -ga** (*seguir → siga*), **-guar → -güe** (*averiguar → averigüe*).

## 9.5 The six true irregulars

Six high-frequency verbs are not derivable from a *-o* *yo*-form (their *yo*-forms are the *-oy* group or *he*). Memorise them as a block:

| ser | estar | ir | haber | saber | dar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sea | esté | vaya | haya | sepa | dé |
| seas | estés | vayas | hayas | sepas | des |
| sea | esté | vaya | haya | sepa | dé |
| seamos | estemos | vayamos | hayamos | sepamos | demos |
| seáis | estéis | vayáis | hayáis | sepáis | deis |
| sean | estén | vayan | hayan | sepan | den |

Note the accents: *esté/estés/esté/estén* and *dé* (to distinguish *dé* the verb from *de* the preposition).

## 9.6 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** for now the job is pure recognition — see *hable/coma/tenga/sea/vaya/haya* and register "subjunctive" (the *why* comes in Modules 10–13). Watch the **1sg/3sg identity**. **Settled** — the forms are uniform and uncontroversial (RAE). The one thing worth flagging is conceptual, not formal: a subjunctive form on its own means little; it acquires meaning only from the clause it sits in, which is the whole subject of the next module.

**Sources & work (Weeks 17–18).** Wk 17: the *presente de subjuntivo* regular-formation unit in **GdU** + **Castro-A**; **B&B** subjunctive chapter, opening sections. Wk 18: the irregular/stem-changing unit; drill in **501/PMP**. **Own:** the opposite-vowel rule; the carry-through of irregular *yo*-forms; the *-ir* raising in *nosotros/vosotros*; the six irregulars. **Self-test:** form the present subjunctive of *hablar, comer, tener, conocer, pedir, dormir, buscar* and all six irregulars cold — and notice you can already say them as *usted* commands.

# Module 10 — El presente de subjuntivo: el sistema de disparadores
### *(Weeks 19–23)*

This is the central usage module of the phase. It teaches **when** the subjunctive appears in **nominal clauses** — *que*-clauses acting as the object of a main verb.

## 10.1 The governing principle and the two-subject rule

A nominal *que*-clause takes the **subjunctive** when the main predicate expresses something **other than an assertion of the clause's truth** — will, emotion, doubt, or a value judgment. It takes the **indicative** when the main predicate **asserts or reports** the clause as fact (*sé que…, creo que…, es verdad que…, dice que…* [reporting]). This is the non-assertion principle of the front matter, applied.

Before any trigger fires, one structural gate: the subjunctive requires **two different subjects**. When the subject of the main verb and the subordinate action are the **same**, Spanish uses an **infinitive**, not *que* + subjunctive:

- *Quiero **ir**.* (I want to go — same subject → infinitive)
- *Quiero que **vayas**.* (I want *you* to go — different subjects → *que* + subjunctive)
- *Espero **aprobar**.* vs *Espero que **apruebes**.*

Missing this — producing *\*quiero que yo vaya* — is a classic error. The categories below (a useful mnemonic is **WEIRDO**: Wishes, Emotions, Impersonal, Requests, Doubt, Ojalá) are all *types of non-assertion*.

## 10.2 Trigger: desire, will, wishes *(Week 19)*

Verbs of wanting, hoping, needing, preferring: *querer que, desear que, esperar que* (to hope), *preferir que, necesitar que, pedir que* — and the particle **ojalá (que)** ("I hope / if only"), which always takes the subjunctive.

> *Quiero que **vengas**.* · *Espero que **estés** bien.* · *Prefiero que lo **hagas** tú.* · *Ojalá **llueva**.*

## 10.3 Trigger: emotion & reaction *(Week 20)*

Any expression of feeling *about* the subordinate fact: *me alegro de que, me gusta que, me molesta que, me sorprende que, es una pena/lástima que, temer que, tener miedo de que, sentir que* (to regret).

> *Me alegro de que **hayas** venido.* · *Es una pena que no **puedas** venir.* · *Temo que **sea** demasiado tarde.*

The logic is subtle but consistent: even though the fact may be *true*, the main clause is not asserting it — it is **reacting emotionally** to it, so the mood is subjunctive.

## 10.4 Trigger: impersonal value judgments *(Week 21)*

Impersonal expressions that evaluate or rate the subordinate proposition: *es importante que, es necesario que, es mejor que, es bueno/malo que, es raro que, es lógico que, conviene que, hace falta que*, and expressions of possibility *es posible/probable que, puede que*.

> *Es importante que **estudies**.* · *Es mejor que te **vayas**.* · *Puede que **llueva**.*

**Critical sub-rule — certainty flips the mood.** Impersonal expressions of **certainty** take the **indicative**: *es verdad que, es evidente que, es cierto que, está claro que, es obvio que* → *Es verdad que **viene**.* But **negate** them and they express doubt, flipping to subjunctive: *No es verdad que **venga**. No está claro que **sea** así.* This assertion/non-assertion flip is the principle in action.

## 10.5 Trigger: doubt & denial + the *creer* flip *(Week 22)*

Verbs that question or deny the subordinate content: *dudar que, no creer que, no pensar que, negar que, es dudoso que*.

> *Dudo que **venga**.* · *Niego que **sea** culpa mía.*

**The *creer/pensar* flip** is the cleanest illustration of the whole principle. Affirmed, these verbs **assert a belief as fact** → indicative; negated, they **withhold assertion** → subjunctive:

- *Creo que **viene**.* (indicative — I assert I believe it) vs *No creo que **venga**.* (subjunctive — I withhold the assertion)
- *Pienso que **es** verdad.* vs *No pienso que **sea** verdad.*

Related: **quizá(s), tal vez, probablemente, posiblemente** take **either** mood, with the subjunctive signalling *greater doubt*: *Quizás **venga*** (less sure) vs *Quizás **viene*** (more sure). Reading the mood here tells you how confident the writer is.

## 10.6 Trigger: influence, requests & indirect commands *(Week 23)*

Verbs of getting someone to do something — the "indirect command" that connects back to Module 8: *pedir que, recomendar que, aconsejar que, sugerir que, mandar que, ordenar que, prohibir que, permitir que, insistir en que, rogar que*, and *decir que* **in its command sense**.

> *Te pido que **vengas**.* · *Me recomienda que lo **leas**.* · *Insisto en que te **quedes**.*

**The *decir* contrast** is worth pinning, and it previews reported speech (Module 14): *decir que* + **indicative** *reports a fact* (*Me dice que **descansa*** = "he tells me that he's resting"), while *decir que* + **subjunctive** *issues a command* (*Me dice que **descanse*** = "he tells me to rest"). The mood alone distinguishes reporting from commanding. (Note that several influence verbs — *hacer, dejar, mandar, prohibir, permitir* — also freely take an **infinitive**: *Me hizo esperar, Déjame ir, Le prohibió salir*.)

## 10.7 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** the practical skill is to read the **main predicate as a mood-signal** — spot the trigger, and you know the *que*-clause's mood before you reach the verb; conversely, an unexpected subjunctive sends you back to find the trigger (a wish, a doubt, an emotion) that licensed it, which is itself information about the writer's stance. Watch the **certainty/negation flips** (*es verdad que* + ind vs *no es verdad que* + subj; *creo* vs *no creo*). **Settled** — the trigger system is standard and codified; the only genuine flexibility is *quizás* + either mood and a handful of verbs that alternate subjunctive/infinitive. English offers little help here, so trust the principle over translation.

**Sources & work (Weeks 19–23).** One trigger category per week in **GdU** + **Castro-A**; **B&B** subjunctive chapter (noun clauses, doubt, influence) throughout. **Own:** the two-subject rule; the five trigger families; the certainty and *creer* flips. **Self-test:** given a main clause, complete the *que*-clause in the right mood — *Quiero que… / Creo que… / No creo que… / Es verdad que… / Es posible que… / Me alegro de que…* — and justify each by the non-assertion principle.

# Module 11 — El subjuntivo en oraciones adverbiales y las condicionales reales
### *(Weeks 25–28)*

The subjunctive's second home: **adverbial clauses**. Plus the first conditional type, which — importantly — does *not* take the subjunctive.

## 11.1 Temporal clauses: subjunctive for the unrealised future *(Week 25)*

With *cuando, en cuanto, tan pronto como, hasta que, mientras, después de que, una vez que*, the mood depends on whether the event is **realised** (habitual or past → **indicative**) or **still unrealised** (future → **subjunctive**):

- *Cuando **llegues**, llámame.* (future → subjunctive) vs *Cuando **llego** a casa, ceno.* (habitual → indicative) vs *Cuando **llegué**, cené.* (past → indicative)
- *En cuanto **termine**, te aviso.* (future) vs *En cuanto **termino**, me voy.* (habitual)

The logic is the non-assertion principle again: a future event referred to before it happens is **not yet fact**. Note **antes de que** *always* takes the subjunctive (the event by definition hasn't occurred at the reference point): *Antes de que te **vayas**…* — but with the **same subject**, use *antes de* + **infinitive**: *antes de salir*.

## 11.2 Purpose and other fixed-subjunctive conjunctions *(Week 26)*

Some conjunctions **always** take the subjunctive because they inherently mark something unrealised or hypothetical:

- **Purpose:** *para que, a fin de que* — *Te lo explico para que lo **entiendas**.*
- **Negative circumstance:** *sin que* — *Salió sin que nadie lo **viera**.*
- **Condition/exception:** *a menos que, a no ser que, salvo que* (unless); *con tal de que, siempre que* (provided that); *en caso de que* (in case) — *No iré a menos que **vengas** tú. En caso de que **necesites** ayuda, llámame.*

With the **same subject**, *para* takes an infinitive (*Estudio para **aprender***) versus *para que* + subjunctive across subjects (*Te explico para que **aprendas***).

## 11.3 Concessive clauses: *aunque* and the fact/hypothesis contrast *(Week 27)*

*aunque* ("although / even if") takes **either** mood, and the choice carries real meaning:

- *aunque* + **indicative** concedes a **fact**: *Aunque **llueve**, voy a salir.* ("Although it **is** raining — a fact — I'll go out.")
- *aunque* + **subjunctive** concedes a **hypothesis** or downplays known information: *Aunque **llueva**, saldré.* ("Even **if** it rains" — hypothetical.) Also *Aunque **sea** difícil, lo haré.* (even though/if it's hard — conceded, not asserted).

The same contrast runs through *a pesar de que, por más que, por mucho que, aun cuando*. Reading the mood tells you whether the writer treats the concession as established fact or as supposition — a genuine comprehension nuance.

## 11.4 Conditional sentences, Type 1: real conditions — and NOT the subjunctive *(Week 28)*

This is the counter-intuitive week, and the rule must be hammered: for **real, likely conditions**, *si* ("if") takes the **present indicative**, **never** the subjunctive.

> *Si **tienes** tiempo, ven / vamos / ayúdame.* · *Si **estudias**, aprobarás.* · *Si **llueve**, me quedo en casa.*

Structure: **si + present indicative + [present indicative / future / imperative]**. The trap is that English "if" tempts you toward a subjunctive; in Spanish, **the present subjunctive is never used directly after *si***. (The *imperfect* subjunctive *is* used after *si* — but only for hypotheticals, which is the next module.) Fix this now: *si* for a real condition → indicative.

## 11.5 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** with temporal and concessive clauses, **read the mood for meaning** — *cuando llega* (habitual/factual) vs *cuando llegue* (future/unrealised); *aunque llueve* (it is raining) vs *aunque llueva* (even if). Treat *para que, sin que, antes de que, a menos que* as reliable subjunctive-signals. **Settled**, with the genuine *aunque* alternation being meaningful rather than free. **Overstated/error to kill:** "*si* takes the subjunctive because English uses *if*" — false for real conditions (present indicative).

**Sources & work (Weeks 25–28).** The *oraciones temporales*, *finales/adverbiales*, *concesivas*, and *condicionales reales* units in **GdU** + **Castro-A**; **B&B** (index "subjunctive: adverbial clauses," "conditional sentences"). **Own:** the future/habitual mood split in temporal clauses; the fixed-subjunctive conjunctions; the *aunque* contrast; *si* + present indicative for Type 1. **Self-test:** choose the mood in *Cuando (llegar) …*, *Aunque (ser) difícil …*, *Te lo digo para que (saber) …*, *Si (tener) tiempo …* — and explain each.

# Module 12 — El imperfecto de subjuntivo y las condicionales hipotéticas
### *(Weeks 29–31)*

The past subjunctive — and the payoff of Phase 1. This module unlocks hypothetical conditionals and the sequence of tenses.

## 12.1 Formation: straight from the preterite *(Week 29)*

Take the **third-person-plural preterite**, drop **-ron**, and add the **-ra** *or* **-se** endings. That is the whole rule, and it means **every irregular is already built in** because it lives in the preterite stem you drilled in Phase 1.

| from *ellos* preterite | **-ra** form | **-se** form |
|---|---|---|
| hablaron | hablara, hablaras, hablara, habláramos, hablarais, hablaran | hablase, hablases, hablase, hablásemos, hablaseis, hablasen |
| comieron | comiera… comiéramos… | comiese… comiésemos… |
| vivieron | viviera… | viviese… |

And the irregulars, free:

*tuvieron → **tuviera**/tuviese · dijeron → **dijera** · hicieron → **hiciera** · fueron (ser/ir) → **fuera** · pudieron → **pudiera** · pusieron → **pusiera** · supieron → **supiera** · quisieron → **quisiera** · vinieron → **viniera** · pidieron → **pidiera** · durmieron → **durmiera** · leyeron → **leyera***

Note the accent on the *nosotros* form (*habláramos, comiéramos, tuviéramos, fuéramos*). **Both -ra and -se are Peninsular**; the **-ra** form is more common overall, the **-se** more formal and literary — you must **read both fluently**, and may produce whichever you prefer.

## 12.2 Uses & the sequence of tenses *(Week 30)*

The imperfect subjunctive is triggered by the **same triggers** as the present subjunctive (Modules 10–11), but appears when the main verb is **past or conditional**. This is the **sequence of tenses**:

- **Present main → present subjunctive:** *Quiero que **vengas**.*
- **Past main → imperfect subjunctive:** *Quería que **vinieras**. Me pidió que **viniera**. Era importante que **estudiaras**. No creía que **fuera** verdad.*

Two further homes for the imperfect subjunctive: **como si** ("as if"), which always takes it (or the pluperfect subjunctive): *Habla como si **fuera** un experto. Me miró como si no me **conociera***; and **softened wishes/politeness** as a standalone: *Quisiera un café* (very polite "I'd like"), *Ojalá **viniera*** (I wish he'd come — presented as unlikely).

## 12.3 Conditional sentences, Type 2: hypothetical / counterfactual present *(Week 31)*

The structure that the imperfect subjunctive exists for: **si + imperfect subjunctive + conditional**, for conditions that are hypothetical or contrary to present fact.

> *Si **tuviera** dinero, **viajaría**.* ("If I had money [but I don't], I'd travel.") · *Si **fuera** tú, no lo **haría**.* · *Si **pudiéramos**, te **ayudaríamos**.*

The mirror-image error to Week 28's must also be killed: **never *si* + conditional**. It is *\*Si tendría dinero…* → **Si tuviera dinero…** The conditional goes in the **main** clause; the *si*-clause takes the **imperfect subjunctive**. Reading *si* + imperfect subjunctive as "if (hypothetically)…" is a major comprehension unlock — it is everywhere in fiction and argument.

## 12.4 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** recognise both **-ra and -se** forms as the same mood; use the **sequence of tenses** as a parsing aid — a past main verb predicts an imperfect-subjunctive subordinate; read *como si* + past subjunctive as "as if"; read *si* + imperfect subjunctive as a hypothesis. **Settled and pan-Hispanic**, with the **-ra/-se** distribution the one dialect-register note (both live in Spain; Latin America leans *-ra*). *(Historical aside for recognition only: the **-ra** form was originally a pluperfect indicative and still appears that way in very literary/journalistic Spanish — "el rey que **gobernara** el país" for "had governed"; you'll meet it rarely and passively.)*

**Sources & work (Weeks 29–31).** Wk 29: the *imperfecto de subjuntivo* formation unit in **GdU** + **Castro-A** (note the *-se* forms); derive from preterite stems in **501**. Wk 30: uses + sequence of tenses. Wk 31: the *condicionales irreales* unit; **B&B** (index "imperfect subjunctive," "conditional sentences"). **Own:** the *-ron → -ra/-se* rule; the sequence of tenses; *como si*; *si* + imperfect subjunctive + conditional. **Self-test:** form *tener, hacer, ser, poder, pedir* in both *-ra* and *-se*; convert *Quiero que vengas* to a past frame (*Quería que vinieras*); build three Type-2 conditionals and confirm you never write *si* + conditional.

# Module 13 — El perfecto de subjuntivo y las oraciones de relativo
### *(Weeks 33–34)*

The last subjunctive **form** and the last subjunctive **environment**.

## 13.1 The perfect subjunctive *(Week 33)*

Compound architecture once more (Module 2), now with a **subjunctive** *haber*: **present subjunctive of *haber* + participle**.

| | *haber* (pres. subj.) | + participle |
|---|---|---|
| yo | haya | + hablado |
| tú | hayas | |
| él | haya | |
| nosotros | hayamos | |
| vosotros | hayáis | |
| ellos | hayan | |

*haya hablado, hayas comido, hayan vuelto* — with the same invariable participle and the same irregulars (*haya hecho/dicho/puesto/escrito*). **Use:** after a trigger, for an action seen as **completed** relative to the (present/future) main verb: *Espero que **hayas llegado** bien. Es posible que ya se **hayan ido**. Me alegro de que **hayas venido**. Dudo que lo **haya hecho**.* In the sequence of tenses it is the "already-done" counterpart to the plain present subjunctive.

## 13.2 The subjunctive in relative (adjectival) clauses *(Week 34)*

The third and last subjunctive environment: a relative clause modifying a noun (its **antecedent**). The mood turns on whether the antecedent is **specific/existent** (→ indicative) or **indefinite, hypothetical, or non-existent** (→ subjunctive):

- *Busco a alguien que **habla** ruso.* (a **specific** person known to speak Russian → indicative) vs *Busco a alguien que **hable** ruso.* (**anyone** who speaks Russian — indefinite → subjunctive)
- *Tengo un coche que **funciona**.* (it exists → indicative) vs *No tengo ningún coche que **funcione**.* (non-existent → subjunctive)
- *¿Hay alguien que **sepa** la respuesta?* (existence in question → subjunctive) · *No hay nadie que **sepa**…* (non-existent → subjunctive)

Same non-assertion principle: if the antecedent's existence is not being asserted, the clause describing it is subjunctive. (A syntactic tell that often accompanies the shift: the **personal *a*** may be dropped before an indefinite human antecedent — *Busco **un** secretario que hable inglés*.)

## 13.3 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** read *haya* + participle as the completed-action subjunctive; and in relative clauses, **read the mood to learn whether the antecedent is real or hypothetical** — *un piso que tiene* (a flat that has — real) vs *un piso que tenga* (a flat that would have — sought). **Settled** and pan-Hispanic.

Wk 34: the *oraciones de relativo* + mood unit; **B&B** (index "subjunctive: relative clauses," "perfect subjunctive"). **Own:** *haber* subjunctive + participle; the existence/definiteness mood rule. **Self-test:** complete *Espero que (terminar, ya) …* → *hayas terminado*; and contrast *Necesito un libro que (explicar) …* real vs hypothetical, explaining the mood.

# Module 14 — El estilo indirecto (reported speech)
### *(Weeks 35–36)*

The capstone. Reporting what was said exercises the **entire** system at once, which is why it sits here — it is both a topic and a full-phase review.

## 14.1 Present-frame reporting *(Week 35)*

When the reporting verb is **present, future, or perfect** (*dice, dirá, ha dicho*), the reported tenses generally **do not shift**; only **pronouns, possessives, and deictics** adjust to the new speaker's viewpoint.

- Statement: "Estoy cansado" → *Dice que **está** cansado.* · "Vendré mañana" → *Dice que **vendrá** mañana.*
- Questions become **embedded** (normal word order, no inversion, no question marks): "¿Dónde vives?" → *Me pregunta **dónde vivo**.*; yes/no questions take **si**: "¿Vienes?" → *Me pregunta **si vengo**.*
- **Commands become subjunctive** (tying back to Modules 8, 10.6): "¡Ven!" → *Me dice que **venga**.*

## 14.2 Past-frame reporting and the backshift *(Week 36)*

When the reporting verb is **past** (*dijo, preguntó, comentó*), the reported tenses **backshift** — this is the sequence of tenses applied to speech, and it drills every pairing you have learned:

| Direct (original) | Reported after a past verb |
|---|---|
| present — *estoy* | imperfect — *estaba* |
| preterite / perfect — *llegué / he llegado* | pluperfect — *había llegado* |
| future — *vendré* | conditional — *vendría* |
| imperative — *¡ven!* | imperfect subjunctive — *viniera* |
| present subjunctive — *que vengas* | imperfect subjunctive — *que viniera* |

> "Estoy cansado" → *Dijo que **estaba** cansado.* · "Terminé el informe" → *Dijo que **había terminado** el informe.* · "Te ayudaré" → *Dijo que me **ayudaría**.* · "¡Espérame!" → *Me dijo que le **esperara**.*

**Deictics shift** with the frame: *hoy → aquel día, mañana → al día siguiente, ayer → el día anterior, aquí → allí, este → aquel, ahora → entonces*.

## 14.3 Reading & calibration

**Reading — the payoff:** this is how you **track who said or thought what** across a page of narrative, where reported thought and speech weave through past tenses. Seeing *Dijo que había llegado y que vendría al día siguiente* and unpacking it to the original *"He llegado y vendré mañana"* is the comprehension skill this module builds. **Settled** — the backshift is standard; in casual/journalistic Spanish the shift is sometimes left undone for immediacy (*Dijo que **viene** mañana*), which you'll simply see and understand.

**Sources & work (Weeks 35–36).** Wk 35: the *estilo indirecto* (present frame) unit in **GdU** + **Castro-A**. Wk 36: the past-frame/backshift unit; **B&B** (index "reported speech," "sequence of tenses"). **Own:** the embedded-question and command→subjunctive rules; the full backshift table; the deictic shifts. **Self-test:** report, after *Dijo que…*, the sentences "Estoy aquí," "Llegué ayer," "Te llamaré mañana," and "¡Ven a las ocho!" — producing *estaba allí, había llegado el día anterior, me llamaría al día siguiente, que fuera a las ocho*.

# Module 15 — Repaso crítico para la lectura: verbos como *gustar*, verbos pronominales, *ser* / *estar*
### *(Weeks 37–38)*

Two high-frequency structures that, if misparsed, quietly wreck comprehension. They are grammatically unrelated to each other but share one trait: both reverse or complicate the English mapping, so both need deliberate attention before you meet dense authentic prose.

## 15.1 Verbs like *gustar* + pronominal verbs *(Week 37)*

**The *gustar* construction inverts subject and object relative to English.** In *Me gusta el café*, the grammatical **subject is *el café*** ("coffee is pleasing **to me**") and the person is an **indirect object** (dative experiencer). The verb therefore agrees with the **thing**, not the person: *Me **gusta** el café* / *Me **gustan** los libros*. Word order is typically **[IO pronoun] + verb + subject**, often reinforced by a fronted *a mí/a ti*: *A mí me gusta…, A ella le gustan…*

The same pattern governs a whole family: *encantar, molestar, interesar, importar, doler* (*me duele la cabeza*), *faltar, quedar, apetecer, hacer falta, sobrar, costar* (*me cuesta entenderlo*), *tocar*. **Reading skill:** when you see *me/te/le/nos/os/les* + one of these verbs, identify the **following noun as the subject** — the reverse of the instinctive English reading.

**Pronominal verbs**, reviewed for reading: truly **reflexive** (*me lavo*), **reciprocal** (*se abrazan*), **inherently pronominal** verbs that exist only with the clitic (*arrepentirse, quejarse, atreverse, darse cuenta de*), and the **aspectual/change-of-meaning** *se* (*ir* → *irse* "leave," *dormir* → *dormirse* "fall asleep," *comer* → *comerse* "eat up"). Highest-value of all for reading is the **involuntary/accidental *se me*** construction: *Se me **cayó** el vaso* ("I dropped the glass / it fell on me"), *Se me **olvidó***, *Se le **rompió***. Parse it as "the glass fell, [affecting] me" — the *se* removes agency and the following noun is the subject.

## 15.2 *Ser* vs *estar*: B2 nuances + *estar* + participle *(Week 38)*

Phase 1 introduced the split; here are the distinctions that matter at B2. **Core division:** *ser* = identity, definition, inherent qualities, origin, material, possession, time/date, and the **location of events** (*La fiesta **es** en mi casa*); *estar* = **location of things/people** (*Madrid **está** en España*), **temporary states/conditions**, results, and the progressive.

**The meaning-changing adjective pairs** — the classic B2 reading nuance, where the *ser/estar* choice changes the word's sense:

| adjective | with **ser** | with **estar** |
|---|---|---|
| aburrido | boring | bored |
| listo | clever | ready |
| rico | rich | tasty |
| malo | bad | ill |
| bueno | good (quality) | tasty / attractive |
| verde | green (colour) | unripe |
| vivo | sharp, lively | alive |
| orgulloso | proud (arrogant) | proud (of something) |
| callado | quiet (by nature) | being quiet (now) |

**Estar + participle = a resultant state** — the *result* of a completed action, with the participle **agreeing** in gender/number: *La puerta **está abierta*** (the result of opening it), *Los platos **están rotos***, *La tienda **está cerrada***. This is the payoff of the Module 2 diagnostic and the direct set-up for Week 41's passive, where you'll contrast the **state** *está abierta* with the **action** *fue abierta* ("was opened [by someone]").

## 15.3 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** for *gustar*-verbs and *se me*, retrain the **subject-hunt** to the noun *after* the verb; for *ser/estar*, **read the choice for meaning** (*es aburrido* ≠ *está aburrido*) and read *estar* + agreeing participle as a resulting state. **Settled**, with the usual note that *ser/estar* boundaries have a few genuinely variable or idiomatic cases you'll absorb by exposure.

Wk 38: the *ser/estar* units; **B&B** ser/estar chapter. **Own:** the *gustar* agreement pattern; the *se me* construction; the meaning-changing *ser/estar* pairs; *estar* + participle as state. **Self-test:** explain the subject of *Me gustan las novelas* and of *Se me perdió la llave*; and give the meaning of *ser/estar* + *aburrido, listo, rico, malo, verde* in each pairing.
