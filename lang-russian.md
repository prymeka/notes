# A Polish speaker's fastest path to reading Russian literature

**A native Polish speaker focusing on reading comprehension can reach B2 Russian in roughly 14–18 months at 5 hours per week.** This is dramatically faster than the 3+ years an English speaker would need, thanks to shared Slavic grammar (cases, verbal aspect, word order) and **77% basic vocabulary cognate overlap**. The plan below combines Polish-medium textbooks — which skip lengthy explanations of concepts you already know — with the best English-medium resources for grammar reference and literature-oriented study, plus graded readers that build systematically toward authentic Russian classics. The four-phase approach front-loads grammar recognition for reading, then shifts progressively toward extensive reading of real Russian texts.

---

## Why Polish speakers have an enormous head start

Polish and Russian share the core architecture that makes Russian notoriously difficult for English speakers. You already understand six grammatical cases intuitively (Polish has seven), verbal aspect (perfective/imperfective), grammatical gender with adjective agreement, flexible word order, and consonant palatalization. The FSI estimates **1,100 hours** for English speakers to reach professional proficiency in Russian; linguistic analysis and polyglot community reports suggest Polish speakers need roughly **40–50% fewer hours** — around **380–460 hours for balanced B2**, or **300–380 hours for reading-focused B2**.

At 5 hours per week, that translates to approximately **60–76 weeks of study**, or **14–18 months**. The main obstacles you will face are not grammatical concepts but rather: mobile word stress (versus Polish fixed penultimate stress), vowel reduction in unstressed syllables, approximately **1,500 documented false friends** (e.g., *запоминать* means "to memorize," not "to forget" like Polish *zapomnieć*; *урод* means "ugly person," not "beauty" like Polish *uroda*), and building the **5,000–6,000 word passive vocabulary** required for B2 reading — of which roughly 3,000–4,000 will be genuinely new to you.

| Milestone | Estimated hours | Weeks at 5h/week | Calendar time |
|-----------|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| A1 reading | 40–60 | 8–12 | 2–3 months |
| A2 reading | 80–120 | 16–24 | 4–6 months |
| B1 reading | 180–240 | 36–48 | 9–12 months |
| **B2 reading** | **300–380** | **60–76** | **14–18 months** |

## Phase 1 — Foundation (months 1–4, target A1→A2)

This phase builds your Russian grammar recognition system and first 1,000–1,300 words. As a Polish speaker, you can move through case explanations at double speed — focus on learning Russian-specific endings rather than the concept of cases itself. Dedicate roughly 30% of time to grammar, 20% to vocabulary, 20% to easy reading, and 30% to intensive text work with a dictionary.

### Primary textbook (Polish-medium)

**"Rosyjski w tłumaczeniach. Gramatyka," Part 1 (A1)** by Katarzyna Łukasiak and Jacek Sawiński, published by Preston Publishing. Book with MP3 audio (6+ hours). This is a bestselling Polish-to-Russian translation exercise course that ranked #1 in a poll of 163 Russian learners on the blog Dagatlumaczy.pl. Each chapter covers one grammar topic with hundreds of Polish→Russian translation sentences, building passive grammar recognition — exactly what reading requires. Grammar headings appear in both Polish and Russian, constantly highlighting parallels and differences. Answer keys included; fully designed for self-study. The translation-based method is impossible to replicate in an English textbook and uniquely efficient for Polish speakers.

### Secondary textbook (English-medium, grammar-forward)

**"The New Penguin Russian Course" by Nicholas J. Brown**, Penguin Books, 1996, 514 pages. Covers A1 through roughly B1. This is the single most recommended self-study Russian textbook across Reddit's r/russian (65+ mentions), polyglot blogs, and language forums. Its 30 lessons with complete answer key are heavily grammar-and-reading oriented (~70% grammar, 20% reading, 10% other), with no audio — a non-issue for a reading-focused learner. All Russian texts include English translations. The systematic coverage of all six cases, verb aspects, and word formation builds exactly the structural knowledge needed for literature. At roughly **$15–18**, it is remarkably affordable. Its main weakness is aggressive pacing in later chapters and dated vocabulary (1996), but for a Polish speaker who already understands Slavic grammar, the pace is actually appropriate.

### Grammar drill supplement

**"Schaum's Outline of Russian Grammar" (3rd edition) by James S. Levine**, McGraw-Hill, 2019. Covers A1 through B2. At ~$16 with **4.7/5 stars on Amazon**, this provides hundreds of grammar exercises with answer keys and stress marks throughout — ideal for self-drilling the Russian-specific endings and patterns. Pair this with your primary textbook for systematic practice.

### Essential reference

**"Polsko-rosyjski słownik fałszywych przyjaciół tłumacza" by Andrzej Wijas** — a dictionary of approximately 1,500 Polish-Russian false friends, available free as PDF at falsefriends.awijas.pl. Consult this regularly from day one. False friends are the single biggest trap for Polish speakers, and systematic awareness prevents fossilized errors.

### First reading materials

**Zubakhin "First Russian Reader for Beginners: Bilingual for Speakers of English"** (Language Practice Publishing, A1–A2). Parallel Russian-English texts with progressive vocabulary (~30 new words per chapter, with constant recycling). Free audio at lppbooks.com. Well-reviewed on Amazon as "the best beginner's reading/listening book I have found." This gets you reading continuous Russian text from month one.

**ELI Russian Graded Readers** (ELI Publishing, Italy): *Пиковая дама* (Pushkin's "Queen of Spades," adapted, A1+) with stress marks, glossary, exercises, and audio CD. Professional European publisher; available through russianbookshop.co.uk. Only ~4–5 Russian titles exist, but the production quality is excellent.

## Phase 2 — Intermediate grammar and first adapted classics (months 5–10, target A2→B1)

Shift your time allocation toward more reading: 20% grammar, 20% vocabulary, 40% extensive reading of graded texts, 20% intensive reading with dictionary. Your grammar study now focuses on the areas where Russian diverges most from Polish: verbs of motion (prefixed and unprefixed), Russian-specific prepositional governance, and stress patterns.

### Primary textbook (Polish-medium)

**"Rosyjski w tłumaczeniach. Gramatyka," Part 2 (A2–B1)** by Łukasiak and Sawiński, Preston Publishing. Continues the translation-method approach through intermediate grammar. Follow this with **Part 3 (B1–B2)** when ready — the four-part series covers A1 through C1, providing a single coherent grammar spine for the entire journey.

### Comprehensive Polish-medium course

**"Język rosyjski dla Polaków" (Parts 1–2) by Irena Matczyńska** (with Tatiana Pachałkowa-Soicz), Wydawnictwo Naukowe UMK (Nicolaus Copernicus University Press, Toruń). This is the only major textbook explicitly designed from the ground up for Polish speakers, developed by a professor with decades of experience teaching Russian to Polish university students. It "sensitizes learners to differences between Polish and Russian," includes authentic and adapted texts (including a fragment of Żeromski's *Przedwiośnie* in Russian), and covers through A2+ with academic rigor. The keyed exercises support self-study. Use this alongside the Preston series — it provides the communicative context and authentic reading that pure translation drills lack.

### Grammar reference (begin using now, keep forever)

**"A Comprehensive Russian Grammar" (3rd edition) by Terence Wade**, Wiley-Blackwell, 2011, ~650 pages. This is universally regarded as **the gold-standard English-language Russian reference grammar**. *Choice* magazine called it "the most complete, accurate, and authoritative English-language reference grammar of Russian ever published." Every Russian example has a full English translation. It is organized by grammar topic (not progressively), so use it as a look-up reference whenever you encounter an unfamiliar construction in your reading. Available used for ~$25; new copies ~$120. A companion workbook is sold separately.

### Graded readers for this phase

**Zlatoust Library (Библиотека Златоуста)** — the gold-standard graded reader series for Russian, published by Zlatoust (St. Petersburg), the leading publisher of Russian-as-a-Foreign-Language materials. Their system aligns to TORFL lexical minimums across five levels. At A2–B1, key titles include:

- *Кавказский пленник* (Tolstoy's "Prisoner of the Caucasus," adapted, 1,300-word vocabulary)
- *Пиковая дама* (Pushkin's "Queen of Spades," adapted, 3 stories)
- *Трудно быть богом* (Strugatsky brothers' "Hard to Be a God," adapted)
- *Азазель* (Boris Akunin's detective novel, adapted, B1, 2,300-word vocabulary)
- *Белые ночи* (Dostoevsky's "White Nights," adapted, B1)
- *Ионыч* (Chekhov, adapted, B1)

Each includes stress marks, glossary, comprehension exercises, and answer keys. Available through Ruslania.com (Finland) and some on Amazon/AbeBooks. **These are the most important reading resources in the entire plan** — they let you engage with adapted Russian literary classics while systematically building vocabulary according to TORFL standards.

**"Short Stories in Russian for Beginners" by Olly Richards** (Teach Yourself, 2018, A2–B1). Eight original stories across genres with bolded stress marks, glossary, comprehension questions, and audiobook. At ~$12–16, a solid bridge for building reading stamina before Zlatoust's literary adaptations.

### Supplementary vocabulary work

**"Rosyjski. Repetytorium leksykalno-tematyczne A2–B1"** (Edgard Publishing) — 160+ exercises with texts and dialogues across 13 thematic chapters, MP3, answer keys. Excellent for structured vocabulary expansion through reading-style exercises.

## Phase 3 — Upper-intermediate push to B2 (months 11–15, target B1→B2)

Now reading should dominate: 10% grammar refinement, 20% vocabulary through context, 50% extensive reading, 20% intensive literary reading. The critical grammar you must master in this phase for literary reading is **participles** (*причастия* — present/past, active/passive) and **verbal adverbs** (*деепричастия*). These constructions are rare in conversational Russian but pervasive in literary prose.

### Grammar completion (Polish-medium)

**"Rosyjski w tłumaczeniach. Gramatyka," Parts 3 (B1–B2) and 4 (B2–C1)** by Łukasiak and Sawiński, Preston Publishing. These final volumes cover all grammar required for B2+ reading comprehension, including participles, verbal adverbs, complex subordination, and passive constructions. Completing all four parts gives you the full grammatical toolkit for Russian literature.

### Literature-oriented English-medium textbook

**"Russian Through Propaganda" (Books 3–4) by Mark R. Pettus, PhD (Princeton)**, self-published, 3rd edition. This extraordinary four-book series is explicitly designed to take learners from zero to reading Russian literature, created by a Princeton Slavicist and translator. Books 1–2 (A1–A2) use Soviet propaganda posters to illustrate grammar; Book 3 (B1) transitions to Russian paintings and poems, culminating with Pushkin's *The Bronze Horseman* in its entirety and Chekhov short stories; **Book 4 (B2) includes Dostoevsky, Gogol, and Shalamov**. All texts feature stress marks. Free YouTube video lectures accompany each lesson. Answer keys and learner dictionaries included. At **4.5+ stars on Amazon**, this is the single most uniquely aligned resource for a literature-focused learner. Even if you use Polish-medium textbooks for grammar, Books 3–4 are invaluable for their curated literary reading progression.

### Advanced usage and register guide

**"Using Russian: A Guide to Contemporary Usage" by Derek Offord**, Cambridge University Press, 2nd edition, 2005, 528 pages. Rated **4.8/5 stars on Amazon** and called "one of the most exciting and useful books in the field" by the *East-West Journal*. This is not a grammar book — it covers style, register, idioms, and usage that other references ignore. Chapters on the differences between literary, formal, colloquial, and journalistic Russian are precisely what you need to understand why Tolstoy's prose reads differently from a newspaper. The chapter on word formation helps decode unfamiliar vocabulary by recognizing roots and affixes. Begin using at B1+; it becomes increasingly valuable as you approach authentic literary texts.

### Graded readers for this phase

**Zlatoust Library Levels 4–5 (B2–C1)**: fragments of prose by Pushkin, Tolstoy, Gogol, Goncharov, Chekhov, Teffi, Nabokov, Bulgakov, and Ilf and Petrov. These are the final stepping stone before fully unassisted reading.

**"Short Stories in Russian for Intermediate Learners" by Olly Richards** (Teach Yourself, 2021, B1–B2). Eight stories with glossary and comprehension questions. Note that this volume lacks stress marks, unlike the beginner book.

### Grammar reference upgrade

**"Modern Russian Grammar: A Practical Guide" by John Dunn and Shamil Khairov**, Routledge, 2009. Combines traditional and functional grammar — Part A explains structures, Part B shows how they're used communicatively. One author is a native Russian speaker, giving superior accuracy for nuanced questions. Several reviewers consider it "even better than Wade" for practical understanding. A companion workbook provides exercises.

## Phase 4 — B2 consolidation and the bridge to real literature (months 15–18)

Allocate 80% of time to reading, split between extensive reading (60%) and intensive literary analysis (20%), with only 10% each for grammar review and vocabulary. Your goal: **70–80% comprehension of B2-level texts on first reading**, with dictionary support for the remainder.

### The critical bridge: annotated original texts

**Mark R. Pettus "Reading Russian" series** — this is the single most important resource for your final goal. These are **unsimplified, original Russian literary texts** with comprehensive learner support: stress marks on every word, new facing English translations made specifically for language learners, and detailed vocabulary notes including aspectual verb pairs and conjugation types. Titles include:

- *Reading Chekhov's Stories in Russian, Vols. 1 and 2* (18 stories total) — **start here**
- *Reading Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich in Russian*
- *Reading Dostoevsky's Notes from the Underground in Russian*
- *Reading Lermontov's A Hero of Our Time / The Demon in Russian*
- *Reading Chekhov's Plays in Russian: Uncle Vanya / The Cherry Orchard*
- *Reading Crime and Punishment in Russian* (condensed but unedited)
- *Reading Zamyatin's "We" in Russian*

At **$13–17 per volume** with **4.6+ star Amazon ratings**, these bridge the gap between adapted texts and fully unassisted reading. Begin with Chekhov's stories — the consensus #1 entry point for learners approaching Russian literature, thanks to clear prose, modern vocabulary, and manageable length.

### Parallel text collections

**"Russian Stories: A Dual-Language Book" (ed. Gleb Struve)**, Dover Publications. Twelve stories by Pushkin, Dostoevsky, Gogol, Tolstoy, and Chekhov with Russian on the left page and English on the right. No stress marks, but at ~$10, it is a classic and affordable companion for building confidence with original texts.

**"Short Stories in Russian: New Penguin Parallel Text" (ed. Brian James Baer)**, Penguin, 2012. Contemporary Russian stories by Lukyanenko, Ulitskaya, Sorokin, and others. Difficulty is notably steep — best for strong B2 readers wanting exposure to modern prose after building confidence on 19th-century classics.

## What B2 reading actually unlocks — and what still requires patience

At B2, roughly **92% of vocabulary in contemporary Russian fiction** falls within your range (per a *Russian Language Journal* study analyzing Grishkovets's novel *Реки*). This means you can read with modest dictionary support. Specifically, B2 unlocks:

- **Fully accessible**: contemporary short fiction, newspaper articles, blogs, non-fiction
- **Accessible with dictionary**: Chekhov's stories, Pushkin's prose (*The Queen of Spades*, *The Captain's Daughter*), Bulgakov's *Heart of a Dog*, Dovlatov, Zoshchenko's humor, Turgenev's novellas (*Asya*, *First Love*)
- **Challenging but achievable**: Dostoevsky's shorter works, Gogol's satirical stories (*The Nose*, *The Overcoat*), Lermontov's *A Hero of Our Time*
- **Requires C1+**: Tolstoy's major novels (dense philosophical passages), Dostoevsky's longer works in full, poetry (Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin*, Akhmatova)

The transition from B2 to comfortable C1 literary reading typically takes an additional **1–2 years of regular reading practice** (30+ minutes daily). The Pettus annotated editions are specifically designed to accelerate this transition.

## Conclusion

The three strategic advantages to exploit are your Polish grammar intuitions, the translation-based Polish textbook approach (the Preston *Rosyjski w tłumaczeniach* series), and the literature-focused trajectory offered by Pettus's *Russian Through Propaganda* and *Reading Russian* series. No other textbook series on the market explicitly builds from zero toward reading Dostoevsky and Chekhov in the original — these two combined effectively create a purpose-built highway to your goal. Wade's grammar and Offord's usage guide serve as the reference pillars you will reach for repeatedly as texts grow more complex. The Zlatoust adapted classics provide the crucial middle bridge where you encounter real Russian authors within a controlled vocabulary. At 5 hours per week, reaching B2 reading proficiency in **14–18 months** is a realistic and efficient target — significantly faster than the 3+ years an English speaker would need — after which Chekhov, Bulgakov, and Turgenev open up with only modest dictionary support, and the path to Dostoevsky and Tolstoy becomes a matter of continued reading practice rather than further study.
