# Research Report: Building Blocks for a Global History-of-Art Autodidact Curriculum

## TL;DR
- The strongest **global spine** is Kleiner, *Gardner's Art Through the Ages: A Global History*, 16th ed. (2020), complemented by Stokstad & Cothren, *Art History*, 7th ed. (2024/25); pair one survey with tradition-specific scholarly volumes (mostly the **Oxford History of Art** and **Yale/Pelican History of Art** series) and a parallel methods/historiography track.
- **Free resources are unusually strong** for this field: Smarthistory (with Khan Academy), the Met's Heilbrunn Timeline, the Städel's own free Digital Collection/Digitorials (a Frankfurt asset), Open Yale's Roman Architecture course, and multiple open-access museum image banks — enough to build a rigorous parallel curriculum at near-zero cost.
- The **live scholarly debates** to tag are: Eurocentrism/decolonizing the canon; "global art history" vs "world art studies"; whether "art" validly applies to ritual/functional non-Western objects; connoisseurship/attribution; conservation ethics (Sistine Chapel cleaning); and formalism vs the social history of art / the status of iconology after Panofsky.

## Key Findings
1. There is no single global survey that all scholars endorse; the field's two dominant survey "spines" (Gardner's/Kleiner and Stokstad & Cothren) are both current, both broadly global, and both criticized as compromise-driven and residually Eurocentric. Treat them as scaffolding, not authority.
2. For deep coverage, the accepted move is to layer specialist volumes. The **Oxford History of Art** and the **Yale University Press Pelican History of Art** series supply most of the field-standard single-volume references, though some key entries (Islamic, African, Japanese) are 15–30 years old.
3. The learner's Frankfurt base is genuinely advantageous: the Städel (European painting Old Masters→contemporary), Liebieghaus (sculpture across cultures), and the Weltkulturen Museum (non-European/ethnographic) plus fast rail to the Louvre, British Museum, Berlin's museum island, Rijksmuseum, Prado, and Vienna give first-hand access to nearly every tradition in scope.
4. The historiography/methods literature is compact and stable — five or six textbooks/anthologies cover the entire terrain and should be studied in parallel with the chronology, not after it.

## Details

### 1. Survey / Spine Texts (global)

**Kleiner, Fred S. *Gardner's Art Through the Ages: A Global History*. 16th ed. Boston: Cengage Learning, 2020.** Print ISBN 9781337630702. Also split into Volume I and Volume II, and available as a MindTap e-book with zoomable images. ~1,224 pp. in the single-volume 15th-ed. print. First published in 1926 by Helen Gardner (Harcourt Brace); per the publisher it has "sold more than two million copies since it was first published in 1926." Strong on chronology, architecture, and canonical works. Kleiner (Ph.D. Columbia; Boston University) is a Greek/Roman specialist and coordinates ~45 contributing specialists. There is also a *Concise History* and a separately packaged *Western Perspective* volume — the learner wants the **Global History**. Strength: comprehensive, image-rich, reliable chronological backbone. Weakness/critique: still organized on a largely Western periodization; non-Western chapters can feel like insertions into a Western frame.

**Stokstad, Marilyn, and Michael W. Cothren. *Art History*. 7th ed. Hoboken, NJ: Pearson, 2024 (©2025).** eTextbook ISBN 9780138118556; also Volume 1 (ISBN 9780138184735, 2025 update) and Volume 2. 6th ed. (2018, print ISBN 9780135570661) is still widely used. The 7th edition adds examination of 220+ new works, revised chapter structure with more thorough Asia and Islamic coverage, and an updated contemporary chapter (Abramović, Sam Gilliam, and others). Widely regarded as the most "student-friendly, contextual and inclusive" survey; balances formal/iconographic analysis with contextual history. Marilyn Stokstad (1929–2016) was a medievalist and CAA president; Cothren (Swarthmore) revised the 4th–7th editions. Strength: inclusive tone, strong contextual framing, expanded global coverage. Weakness: less dense on connoisseurship than Gardner's.

**Honour, Hugh, and John Fleming. *The Visual Arts: A History*. Revised 7th ed. London: Laurence King, 2009/2013 (Michael Archer updated the contemporary sections).** ISBNs include 9781780671178 (Laurence King hardcover, 2013) and 9781529432664 (revised 7th ed. paperback). ~984–1,000 pp. Distinctive for integrating non-Western art *within* the chronological narrative rather than in separate regional silos, and for treating colonialism and indigenous art in-line — arguably the most genuinely "one-world" of the single-volume narratives, and the most elegantly written. Weakness: not updated as recently as Gardner's/Stokstad; fewer pedagogical scaffolds.

**Verdict:** Use **Kleiner (Gardner's) 16th** as the chronological backbone for its canonical coverage and scale; keep **Stokstad & Cothren 7th** as a second read for contextual/inclusive framing; consider **Honour & Fleming** as the "narrative" survey that best models integrated global storytelling. Gombrich's *The Story of Art* is a classic but explicitly Western and idiosyncratic — useful as prose modeling, not as a global spine.

### 2. Tradition-Specific Authoritative Texts

**Chinese art:** Clunas, Craig. *Art in China*. 2nd ed. Oxford History of Art. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2009 (ISBN 9780199217342). Thematic (art in the tomb, at court, in the temple, for the elite, in the marketplace) rather than strictly chronological; expanded modern/contemporary coverage. Widely judged the best up-to-date one-volume introduction; a "serious challenge to the conventional practice of art history." Michael Sullivan's *The Arts of China* (6th ed., University of California Press) remains a respected chronological alternative (confirm current edition at purchase).

**Japanese art:** Mason, Penelope E. (rev. Donald Dinwiddie). *History of Japanese Art*. 2nd ed. Upper Saddle River, NJ: Pearson Prentice Hall, 2005 (ISBN 0131176021). ~432 pp., 480 illustrations. Still described as "the only comprehensive survey of its kind in English"; the standard, though now two decades old.

**Indian/South Asian art:** Vidya Dehejia, *Indian Art* (Phaidon, 1997) is the standard accessible one-volume overview; Dehejia (Barbara Stoler Miller Professor Emerita, Columbia; Padma Bhushan 2012; Freer Medal 2023) is a foremost authority. Partha Mitter, *Indian Art* (Oxford History of Art) is the standard critical-historiographical companion (confirm current edition).

**Islamic art & architecture (the field-standard two-volume Yale/Pelican pair):** Ettinghausen, Richard, Oleg Grabar, and Marilyn Jenkins-Madina. *Islamic Art and Architecture, 650–1250*. 2nd ed. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2001. And Blair, Sheila S., and Jonathan M. Bloom. *The Art and Architecture of Islam, 1250–1800*. New Haven: Yale University Press, 1994 (ISBN 9780300064650). Note a scholarly critique: the CAA reviewer observed that by 1994 "Pelican standards had changed markedly, and in comparison Grabar and Ettinghausen's book seemed to lack vigor," and that the Pelican division awkwardly assigns the South Asian sultanate period to the later volume. Robert Hillenbrand, *Islamic Art and Architecture* (Thames & Hudson World of Art) and *Islamic Architecture* are standard companions; Barbara Brend, *Islamic Art* (British Museum Press) is a reliable shorter survey.

**African art:** Visonà, Monica Blackmun, Robin Poynor, Herbert M. Cole, with Suzanne Preston Blier (intro) and Rowland Abiodun. *A History of Art in Africa*. 2nd ed. Upper Saddle River, NJ: Pearson/Prentice Hall, 2008 (ISBN 9780136128724). The only comprehensive art-historical survey of the continent including contemporary art and the Diaspora; adds a chapter on African artists abroad. Note: Visonà now holds copyright and has posted an adapted full-text version through the University of Kentucky (uknowledge.uky.edu), and is preparing a **3rd edition** with University of Michigan Publishing (online + print). Suzanne Preston Blier, *The History of African Art* (Thames & Hudson Art Essentials, ISBN 9780500296257) is a newer short synthesis.

**Mesoamerican / Ancient Americas:** Miller, Mary Ellen. *The Art of Mesoamerica: From Olmec to Aztec*. 6th ed. London: Thames & Hudson (World of Art), 2019 (ISBN 9780500204504; e-book 9780500775035). 50 new color images and updates from recent Maya/Teotihuacan/Tenochtitlan excavations. Miller (former Dean of Yale College; Getty Research Institute director) is a leading Maya scholar. For the Andes and broader Pre-Columbian scope: Esther Pasztory, *Pre-Columbian Art* (Cambridge/Everyman Art Library); Rebecca Stone, *Art of the Andes: From Chavín to Inca* (Thames & Hudson World of Art).

**Native North American art:** Berlo, Janet Catherine, and Ruth B. Phillips. *Native North American Art*. 2nd ed. Oxford History of Art. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014 (ISBN 9780199947546; 1st ed. 0192842188). Covers 3,000 years, expanded 20th–21st-century coverage (James Luna, Kent Monkman, Nadia Myre, Jaune Quick-to-See Smith, Will Wilson) and issues of ethics, gender, representation, colonial encounter. The accepted standard.

**Oceanic / Aboriginal Australian art:** Wally Caruana, *Aboriginal Art* (Thames & Hudson World of Art) is the standard concise introduction. For Oceania broadly, Anne D'Alleva, *Arts of the Pacific Islands* (Yale/Pelican) and Nicholas Thomas, *Oceanic Art* (Thames & Hudson World of Art) are the standard references.

**Byzantine & early Christian:** Cormack, Robin. *Byzantine Art*. 2nd ed. Oxford History of Art. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2018 (ISBN 9780198778790). "The best single-volume introduction," tracing 1,500 years and now in a color edition with a new epilogue. Cormack is Professor Emeritus, Courtauld Institute.

**Greek & Roman:** Boardman, John. *Greek Art*. 5th ed. London: Thames & Hudson (World of Art), 2016 (ISBN 9780500204337) — now full color, the standard concise history. Nigel Spivey, *Greek Art* (Phaidon Art & Ideas, 1997, ISBN 9780714833682) is the thematic alternative. Mary Beard and John Henderson, *Classical Art: From Greece to Rome* (Oxford History of Art, 2001, ISBN 9780192842374) is a critical study of Rome's reception of Greek art (not a conventional survey). For Roman art: Kleiner, Fred S. *A History of Roman Art*. 2nd ed. Boston: Cengage, 2017 (ISBN 9781305885127); and Nancy H. and Andrew Ramage, *Roman Art: Romulus to Constantine*, 5th ed. (Pearson, 2008, ISBN 9780136000976).

**Renaissance & Baroque:** Hartt, Frederick, and David G. Wilkins. *History of Italian Renaissance Art: Painting, Sculpture, Architecture*. 7th ed. Pearson/Prentice Hall, 2010/2011 (ISBN 9780205705818). Still a standard survey but retains Hartt's traditional framework and is aging. Wittkower, Rudolf. *Art and Architecture in Italy 1600–1750*. Revised 6th ed., 3 vols. (rev. Joseph Connors and Jennifer Montagu). New Haven: Yale University Press, 1999: Vol. 1 *Early Baroque* (ISBN 9780300079395), Vol. 2 *High Baroque* (9780300079401), Vol. 3 *Late Baroque* (9780300079418). Remains the classic standard for Italian Baroque. John Shearman, *Mannerism* (Penguin Style and Civilization, 1967; reprints ISBN 9780140137590) is the classic concise interpretive essay (now dated). Michael Levey's Penguin *Early Renaissance* (1967) and *High Renaissance* (1975) are respected but dated (choose a specific title before citing).

**Modern & Contemporary (global):** Foster, Hal, Rosalind Krauss, Yve-Alain Bois, Benjamin H. D. Buchloh, and David Joselit. *Art Since 1900: Modernism, Antimodernism, Postmodernism*. 3rd ed. London: Thames & Hudson, 2016. Two volumes: Vol. 1 *1900 to 1944* (ISBN 9780500292716), Vol. 2 *1945 to the Present* (ISBN 9780500292723); combined ISBN 9780500293287. Year-by-year structure with 125+ essays; four/five methodological introductions (Foster on psychoanalysis, Buchloh on the social history of art, Bois on formalism/structuralism, Krauss on poststructuralism). The 3rd edition adds Moscow conceptualism, abstract film, postmodern architecture, queer art, and artists from emerging economies. Note: it is theory-forward and October-affiliated — critics observe it "trains our focus on the theoretical framing of art history rather than its facts or familiar monuments." Charles Harrison & Paul Wood, *Art in Theory 1900–2000* is the standard primary-source anthology of modern art writing.

### 3. Visual Analysis / "How to Look"

- **Barnet, Sylvan. *A Short Guide to Writing About Art*. 11th ed. Pearson, 2015 (ISBN 9780205886999).** The best-selling student guide to formal analysis and art writing; the 11th edition adds a chapter on writing text panels for virtual exhibitions.
- **D'Alleva, Anne. *Look! The Fundamentals of Art History* (a.k.a. *Look! Art History Fundamentals*). 3rd ed. Prentice Hall/Pearson, 2010 (ISBN 9780205768714).** Companion "how to look" primer.
- **Pointon, Marcia. *History of Art: A Student's Handbook*. 5th ed. London: Routledge, 2014 (ISBN 9780415639248).** Standard introductory handbook.
- **Wölfflin, Heinrich. *Principles of Art History: The Problem of the Development of Style in Early Modern Art*.** The foundational formalism text (linear vs painterly, etc.); the Getty Research Institute published a centennial translation (Jonathan Blower, 2015). The classic Dover translation (Hottinger) remains in print.
- **Panofsky, Erwin. *Studies in Iconology* (1939) and *Meaning in the Visual Arts* (1955; Penguin/Anchor).** Foundational iconology/iconography.
- **Baxandall, Michael. *Painting and Experience in Fifteenth-Century Italy: A Primer in the Social History of Pictorial Style*. 2nd ed. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1988 (ISBN 019282144X).** Introduces the "period eye." And *Patterns of Intention* (Yale, 1985) for the logic of pictorial explanation. Note a scholarly nuance: Baxandall is often mislabeled a "social historian of art"; he emphasized style, and some argue he was "wrongly interpreted as advocating a social history of art."
- **James Elkins** — e.g. *Our Beautiful, Dry, and Distant Texts*, *Visual Studies: A Skeptical Introduction*, and *How to Use Your Eyes* — useful for critical reflection on looking.
- **Practical slow-looking:** Jennifer L. Roberts, "The Power of Patience: Teaching Students the Value of Deceleration and Immersive Attention," *Harvard Magazine* (Nov–Dec 2013) — her signature assignment has students spend three full hours before a single work. Amy E. Herman, *Visual Intelligence* (2016). Peter Clothier, *Slow Looking* / "One Hour/One Painting." Shari Tishman, *Slow Looking: The Art and Practice of Learning Through Observation* (Routledge, 2018). Slow Art Day (global annual event) as a structuring device.

### 4. Theory, Method & Historiography

- **D'Alleva, Anne. *Methods & Theories of Art History*. 2nd ed. London: Laurence King, 2012 (ISBN 9781856698993).** The most accessible entry; applies each approach to sample works and includes writing guidance. Often paired with Hatt & Klonk.
- **Hatt, Michael, and Charlotte Klonk. *Art History: A Critical Introduction to Its Methods*.** 1st ed. Manchester University Press, 2006 (ISBN 9780719069598); **2nd ed. 2022 (ISBN 9781526154767), with new contributors (Dorothy Price and others)** updating for the global/decolonial turn. Approaches from Hegel to post-colonialism; the more "meaty" methods text.
- **Preziosi, Donald, ed. *The Art of Art History: A Critical Anthology*. New ed. Oxford History of Art. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2009 (ISBN 9780192842428).** 30+ primary readings (Winckelmann, Kant, Wölfflin, Warburg, Panofsky, Heidegger, Schapiro, Derrida, Krauss, Foucault, etc.) organized by debate/theme. The standard graduate-level anthology.
- **Fernie, Eric. *Art History and Its Methods: A Critical Anthology*. London: Phaidon, 1995.** Classic anthology with glossary of key thinkers.
- **Minor, Vernon Hyde. *Art History's History*. 2nd ed. Pearson, 2001.** Accessible historiography.
- **Laurie Schneider Adams, *The Methodologies of Art: An Introduction* (2nd ed., Westview, 2010)** — useful, if selective (stronger on semiotics/psychoanalysis, weaker on 19th/early-20th-c. approaches).
- Recent: *The Routledge Companion to Decolonizing Art History* (2021, ISBN 9780367714819) for the current decolonial methods debate.

### 6. Museum-Looking Pedagogy & European Collection Mapping

**Methods:** (a) *Slow looking* — Roberts's 3-hour immersion, Clothier's one-hour/one-painting, Slow Art Day; (b) *comparative looking* — deliberately juxtapose two works (e.g., a Song landscape vs a Dutch landscape) to train the "period eye" (Baxandall); (c) *sketching-to-see* — drawing to force sustained attention; (d) formal-analysis worksheets from Barnet/D'Alleva applied on-site. The learner should pre-read the relevant Städel Digitorial or Heilbrunn essay, then do a structured looking session, then write a formal analysis.

**Collections reachable from Frankfurt, by strength:**
- **Frankfurt (home base):** Städel Museum — European painting from the medieval period through Old Masters (Botticelli's *Idealised Portrait of a Lady*, Vermeer's *The Geographer*, Rembrandt) to modern/contemporary. Liebieghaus Skulpturensammlung — sculpture spanning ancient Egypt, Greece/Rome, medieval, Renaissance, Baroque, and East Asian. **Weltkulturen Museum** — non-European/ethnographic collections (Oceania, Africa, Americas, Southeast Asia), essential for the traditions least represented in fine-art museums. The Schirn Kunsthalle for temporary modern/contemporary shows; the Museum für Angewandte Kunst (applied art, incl. East Asian).
- **Paris (Louvre; Musée d'Orsay; Musée Guimet; Musée du quai Branly):** Louvre — antiquities (Near East, Egypt, Greece/Rome), European painting/sculpture, and a major **Islamic art** department. Orsay — 19th-c./Impressionism. Guimet — the premier European collection of **Asian art**. Quai Branly — **African, Oceanic, Asian, and American** indigenous art.
- **London (British Museum; National Gallery; V&A; Tate):** British Museum — global antiquities (Egypt, Assyria, Greece [Parthenon marbles], plus major Asian, African, and Americas holdings). National Gallery — European painting 13th–19th c. V&A — decorative arts, South Asian, Islamic, and East Asian. Tate — British and modern/contemporary.
- **Berlin (Museumsinsel + Humboldt Forum):** Pergamonmuseum — Near Eastern (Ishtar Gate), Greek/Roman (Pergamon Altar), and Islamic art. Neues Museum — Egyptian (Nefertiti). The **Museum für Asiatische Kunst** and **Ethnologisches Museum** (in the Humboldt Forum) — East Asian, South Asian, African, Oceanic, and Americas material.
- **Amsterdam (Rijksmuseum):** Dutch Golden Age and European decorative arts; also Asian art (Asian Pavilion).
- **Vienna (Kunsthistorisches Museum):** Bruegel, Old Masters, Egyptian/Near Eastern and classical antiquities.
- **Madrid (Prado):** Spanish and European painting (Velázquez, Goya, El Greco, Bosch).
- **Florence (Uffizi):** Italian Renaissance painting.

### 7. Major Scholarly Debates for Calibration

- **Eurocentrism & decolonizing the canon:** *Broadly settled* — that the traditional canon and the discipline's methods were formed within a Western, colonial framework, and that this shaped what counted as "art." *Contested* — how to remedy it (add more artists vs rebuild the interpretive tools). *The Routledge Companion to Decolonizing Art History* (2021) argues the discipline is "not only a product of colonial legacies but an active agent in the consolidation of empire."
- **"Global art history" vs "world art studies":** *Contested/methodological.* John Onians distinguishes "world art studies" (long historical span, wide geography, interdisciplinary/neuroscientific methods) from "global art history" (the networked contemporary world through a traditional lens). Critics (e.g., Anthony Gardner) warn the "global" turn's "sense of festival" has "trumped" the sharper critique of the "postcolonial." See the *ARTMargins* special issue "Art History, Postcolonialism, and the Global Turn" (2023) and the *Perspective* (2014) roundtable with Zainab Bahrani, Jaś Elsner, Rosemary Joyce, and Wu Hung.
- **Can "art" apply to ritual/functional non-Western objects?** *Genuinely contested.* Displaying masks, reliquaries, and ritual objects as "art" can strip function and meaning; the term "art" itself carries a post-Renaissance Western genealogy. This is a core reason the Weltkulturen/ethnographic vs fine-art museum distinction matters.
- **Connoisseurship & attribution:** *Mixed.* Connoisseurship (Morelli, Berenson) is no longer the discipline's center of gravity but remains indispensable for attribution, now increasingly combined with technical/scientific analysis; high-stakes attribution disputes (e.g., "Salvator Mundi") remain live.
- **Conservation/restoration ethics — the Sistine Chapel cleaning (1980–1994):** A touchstone case. Directed by Vatican chief conservator Gianluigi Colalucci using the AB 57 gel (ammonium/sodium bicarbonate in a seaweed-based thickener), the cleaning removed soot, animal glue and earlier restorers' varnishes and revealed vivid color. Critics — led by Columbia's James Beck (founder of ArtWatch) and Michael Daley (*Art Restoration: The Culture, the Business and the Scandal*, 1993) — argued it also stripped Michelangelo's own *a secco* shadow-modeling, "flattening" the figures; a 1980s petition to the Pope was signed by artists including Motherwell, Rauschenberg, and Warhol. *Current consensus* largely vindicates the restoration (the dark tone was dirt/later varnish, not Michelangelo's intent), while treating the episode as the origin of today's insistence on pre-cleaning technical study and the rejection of "total cleaning." ArtWatch remains the partisan dissenting voice; Colalucci's own account appears in *Conservation Science in Cultural Heritage* vol. 16 (2017).
- **Formalism vs the social history of art:** *Settled as a pluralism* — the discipline now holds multiple methods simultaneously rather than crowning one. Formalism (Wölfflin, Greenberg) and the social history of art (T. J. Clark, Baxandall's contextualism) are both taught as tools; *Art Since 1900* literally opens with competing method essays.
- **The status of iconology after Panofsky:** *Contested/qualified.* Panofsky's three-level iconology remains foundational teaching but has been critiqued as over-textualizing images, insufficiently attentive to materiality, reception, and non-Western contexts; semiotics (Bal/Bryson) and visual-culture studies partly supplanted it.
- **Feminist & postcolonial reinterpretation:** *Broadly accepted as legitimate and central* since Linda Nochlin's "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?" (1971); *contested* mainly in emphasis and application, not in validity.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Toolkit (before chronology, ~4–6 weeks):** Read D'Alleva, *Look!* and Barnet's *Short Guide* for how-to-look/write; skim Pointon's *Handbook*. Do three slow-looking sessions at the Städel using the Roberts 3-hour method on one painting each, writing a formal analysis. Set up free accounts: Smarthistory, Heilbrunn, JSTOR (register-and-read), Städel Digital Collection.

**Stage 1 — Chronological spine (the backbone, priority #1):** Work through Kleiner's *Gardner's Global History* 16th cover-to-cover, cross-reading the parallel Stokstad chapter and the matching Smarthistory/Heilbrunn essays. Tie each major period to an in-person visit (see mapping above).

**Stage 2 — Deep dives (layer specialist volumes):** For each tradition, read the field-standard volume (Clunas, Mason, Dehejia, Ettinghausen–Grabar + Blair–Bloom, Visonà et al., Miller, Berlo–Phillips, Cormack, Boardman, Hartt, Wittkower, *Art Since 1900*). Prefer a research trip that matches each (Guimet/Berlin for East Asian; Louvre/Pergamon for Islamic and Near Eastern; British Museum/quai Branly for African/Oceanic/Americas).

**Stage 3 — Methods & historiography (priority #3, run in parallel from Stage 1):** D'Alleva's *Methods & Theories* → Hatt & Klonk 2nd ed. → Preziosi and Fernie anthologies. Add Wölfflin, Panofsky, and Baxandall as primary readings when you reach the relevant periods.

**Stage 4 — Calibration:** For each debate in §7, read one representative text from each side and write a short position note tagging "settled vs contested."

**Benchmarks that change the plan:** If a specialist volume is >20 years old (Mason 2005; Blair & Bloom 1994; Visonà 2008), supplement with recent journal articles via JSTOR/Grove and watch for new editions (Visonà's 3rd is forthcoming from U. Michigan). If you find the survey's non-Western chapters thin, shift weight toward the Oxford/Yale specialist volumes and the Heilbrunn essays sooner.

## Caveats
- **Editions move.** Textbook publishers (Pearson, Cengage) issue frequent "updates"; confirm the ISBN at purchase. Gardner's 16th (2020) and Stokstad 7th (2024/25) are current as of this report.
- **Some standards are aging.** Mason (2005), Blair & Bloom (1994), Ettinghausen–Grabar (2001 rev.), Hartt (2010), Shearman (1967), and Wittkower (1999) are the accepted references but predate the "global turn"; read them for coverage, not for current methodology.
- **Prices/access vary by region.** From Germany, Yale/Pelican and Oxford History of Art titles are readily available; the Städel and other EU museums are the learner's cheapest high-quality "labs."
- **A few items could not be verified to a single current ISBN** within research limits (specific Sullivan, Mitter, Pasztory, Stone, Caruana, Hillenbrand, Harrison & Wood, and Dehejia *Indian Art* editions); confirm these individually. "Michael Levey" needs a specific title chosen before citing.
- **Partisan sources flagged:** ArtWatch (Sistine Chapel) is a dissenting advocacy voice, not neutral consensus; present it as such.
- **Yale correction:** the OYC art-history offering is Diana E. E. Kleiner's *Roman Architecture*; there is no general intro-to-art-history or Vincent Scully course on Open Yale Courses.
