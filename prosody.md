# A Curriculum in Prosody & Versification

*Balanced method (analysis + performance + composition). English accentual-syllabic verse (Chaucer → present) and classical quantitative metre (Greek & Latin). Traditional literary prosody throughout — foot-scansion and beat theory, not generative/formal metrics.*

---

## Preface

### What this subject is, and where it sits

Prosody is the study of the sound-patterning of verse: metre (the abstract rhythmic template), rhythm (what an actual line does against that template), and the larger architecture of rhyme, line, and stanza. It is the third leg of the table whose other two legs you already have under construction — it stands to *poetry* roughly as your rhetoric work stands to *oratory* and your logic work to *argument*. Where rhetoric gave you the figures and the canons of delivery (*pronuntiatio*, *numerus*), and your accent/voice work gave you the phonetics of stress and intonation, prosody supplies the missing middle term: the formal grammar of verse rhythm itself. Several terms you have already met from other directions — stress, the line, prose rhythm (the *clausula*), pitch and pace — converge here and acquire a system.

This is a *balanced* curriculum: each phase asks you to **analyse** (scan and explicate), **perform** (read aloud with the metre live in the voice), and **compose** (write in the form under study). The three are not separable in practice. You do not really understand the iambic line until you can hear where a competent reader will let the stresses fall, and you do not hear that reliably until you have tried to write one and felt the constraint from the inside. This tripartite method is the historical norm: the classical and Renaissance schoolroom never separated *reading* verse from *speaking* and *making* it.

### The one methodological caveat you must hold from day one

English prosody has a genuine, unresolved methodological split, and your two anchor authors sit on opposite sides of it. **Hold both; do not pick early.**

- **The foot model** (Fussell, Steele, the whole inherited tradition) describes the English line as a sequence of *feet* — iamb, trochee, anapest, dactyl, spondee, pyrrhic — borrowed from classical metrics, with "substitutions" accounting for variation. It is the *lingua franca*: every editor, footnote, and examiner uses it, so you must be fluent in it.
- **Beat theory** (Attridge) argues that importing classical *feet* onto English is a category error, because English rhythm is organised by **beats and offbeats** (strong and weak positions in a felt pulse), not by counting quantities the way Greek did. On this view "iambic pentameter" is better described as a five-beat line, and much foot-scansion is an artefact of the notation rather than a fact about the rhythm.

The honest scholarly position is that the foot model is a serviceable *fiction* — useful, ubiquitous, and misleading if reified. This curriculum teaches the foot model first because you cannot read the literature without it, then teaches Attridge's critique as the corrective. Wherever you see confident claims that a line "is" iambic, mentally append: *under the foot convention*.

### The three tracks

Run them in parallel within each phase, never sequentially:

1. **Analysis** — reading the theory; scanning real poems; explicating how sound makes meaning.
2. **Performance** — reading verse aloud, and listening to skilled readers. The metre lives in the voice or nowhere. (This track also quietly reinforces your suprasegmental/intelligibility work — thought-group chunking, controlled pace, pitch range are exactly the levers a good verse-reader uses.)
3. **Composition** — writing in the metre and form under study. Imitation first, then free composition. You write to *feel* the constraint, not to become a poet (though you may).

### Pedagogical principles

- **Scan with a pencil, always.** Mark stresses (´), weak syllables (˘), feet (|), caesuras (‖), and line-ends. Scansion is a motor skill; it does not transfer from reading about it.
- **Read everything aloud.** Silent prosody is a contradiction. At minimum, read every analysed poem aloud twice — once for sense, once for sound.
- **Editions matter.** For classical verse, prefer Loeb parallel-text editions (facing original + translation) — the same standard you already use for rhetoric. For English, a good complete-text anthology (Norton) is the working corpus.
- **Recursion over progression.** The metres and forms are not boxes to tick. You return to the iambic line at every phase with a finer ear. Treat each form as something you keep re-scanning and re-attempting.

### Editions & pronunciation note

Classical scansion is impossible without knowing syllable *quantity*, which depends on (reconstructed) classical pronunciation. Allen's *Vox Latina* and *Vox Graeca* are therefore not optional ornaments but working tools for Phase 5 — you cannot scan a hexameter if you do not know which vowels are long. Acquire them with that phase, not before.

## Permanent Reference Library

Acquire these first; they stay on the desk for the whole curriculum.

- **Paul Fussell, *Poetic Meter and Poetic Form*, rev. ed. (McGraw-Hill, 1979).** The classic accessible English handbook. Your foot-model anchor. Lucid, opinionated, occasionally overstated (see Evidence note).
- **Derek Attridge, *Poetic Rhythm: An Introduction* (Cambridge UP, 1995).** The teaching version of beat theory, with graded exercises. Your beat-theory anchor. Work the exercises — this is the most *trainable* book on the list.
- **John Hollander, *Rhyme's Reason: A Guide to English Verse*, latest ed. (Yale UP).** A jewel: every form is demonstrated in a poem written *in* that form and *about* it. Read it cover to cover early, then keep it for reference. Doubles as a composition model.
- **Roland Greene et al. (eds.), *The Princeton Encyclopedia of Poetry and Poetics*, 4th ed. (Princeton UP, 2012).** The reference of record. Look up every unfamiliar term as you meet it (the metrics entries are largely by T.V.F. Brogan, the authority). This is your Lanham's *Handlist* equivalent for prosody.
- **Chris Baldick, *The Oxford Dictionary of Literary Terms* (Oxford UP).** Cheap, portable quick-reference for when the Princeton is too much.

## Phase 0 — Foundations: the ear, stress, and scansion

**Goal.** Acquire the vocabulary, the notation, and — most importantly — the trained ear for English lexical and phrasal stress, on which everything else rests. Be able to mark stress in ordinary prose and simple verse reliably.

**Prerequisites.** None, beyond literacy. Your phonetics/intelligibility work is a real head start here: you already know what lexical stress, sentence stress, and reduction *are*. This phase reframes that linguistic knowledge as metrical equipment.

**Primary readings.**
- Fussell, ch. 1–2 (the nature of metre; the concept of the foot).
- Attridge, *Poetic Rhythm*, ch. 1–3 (rhythm, stress, the beat).
- Princeton Encyclopedia entries: "Scansion," "Stress," "Accent," "Meter," "Foot."

**Supplementary.**
- Attridge & Thomas Carper, *Meter and Meaning: An Introduction to Rhythm in Poetry* (Routledge, 2003) — gentlest possible on-ramp, many worked examples; good if Fussell ch. 1 feels steep.

**Primary corpus.** Short, metrically regular lyrics for first scansion: a Shakespeare sonnet (e.g. 18 or 73), a Housman lyric, a Frost ("Stopping by Woods"). Nothing irregular yet.

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Mark stress in 10 sentences of your own prose, then in 10 lines of regular verse. Learn the four-symbol notation (´ ˘ | ‖) and use it on every poem hereafter.
- *Performance*: Read the corpus aloud; record yourself; listen for where you naturally stress. Compare against your scansion — discrepancies are the lesson.
- *Composition*: Write 10 single lines of strict iambic pentameter on any subject. Aim only for correct count and alternation; ignore meaning.

**Minimum viable path.** Fussell ch. 1–2 + Attridge ch. 1–3; the notation; the stress-marking exercise.

**Time estimate.** ~2–3 weeks.

## Phase 1 — The English foot and the metres

**Goal.** Master the accentual-syllabic system: the named feet, the named line-lengths, and the four metrical bases (iambic, trochaic, anapestic, dactylic). Centre of gravity: the iambic line, the workhorse of English verse.

**Prerequisites.** Phase 0 (stress, notation).

**Primary readings.**
- Fussell, ch. 3 (metrical variations) — but read it *alongside* Attridge's critique, below.
- Timothy Steele, *All the Fun's in How You Say a Thing: An Explanation of Meter and Versification* (Ohio UP, 1999), opening chapters. The best single treatment of metre-vs-rhythm by a practising metrist; superb on *why* the iamb dominates English.
- Hollander, *Rhyme's Reason*, the metre sections.

**Supplementary.**
- Stephen Adams, *Poetic Designs: An Introduction to Meters, Verse Forms, and Figures of Speech* (Broadview, 1997) — clear textbook treatment if you want a third voice.

**Primary corpus.** Iambic: Shakespeare's sonnets, Wordsworth. Trochaic: Blake "The Tyger," Longfellow *Hiawatha* (a cautionary case — relentless trochaic tetrameter). Anapestic: Byron "The Destruction of Sennacherib." Mixed/triple: limericks (anapestic, and good for the ear).

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Scan 20 lines in each of the four bases. Identify the metre of 10 unlabelled short poems blind.
- *Performance*: Read a trochaic and an anapestic poem aloud and feel how different their motion is from the iambic default; read "The Tyger" and notice the catalexis (the dropped final syllable).
- *Composition*: Write a quatrain in each of the four bases. Then write one limerick (this teaches anapestic feel and line-length contrast painlessly).

**Minimum viable path.** Fussell ch. 3 + Steele's metre chapters; scan the iambic and trochaic corpus; write one iambic and one anapestic quatrain.

**Time estimate.** ~4–5 weeks.

## Phase 2 — Metre vs rhythm: the line and its tensions

**Goal.** Internalise the single most important distinction in the subject — between the metrical *pattern* and the rhythmic *performance* — and the devices through which the line plays against its template: substitution, promotion and demotion of stress, caesura, enjambment, and end-stopping. This is where you stop counting and start *hearing*.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–1.

**Primary readings.**
- Attridge, *Poetic Rhythm*, the chapters on beats/offbeats and on the tension between metre and pronunciation — **the heart of the course**. This is where beat theory earns its place: it explains promotion/demotion (why a normally weak word can take a beat, and vice versa) more naturally than foot-substitution does.
- Attridge, *The Rhythms of English Poetry* (Longman, 1982) — the full theoretical statement; read selectively for depth. [Advanced]
- Fussell, ch. 4 (the line; structural principles) for the foot-model account of the same phenomena, so you can translate between the two vocabularies.

**Supplementary.**
- Princeton entries: "Caesura," "Enjambment," "Line," "Rhythm."

**Primary corpus.** Milton, *Paradise Lost* Book I (blank verse with heavy enjambment and mobile caesura — the supreme English example); Donne (violent rhythmic stress against metre); Pope (end-stopped, balanced — the opposite pole) for contrast.

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Take 20 lines of *Paradise Lost*. Scan the metre; then mark, separately, where a good reader's *rhythm* departs from it (promotions, demotions, where the caesura actually falls). The gap between the two markings is the lesson.
- *Performance*: Read the Milton passage aloud honouring the syntax across line-breaks (enjambment) — resist the sing-song end-stop. Then read Pope and let the couplets close. Hear the difference enjambment makes.
- *Composition*: Write 14 lines of blank verse with at least three enjambments and one mid-line caesura. Then rewrite four of them end-stopped and notice what you lose.

**Minimum viable path.** Attridge's beat/offbeat and metre-vs-pronunciation chapters; the *Paradise Lost* double-scansion exercise; the blank-verse composition.

**Time estimate.** ~4–5 weeks.

## Phase 3 — Rhyme, stanza, and the fixed forms

**Goal.** Command the architecture above the line: rhyme (types and notation), the major stanza forms, and the principal fixed forms. Be able to identify and write in each.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–2 (you must hear the line before you build stanzas of them).

**Primary readings.**
- Hollander, *Rhyme's Reason* — now read specifically for the form demonstrations (sonnet, terza rima, ottava rima, rhyme royal, Spenserian stanza, villanelle, sestina, etc.). Hollander is your single best guide here.
- Mark Strand & Eavan Boland, *The Making of a Poem: A Norton Anthology of Poetic Forms* (Norton, 2000) — each form paired with strong examples and a short essay. Your working anthology for this phase.
- Fussell, ch. 5–7 (rhyme; the stanza; the sonnet and other forms).

**Supplementary.**
- Lewis Turco, *The Book of Forms: A Handbook of Poetics*, 4th ed. (UP of New England, 2012) — the encyclopedic forms reference for writers; consult per-form.
- Phillis Levin (ed.), *The Penguin Book of the Sonnet* — for the sonnet deep-dive (Petrarchan vs Shakespearean vs Spenserian volta logic).
- Princeton entries: "Rhyme," "Stanza," "Sonnet," "Sestina," "Villanelle," "Refrain."

**Primary corpus.** Sonnets: Petrarch (in translation) vs Shakespeare vs Milton, to feel the structural difference. Terza rima: Dante (translation) and Shelley "Ode to the West Wind." Spenserian stanza: Spenser, *The Faerie Queene* (a stanza or two); Keats "The Eve of St. Agnes." Villanelle: Bishop "One Art," Thomas "Do Not Go Gentle." Sestina: Bishop "Sestina."

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Notate the rhyme scheme of 10 poems. For three sonnets (one each: Petrarchan, Shakespearean, Spenserian), locate the *volta* and explain how the rhyme structure stages the turn.
- *Performance*: Read a villanelle aloud and attend to how the repetition lands differently each return — performance reveals what scansion cannot.
- *Composition*: Write **one Shakespearean sonnet** (the central exercise of this phase — strict pentameter, correct rhyme, a real volta at line 9 or the couplet). Then attempt **one villanelle** (the constraint is brutal and instructive). Sestina is optional/stretch.

**Minimum viable path.** Hollander's form sections + Strand/Boland on the sonnet; rhyme-scheme notation; write one Shakespearean sonnet.

**Time estimate.** ~5–6 weeks.

## Phase 4 — The English line through its history

**Goal.** See the accentual-syllabic line *diachronically* — how it was made, loosened, tightened, and broken across six centuries. This converts the static system of Phases 1–3 into a historical understanding, and lets you hear period style.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–3.

**Primary readings.**
- George Saintsbury, *A History of English Prosody from the Twelfth Century to the Present Day*, 3 vols. (1906–10). The monumental diachronic survey. **Read selectively and critically** — Saintsbury's foot-scansion and many judgments are dated and idiosyncratic (see Evidence note); use him for sweep and for the primary-text map, not as final authority. [Free — public domain, archive.org]
- T.V.F. Brogan, *English Versification, 1570–1980: A Reference Guide* (Johns Hopkins UP, 1981) — the bibliography of record; use it to find the best study of any specific question.
- Attridge & Carper, *Meter and Meaning* — for clear modern readings of period examples.

**Supplementary.**
- For Chaucer's line specifically (and the vexed question of final -e and how to scan Middle English), the introduction to *The Riverside Chaucer* (ed. Benson) and Princeton "Chaucer, metre of."
- Gerard Manley Hopkins, the **Author's Preface** to his *Poems* — his statement of *sprung rhythm*. Pair with a good edition (Oxford or Penguin). Note: whether sprung rhythm is a genuinely distinct system or a description of accentual / loosened verse is debated (Evidence note).

**Primary corpus (a diachronic spine).** Chaucer (the decasyllabic line in the making) → Wyatt/Surrey (the line regularised; Surrey's invention of English blank verse) → Shakespeare/Milton (the blank-verse summit) → Dryden/Pope (the closed heroic couplet) → Wordsworth/Keats (Romantic loosening) → Tennyson/Browning (Victorian range) → Hopkins (sprung rhythm) → the threshold of free verse.

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Scan one representative passage from each period on the spine. Write a one-paragraph characterisation of each period's metrical "manner."
- *Performance*: Read a Pope couplet-passage and a Wordsworth blank-verse passage back to back; articulate, aloud and then in writing, how the period ear differs.
- *Composition*: Write a closed heroic couplet pair in the manner of Pope (balance, antithesis, end-stop). Then write four lines of loose iambic in the manner of late Wordsworth. The contrast is the exercise.

**Minimum viable path.** Saintsbury read *very* selectively for the spine + Attridge/Carper on three period examples; scan the diachronic spine passages; write the Pope couplet.

**Time estimate.** ~6–8 weeks.

## Phase 5 — Classical quantitative metre (Greek & Latin)

**Goal.** Acquire a genuinely *different* metrical system — one built on syllable **quantity** (length), not stress. Be able to scan the dactylic hexameter and the elegiac couplet confidently, recognise the principal lyric metres, and read classical verse aloud with correct quantities. This is the hardest phase: you are retraining your ear away from its native stress-based default.

**Prerequisites.** Phases 0–2 (the metre/rhythm distinction); the *concept* that metre need not be stress-based (Phase 0). No Greek or Latin language proficiency is assumed — you scan by quantity rules and pronunciation, and read meaning from facing translation. (Your existing comfort with formal rule-systems is the relevant aptitude here.)

**Why now and not earlier.** Quantity is counterintuitive for an English ear, which hears stress automatically and length not at all. Building the stress-based system first (Phases 1–4) gives you a secure home base from which the foreignness of quantity becomes legible by contrast, rather than confusing from the start.

**Primary readings.**
- **Latin (do first — more accessible entry):**
  - D.S. Raven, *Latin Metre: An Introduction* (Faber, 1965; reprints). The standard student introduction.
  - W. Sidney Allen, *Vox Latina: A Guide to the Pronunciation of Classical Latin*, 2nd ed. (Cambridge UP, 1978). Working tool: you cannot scan without knowing quantities and pronunciation.
- **Greek (second):**
  - M.L. West, *Introduction to Greek Metre* (Oxford UP, 1987). The accessible distillation of his major work.
  - W. Sidney Allen, *Vox Graeca: The Pronunciation of Classical Greek*, 3rd ed. (Cambridge UP, 1987).
  - D.S. Raven, *Greek Metre: An Introduction* (Faber, 1962) — companion to his Latin volume.

**Supplementary / reference.**
- M.L. West, *Greek Metre* (Oxford UP, 1982) — the comprehensive scholarly work; reference, not cover-to-cover. [Advanced]
- Paul Maas, *Greek Metre*, trans. Hugh Lloyd-Jones (Oxford UP, 1962) — terse classic; for the rigorous theory of metre and the rules of position/resolution. [Advanced]
- Princeton entries: "Hexameter," "Elegiac distich," "Sapphic," "Alcaic," "Quantity," "Classical prosody."

**Primary corpus (read in Loeb parallel editions).**
- Hexameter: Virgil, *Aeneid* I (Latin); Homer, *Iliad* I (Greek).
- Elegiac couplet: Ovid, *Amores* / *Tristia*; Catullus' elegiac poems.
- Lyric metres: Horace, *Odes* (Alcaic, Sapphic, Asclepiadic — the best single training ground for Latin lyric metre); Catullus 51 & 11 (Sapphic stanza, in Latin); Sappho (Greek, the Sapphic stanza at its source). Pindar — advanced, optional.
- Dramatic: the iambic trimeter of Greek tragedy and Roman comedy (recognition only at this stage).

**Exercises.**
- *Analysis*: Scan 20 lines of Virgilian hexameter (marking quantities, the principal caesura, elision/*synaloepha*). Then scan 10 elegiac couplets, attending to the fixed shape of the pentameter's second half. Then identify the metre of five Horatian odes.
- *Performance*: Read the Virgil passage aloud honouring *quantity* (long syllables genuinely longer), using Allen's restored pronunciation. This feels deeply alien at first — that is the point; persist until the hexameter's rhythm becomes audible as rhythm.
- *Composition*: [Optional / stretch] Attempt a few lines of Latin dactylic hexameter, or — far more practical — compose **English verse in imitation of classical quantitative metre** (English "hexameters" or Sapphics), which previews Phase 6's central problem. Longfellow's *Evangeline* (English dactylic hexameter) is the model to study.

**Minimum viable path.** Raven *Latin Metre* + Allen *Vox Latina*; scan Virgilian hexameter and the elegiac couplet; read one Virgil passage aloud by quantity. Defer Greek to a later pass if time is short (but do not delete it).

**Time estimate.** ~8–10 weeks (longest phase — two languages, a foreign system, and ear-retraining).

## Phase 6 — The interface, free verse, and capstone

**Goal.** Bring the two systems into contact and carry the story to the present. Three movements: (a) the historical English *attempt* to write quantitative metre and why it largely failed; (b) the modernist break into free verse and the question of whether free verse has a prosody at all; (c) a sustained capstone in each of the three tracks.

**Prerequisites.** All prior phases (this phase presupposes you can hear both stress-based and quantity-based metre).

**Primary readings.**

*(a) The quantitative experiment in English:*
- **Derek Attridge, *Well-weighed Syllables: Elizabethan Verse in Classical Metres* (Cambridge UP, 1974).** The definitive study of exactly this episode — your beat-theory anchor turns historian. Excellent.
- Thomas Campion, *Observations in the Art of English Poesie* (1602) — the case *for* quantitative, *against* rhyme. [Free — public domain]
- Samuel Daniel, *A Defence of Ryme* (1603) — the rejoinder that won the argument. [Free — public domain]
- The Spenser–Harvey correspondence (extracts) on English quantitative verse. [Free — public domain]

*(b) Free verse and modern prosody:*
- **Charles O. Hartman, *Free Verse: An Essay on Prosody* (Princeton UP, 1980).** The serious account of how free verse is *organised* (by lineation working against the absence of metre) — argues, against the lazy view, that free verse has a prosody. Whether it does is genuinely debated (Evidence note).
- T.S. Eliot, "Reflections on Vers Libre" (1917) and "The Music of Poetry" (1942). [First essay free — public domain]
- Ezra Pound, "A Retrospect" / "A Few Don'ts by an Imagiste" (1918) — "compose in the sequence of the musical phrase, not the metronome." [Free — public domain]

**Supplementary.**
- Princeton entries: "Free verse," "Vers libre," "Sprung rhythm," "Quantity (English)."

**Primary corpus.** Sidney and Campion's own quantitative attempts (to hear *why* they fail against the English stress-ear); Longfellow *Evangeline* (the most sustained English "hexameter"); then free verse: Whitman (the long biblical/anaphoric line), Eliot *The Waste Land*, Pound *Cantos* (a little), W.C. Williams (the short-line, "variable foot" claim — itself contested).

**Exercises (capstone).**
- *Analysis*: Write a 1,500–2,000-word explication of one metrically complex poem of your choice, integrating foot-scansion, Attridge's beat analysis, and (where relevant) the metre/rhythm and form material — explicitly noting where the two analytic vocabularies agree and disagree.
- *Performance*: Prepare and record a confident reading of one substantial passage (≥ 20 lines) — ideally something rhythmically demanding (late Shakespeare, Hopkins, or a Horatian ode by quantity). This is the synthesis of the whole performance track.
- *Composition*: Complete a small portfolio — at minimum: one Shakespearean sonnet (revised from Phase 3), one passage of enjambed blank verse, and one poem in either a fixed form (villanelle/sestina) or in deliberate free verse with a defensible lineation logic. Write a one-page note on the prosodic choices in each.

**Minimum viable path.** Attridge *Well-weighed Syllables* + Hartman *Free Verse* + the Campion/Daniel pair; the analysis capstone and the performance recording.

**Time estimate.** ~6–8 weeks.

| 5 Classical quantitative | 0–2 (concept), helped by 4 | the *other* system (quantity) |

Phases 1→2→3 are a strict spine. Phase 4 (history) and Phase 5 (classical) can be **swapped or interleaved** — both depend mainly on 0–2. If you want the classical roots earlier for historical logic, move Phase 5 ahead of Phase 4; the only cost is tackling quantity before your stress-ear is fully secure.

## Minimum viable curriculum (the ~80% path)

If time is constrained, this skeleton covers most of the conceptual yield in roughly half the hours:

1. **Foundations** — Fussell ch. 1–2 + Attridge ch. 1–3; the notation; stress-marking.
2. **The iambic line + metre/rhythm** — Steele's metre chapters + Attridge's beat/offbeat chapters; the *Paradise Lost* double-scansion; write one sonnet.
3. **Forms** — Hollander's form sections; rhyme-scheme notation; one Shakespearean sonnet (counts for both).
4. **Classical, Latin only** — Raven *Latin Metre* + Allen *Vox Latina*; scan hexameter and the elegiac couplet; read Virgil aloud by quantity.
5. **The break** — Hartman *Free Verse* + the Campion/Daniel pair; the analysis capstone.

This omits the full diachronic history (Phase 4) and Greek metre — both worth returning to, neither deleted.

## Evidence quality — what is settled, contested, and overstated

Per your usual preference, an explicit calibration of the claims you will meet:

- **Foot-scansion vs beat theory** *(genuinely contested; central).* The foot model is universal in practice but is, on the best modern view (Attridge), a classical import that misdescribes a stress-language. Treat foot-scansion as a useful notation, not a fact about English rhythm. Learn both; trust neither uncritically.
- **"Iambic pentameter mirrors the natural rhythm of English / the heartbeat"** *(overstated trope).* A popular claim with a grain of truth (the iamb suits English's predominantly rising rhythm and many disyllables) wrapped in a lot of romantic exaggeration. The heartbeat analogy is decorative, not explanatory. Be sceptical when you meet it.
- **English quantitative metre** *(effectively settled).* The Elizabethan attempt to write genuine quantity-based verse in English largely failed because English organises rhythm by stress, not length; Attridge's *Well-weighed Syllables* is the authority. English "hexameters" (e.g. *Evangeline*) are accentual imitations of a quantitative shape, not true quantitative verse.
- **Sprung rhythm** *(contested).* Whether Hopkins described a genuinely distinct metrical system or simply re-badged accentual / strongly-stressed verse is an open question. Read his Preface, but hold his claims at arm's length.
- **Free verse has a "prosody"** *(contested).* Hartman argues lineation itself constitutes a prosody in the absence of metre; sceptics hold that "free-verse prosody" stretches the term past usefulness. A real, live disagreement — not a settled doctrine.
- **Saintsbury's *History*** *(dated authority).* Indispensable for sweep and primary-text mapping, unreliable in detail: his foot-based scansions and aesthetic verdicts reflect 1900s assumptions now superseded. Use as a map, not a judge.
- **Reconstructed classical pronunciation** (Allen, *Vox Latina/Graeca*) *(strong scholarly consensus, with acknowledged uncertainty).* The reconstructions are well-evidenced and standard, though some fine points (vowel qualities, pitch accent realisation in Greek) remain debated. Solid enough to scan and read by.

## Beyond this curriculum

Natural continuations, none required, each building on this base:

- **Formal / generative metrics** — the layer you set aside for now. If you ever want metre treated as a *formal grammar* (which would sit naturally beside your logic and formal-systems interests), the route is: Halle & Keyser's early generative metrics → Kiparsky and Hanson–Kiparsky's parametric theory → Bruce Hayes' work → **Nigel Fabb & Morris Halle, *Meter in Poetry: A New Theory* (Cambridge UP, 2008)** for the bracketed-grid account. This reframes everything here in explicitly rule-based, almost compiler-like terms.
- **Comparative metrics for your languages** — Russian verse (the Russian syllabo-tonic tradition is exceptionally regular and well-theorised — Nabokov's *Notes on Prosody* and the Russian Formalists are the entry) and Spanish versification (the syllable-counting Romance system, with its own rules of synalepha and accent) would both extend the comparative frame and feed directly into your reading there.
- **The prosody of song** — the interface between textual stress and musical stress (text-setting, word-music) bridges directly to the music-theory and vocal-pedagogy gap noted earlier. This is the obvious connective tissue between this curriculum and a future music curriculum.
