# Optimizing English for International Audiences

*A research synthesis on intelligibility, social perception, and warmth-first priorities — for an L1 Polish speaker in international professional contexts (UK/Ireland secondary).*

---

## 1. The Goal, Restated

Your prioritization is unusual and worth flagging because it's *correct* in a way most L2 learners get wrong:

> Warmth > Competence > Education > Native-likeness

The L2 perception research actively supports this ordering. The dominant finding across three decades of work (Munro, Derwing, Levis, Jenkins, and others) is that **intelligibility and comprehensibility — not native-likeness — drive almost all the perceptual gains worth chasing**. Going from "comprehensible accented" to "near-native" yields diminishing returns: enormous training cost, marginal perceptual benefit. Worse, in international and lingua franca contexts, a strongly native-target accent (especially RP-leaning British) can *reduce* warmth ratings, registering as distant, formal, or affected rather than approachable.

So the actual operational goal is:

**Be effortlessly understood by international English speakers (native and non-native), at a natural conversational pace, with prosodic patterns that signal openness and engagement rather than effort or formality.**

Native-likeness is not the target. Effortlessness from the listener's side is the target.

## 2. How Accent Drives Social Perception in English

### The three-dimension framework (Munro & Derwing, 1995)

Three perceptual properties of L2 speech are partially independent and need to be optimized separately:

- **Intelligibility**: how much of the message is actually understood (objective; transcription-based).
- **Comprehensibility**: how much listener effort is required (subjective; ease-of-processing).
- **Accentedness**: how different the speech sounds from listener expectations.

A speaker can be heavily accented yet fully intelligible; another can be only mildly accented yet hard to comprehend due to prosodic mismatch. **Comprehensibility predicts social judgments far better than accentedness does**. Listeners forgive accent when processing is easy; they downgrade speakers (on competence, credibility, *and* warmth) when processing is hard, regardless of how "native" the speech sounds.

### The processing fluency mechanism

Lev-Ari and Keysar (2010) demonstrated that disfluent speech is judged less truthful — listeners misattribute their own processing effort to the speaker's content. The mechanism is general: hard-to-process speech feels less reliable, less intelligent, less competent, and yes, less warm. Comprehensibility gains transfer across all four of your priority dimensions simultaneously, which is why optimizing for it is high-leverage.

### Status–solidarity trade-off

Sociolinguistic matched-guise studies consistently find a trade-off: accents rated highest on **status** dimensions (competence, education, intelligence) are typically rated lower on **solidarity** dimensions (warmth, trustworthiness, likability). RP exemplifies this — high competence ratings, distinctly lower warmth ratings in many UK studies (Coupland & Bishop 2007 and follow-ups). Standard American shows similar patterns in US contexts. This trade-off is structural, not accidental: accents associated with institutional authority pay a warmth tax.

For your priorities, this rules out aiming for institutional-authority accents.

### Reverse linguistic stereotyping

Kang and Rubin (2009) showed that listeners' judgments of accent are shaped by their *expectations* — when given cues about a speaker's ethnicity or nationality, listeners hear accent features that aren't actually there. Conversely, when speech is genuinely highly intelligible, expectation-driven downgrades shrink. This is a structural argument for over-investing in the high-leverage intelligibility features and under-investing in fine-grained native-mimicry: the latter buys you almost nothing once expectation effects are accounted for.

### Specific findings on non-native accent and warmth

Gluszek and Dovidio (2010) and related work show non-native accent is generally associated with reduced sense of belonging and some perceptual penalties — but these penalties are dramatically mitigated by **high comprehensibility, longer interaction time, and warmth-signaling prosody**. In short interactions or low-comprehensibility conditions, the penalty is real; in extended high-comprehensibility interaction, it largely disappears and can even invert (mild accent has been associated with positive personality attributions in some contexts — distinctiveness, internationalness, perceived multilingualism).

For your audience profile (international professional, often extended interaction), this is favorable: the penalty zone is exactly the one your priorities push you to escape.

## 3. Which Accent Is "Most Positively Received"?

This is the question to interrogate rather than answer directly. The research does provide rankings, but the rankings aren't useful for your case. Here's why.

### Native-target accent rankings (for reference)

In UK matched-guise and large-scale survey studies (Coupland & Bishop 2007; YouGov-style polls; ITV/BBC research):

- **Standard Southern British English / "educated southern"** — high competence, mid warmth.
- **RP (traditional)** — high competence, lower warmth, increasingly perceived as posh/distant in younger demographics.
- **Southern Irish English (Dublin/Cork educated)** — high on *both* competence and warmth. Consistently the top-rated accent across UK studies.
- **Edinburgh Scottish English** — high on both, especially trustworthiness.
- **Yorkshire** — high warmth and honesty, mid competence.
- **Geordie (Newcastle)** — high warmth, mid competence.
- **Liverpool, Birmingham, Black Country, Cockney** — penalized on most dimensions, including warmth in some studies.

In US contexts:

- **General American / "Newscaster"** — high competence, neutral warmth.
- **Mid-Atlantic / educated coastal** — high competence.
- Southern, New York, AAVE accents face well-documented competence penalties, with variable warmth scores.

In international contexts:

- The "most positively received" research thins out considerably.
- What replaces it is a different question: what's most *intelligible* across mixed-L1 audiences? Answer: speech conforming to the **Lingua Franca Core** (see §5), at moderate pace, with clear prosodic chunking.

### Why none of the native-target rankings should be your goal

1. **Reachability**. Acquiring a convincing Irish or Edinburgh accent as an L1 Polish adult speaker is not a realistic project. The phonetic detail is enormous; the perceptual cost of an unconvincing approximation is high (it reads as "trying and failing," which carries its own social penalty).
2. **Audience mismatch**. Optimizing for UK insider perception in international meetings is the wrong audience model. International speakers don't share UK accent associations; they bring their own L1-based perceptions plus intelligibility expectations.
3. **Status–solidarity trap**. The accents that win UK competence rankings (RP, SSBE) lose on warmth. The ones that win warmth (Irish, Edinburgh) are unrealistic targets *and* carry strong region-specific identity associations that read as performative coming from a non-native speaker.
4. **Native-target failure mode**. The asymptote of native-mimicry pursuit isn't "native-like accent." It's an accent that hovers in the uncanny valley — close enough to a native variety that listeners process it as "trying to be British/American" rather than as "international professional." This *reduces* warmth.

### The actual target: intelligibility-optimized international English with retained L1 traces

The realistic and research-supported target is:

- **Lingua Franca Core compliance** for segmentals: get the high-functional-load contrasts right; tolerate the low-load ones.
- **Native-like suprasegmentals**: word stress, sentence stress, vowel reduction, thought-group chunking, intonation patterns. These are the largest comprehensibility levers.
- **Warmth-signaling prosody**: pitch range, pace, smile-voice, rising contours on listener-engaging utterances.
- **Retained mild L1 colour**: not a goal in itself, but explicitly *not* a target for elimination. It signals authenticity, distinguishes you from native speakers in a way that often reads positively in international contexts, and saves you the enormous training cost of native mimicry.

This is a known target shape. In ELT discourse it's sometimes called "international English" or "Euro-English"; in research, it descends from Jenkins's lingua franca work.

## 4. Polish L1 → English: Transfer Issues Prioritized by Impact

Polish-English contrastive phonology is well-documented (Sobkowiak, Szpyra-Kozłowska). The issues below are ranked by **functional load** (Munro & Derwing 2006) — the empirically estimated cost-to-intelligibility of *not* getting them right. Spend training time in proportion to impact.

### High functional load — fix these first

**1. Vowel length and quality contrasts.** Polish has six oral vowels; English has roughly twelve. The collapses that hurt intelligibility most:
- /iː/ vs /ɪ/ (*sheep* vs *ship*) — Polish /i/ tends to merge these toward /i/.
- /uː/ vs /ʊ/ (*pool* vs *pull*) — same issue.
- /æ/ vs /ʌ/ vs /ɑː/ (*cat* vs *cut* vs *cart*) — Polish /a/ tends to undershoot all three.
- /ɛ/ vs /æ/ (*bed* vs *bad*) — common merger.

These are the single highest-leverage segmental targets. Train with HVPT (multi-talker minimal-pair identification) before production work.

**2. Word stress placement.** Polish has fixed penultimate stress; English has variable lexical stress with semantic and grammatical roles. Misplaced word stress is one of the strongest predictors of comprehensibility loss in L2 English. *PHO-to-graph* vs *pho-TO-gra-phy* vs *pho-to-GRA-phic* — getting these wrong derails listener parsing immediately.

**3. Vowel reduction in unstressed syllables.** This is connected to (2) and arguably the highest-leverage suprasegmental feature. English unstressed syllables reduce to schwa /ə/; Polish maintains full vowel quality. Failure to reduce makes English sound stilted, robotic, and crucially *slower* to process — every syllable demands equal listener attention. Training reduction is hard but transformative.

**4. Aspiration on initial /p/, /t/, /k/.** English aspirates these word-initially (*pin*, *top*, *cat*); Polish doesn't. Without aspiration, English listeners often hear /b/, /d/, /g/ instead. Voicing perception in English is largely driven by aspiration (VOT), not voicing per se.

**5. Final obstruent voicing.** Polish devoices final obstruents (*klub* → [klup]); English maintains voicing distinction (*lab* vs *lap*). Devoicing creates lexical confusions and a strongly marked Slavic L1 signature.

### Medium functional load — worth addressing

**6. Sentence-level prosody and thought-group chunking.** English uses pitch contours and pauses to mark information units, focus, and turn-taking. Polish prosody patterns transfer poorly. Listeners rely heavily on prosodic cues to parse, and prosodic mismatch increases comprehensibility cost even when segmentals are clean.

**7. /v/ vs /w/.** Modern Polish has both [v] and [w] (the latter from historical *ł*), so this is *less* of an issue for younger Polish speakers than for many other Slavic L1s. Worth checking individually; if you have it solid, skip.

**8. Dark /l/ at syllable end.** English has [ɫ] in *milk*, *bell*; Polish doesn't reliably distinguish. Affects accent quality but not core intelligibility.

**9. /r/ realisation.** Polish trill or tap, English approximant. Highly perceptually salient (signature L1 marker) but low intelligibility cost. Often near the bottom of training priority for *intelligibility* purposes; near the top for *accent reduction* purposes. Given your priorities, deprioritize it.

### Low functional load — can be ignored or deprioritized

**10. /θ/ and /ð/ (the *th* sounds).** Counterintuitive but well-supported finding: substituting these with /t/-/d/ or /s/-/z/ or /f/-/v/ has minimal intelligibility cost. The Lingua Franca Core explicitly excludes them. Native-target accent reduction obsesses over them; international comprehensibility doesn't need them. Spend time elsewhere.

**11. Specific vowel quality targets within already-distinguished pairs.** Once you reliably contrast /iː/-/ɪ/, hitting the precise British or American vowel quality is low-leverage. Approximate is fine.

**12. /h/ vs /x/.** Polish "h" is often [x]; English /h/ is glottal. Audible but rarely causes confusion.

### Polish-accent-specific perceptual context

Polish-accented English in UK contexts carries some specific stereotype baggage from the post-2004 migration narrative, often associating it with manual labour and working-class registers in the British social imagination (some sociolinguistic work post-2010 documents this). In **international** contexts (your primary audience), this baggage doesn't transfer — Polish L1 traces register simply as "European" or "Slavic," with weaker stereotype loading. This is another structural reason your audience-prioritization is well-chosen: it places you outside the zone where Polish-specific perceptual penalties are sharpest.

## 5. General L2 Methods for English Intelligibility

### The Lingua Franca Core (Jenkins 2000, 2007)

Jenkins's empirically derived minimum feature set for intelligibility in international (non-native-to-non-native) English. Within the LFC, train hard:

- All consonant contrasts *except* /θ/ and /ð/.
- Aspirated /p t k/ in initial position.
- Initial and medial consonant clusters intact (not simplified or epenthesized — Polish speakers sometimes break clusters).
- Vowel **length** contrasts (the long-short distinction matters; the precise quality less so).
- **Nuclear stress placement** — which word in a thought group carries the prominence. This is the single largest comprehensibility lever in LFC research.

Outside the LFC (lower priority for international intelligibility):

- /θ/ and /ð/.
- Specific vowel qualities matching a native variety.
- Weak forms / connected speech reductions in input (helpful for understanding native speech, but not required for being understood internationally).
- /r/ quality.
- Pitch-movement patterns at the level of "British vs American" intonation.

### Functional load principle (Munro & Derwing 2006; Catford-derived)

Train segmental contrasts in proportion to their lexical disambiguation work in English. High-load contrasts (the vowel pairs above, voicing in obstruents) get the time. Low-load contrasts get triaged. This sounds obvious but contradicts most pronunciation-textbook structure, which treats all segments equally.

### High-Variability Phonetic Training (HVPT) — application

For Polish learners, the highest-yield HVPT targets:

- /iː/-/ɪ/ identification, multi-talker.
- /æ/-/ʌ/-/ɑː/ identification.
- Final voicing identification (*lab*-*lap*, *bag*-*back*).
- Word stress identification on common multi-syllable words (especially Latinate vocabulary where Polish penultimate stress strongly mispredicts).

Available tools: English Accent Coach (web, free, Bradlow lab–derived); the Englisch Lernen / EnglishCentral platforms (commercial); custom Praat-based identification tasks if you want to build your own.

### Suprasegmental training

The largest gains for least training time come from suprasegmentals:

- **Word stress** — explicit study of stress patterns in your active vocabulary, especially Latinate and Greek-origin polysyllables where Polish stress predicts wrongly.
- **Sentence stress / nuclear prominence** — practising thought groups with deliberate prominence on the new-information word.
- **Vowel reduction** — shadowing exercises focused on rhythm, with explicit attention to schwa in unstressed syllables.
- **Thought-group chunking** — pause placement aligned with syntactic and informational units.

Method: shadowing of recordings (TED talks, podcasts) with focus *only* on rhythm and prosody, not on segmental imitation. 10–15 minutes daily is high-leverage.

### Voice quality, pace, and fluency markers (not "accent" but high-leverage for warmth)

These are often the largest perceptual levers and are usually ignored in pronunciation work:

- **Pace**. Slight slowing relative to default (especially for L2 speakers under stress) is associated with higher comprehensibility *and* higher warmth ratings. Not slow — moderate. Rushed L2 speech is the most common pattern and the most comprehensibility-costly.
- **Pause behaviour**. Filled pauses (*um*, *er*, *yyy*) reduce competence ratings; clean silent pauses for thinking time are neutral or positive. This is trainable.
- **Pitch range**. Wider melodic range correlates with warmth and engagement perception. Polish English tends to flatten pitch range — actively expanding it is high-leverage for warmth specifically.
- **Smile-voice**. The acoustic correlates of smiling while speaking (slightly raised formants, modified spectral tilt) are perceived as warmth markers cross-linguistically. Smiling on the phone is not a cliché; it's a measurable acoustic effect.
- **Voice setting / register**. Polish phonation is often slightly creakier and lower; English (especially American) uses more modal voice with greater breathiness in social registers. Adjusting voice setting affects perceived warmth more than any individual segment.

### Mismatch the priorities you don't have

The corollary to your prioritization: **actively do not train** features that cost time without serving your goals. Don't train /θ/ and /ð/. Don't try to acquire a specific native vowel quality once length is solid. Don't try to acquire a non-rhotic /r/ or any specific regional /r/. Don't try to imitate connected-speech reductions of a specific variety. The training time saved goes to suprasegmentals and voice quality.

## 6. The Practical Leverage Map

If I had to rank training priorities by *expected gain on your specific objective* (warmth-first international perception):

**Tier 1 — highest return, train first**
1. Vowel length contrasts (/iː/-/ɪ/, /uː/-/ʊ/, /æ/-/ʌ/-/ɑː/).
2. Word stress placement on polysyllabic vocabulary.
3. Vowel reduction (schwa in unstressed syllables).
4. Pace and pause behaviour.

**Tier 2 — high return, train second**
5. Nuclear stress / sentence-level prominence.
6. Thought-group chunking and intonation contours.
7. Pitch range expansion / warmth-signaling prosody.
8. Aspiration on initial /p t k/.
9. Final obstruent voicing.

**Tier 3 — moderate return**
10. Voice quality / phonation register.
11. /v/-/w/ check (likely already solid).
12. Cluster integrity (no epenthesis).

**Tier 4 — deprioritize or skip entirely**
13. /θ/-/ð/ — skip (LFC).
14. /r/ quality — skip (low intelligibility cost).
15. Specific vowel quality match to a native variety — skip once length is solid.
16. Connected-speech reduction patterns of any specific native variety — skip.
17. Acquiring features of a specific UK or US regional accent — actively avoid.

This is a research-grounded leverage ranking; it'll look very different from any commercial "accent coaching" curriculum, which tends to over-weight Tier 4 because that's what learners traditionally *want* and what looks like obvious "accent reduction" progress.

Organized by theme. Mostly classics and standard references, with a few Polish-specific items.

5. **Munro, M. J., & Derwing, T. M. (1995).** Foreign accent, comprehensibility, and intelligibility in the speech of second language learners. *Language Learning, 45*(1), 73–97.

6. **Derwing, T. M., & Munro, M. J. (2015).** *Pronunciation Fundamentals: Evidence-based Perspectives for L2 Teaching and Research.* John Benjamins.

## Final Note: A Realistic Time Horizon

The research suggests that a focused programme on the Tier 1 and Tier 2 items above, with consistent weekly practice (HVPT for perception, shadowing for prosody, voice work for warmth markers), produces measurable comprehensibility gains within 3–6 months and continues to improve over 12–24 months. The gains plateau but don't disappear. This is faster than the traditional native-mimicry curriculum because it's training fewer, better-chosen things.

Crucially, you don't have to "finish" before benefits accrue. Comprehensibility is roughly linear in effort up to a high asymptote, and warmth-perception gains transfer across all your priority dimensions simultaneously. Each week of focused work produces real perceptual change.
