### The one idea that ties Phase 1 together

These are **not** eight isolated tenses. Spanish verb morphology is a system in which each piece you learn pre-builds later ones, so effort spent now is repaid several times over. Keep this scaffold in view as you go:

- The **present** *yo*-form (Module 1) is the base of the present subjunctive (Week 17) **and** of the negative/*usted* commands you meet in Module 8.
- The **participle** and the *haber* + participle **architecture** (Module 2) are reused, unchanged, in the pluperfect (Module 5) and in five further compound tenses across Phases 2–3.
- The **third-person-plural preterite** (Module 3) is the stem of the entire imperfect subjunctive (Week 29).
- The **imperfect of *haber*** (Module 4) builds the pluperfect (Module 5), and the imperfect's *-ía* endings reappear as the **conditional** endings (Module 6).
- The **future and conditional share one set of irregular stems** (Module 6): learn twelve stems once, use them in two tenses.
- The **imperative's** negative, *usted*, and *nosotros* forms **are** subjunctive forms (Module 8) — your first real exposure to the subjunctive before Phase 2.

Whenever a section is headed *"where this feeds,"* it is pointing at one of these links.

# Module 1 — El presente de indicativo
### *(Week 1)*

## 1.1 The first idea: the Spanish present covers more ground than the English present

English splits present time across several forms — *I work*, *I am working*, *I do work* — and reserves the simple present mostly for habits and general truths. Spanish has **one** simple present, *trabajo*, that does all of that at once: it can mean *I work*, *I am working*, *I do work*, and, in the right context, *I'll work* or *I'm about to work*. The consequence for a reader: do not expect Spanish to mark "habitually" versus "right now" the way English does. *¿Qué haces?* can be *What do you do (for a living)?* or *What are you doing (this second)?* — context decides. Spanish **can** be explicit about "right now" with the continuous (Module 7), but reaches for it far less than English. Where English almost always says *I'm reading*, Spanish is content with plain *leo*. Hold onto this: **one present form, many English translations.**

## 1.2 The regular system

Every verb splits into a **stem** (the meaning) and an **ending** (person, number, tense, mood). Strip the infinitive's *-ar / -er / -ir* and the personal endings attach to what remains:

| | **-ar** (hablar) | **-er** (comer) | **-ir** (vivir) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablo | como | vivo |
| tú | hablas | comes | vives |
| él/ella/usted | habla | come | vive |
| nosotros/as | hablamos | comemos | vivimos |
| vosotros/as | habláis | coméis | vivís |
| ellos/ellas/ustedes | hablan | comen | viven |

Two load-lightening facts. The *-er* and *-ir* classes are **identical except at *nosotros* and *vosotros*** (*comemos/coméis* vs *vivimos/vivís*) — really two-and-a-bit patterns, not three. And the *yo*-form is *-o* for **all** regular verbs; when it deviates, the deviation is lexically specified and worth noting, which is why it is such a useful anchor.

**Pro-drop and the pronoun system.** Because each ending identifies its person, the subject pronoun is normally **omitted**: *hablo español*, not *yo hablo español*. It reappears only for emphasis/contrast (*Yo trabajo, pero él no*) or to disambiguate the third person. For reading this is fundamental — **the ending is the subject**; reading fluently means reading person and number off the ending with zero latency.

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | yo | nosotros/as |
| 2nd familiar | tú | **vosotros/as** |
| 2nd formal | usted | ustedes |
| 3rd | él / ella | ellos / ellas |

**A Peninsular point to absorb now.** In Spain the second-person plural splits by register exactly as the singular does: *vosotros* is the familiar plural, *ustedes* the formal. Latin America has dropped *vosotros* entirely and uses *ustedes* for every plural "you." Your reading is Peninsular, so *vosotros* forms (*habláis, coméis, vivís*) are **live and frequent** — read them instantly as "you-all (informal)." Note too that *usted/ustedes* take **third-person** endings (*usted habla*) — a fossil that briefly reads as "who is this?" until it's second nature.

## 1.3 Stem-changing verbs: regular machinery, lexically triggered

A large class modifies the **stressed stem vowel** in the present. Four patterns: **e→ie** (*pensar → pienso*, *querer → quiero*), **o→ue** (*poder → puedo*, *dormir → duermo*), **e→i** (only *-ir* verbs: *pedir → pido*, *seguir → sigo*), and **u→ue** (one verb: *jugar → juego*). The change appears only where the stem is stressed — *yo, tú, él, ellos* — and is absent at *nosotros/vosotros*, where stress falls on the ending. Laid out, the affected cells form the well-known **"boot"**:

| | pensar (e→ie) | poder (o→ue) | pedir (e→i) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | pienso | puedo | pido |
| tú | piensas | puedes | pides |
| él | piensa | puede | pide |
| nosotros | pensamos | podemos | pedimos |
| vosotros | pensáis | podéis | pedís |
| ellos | piensan | pueden | piden |

The shape is not arbitrary: the alternation is **stress-conditioned** (historically, stressed short Latin *e/o* diphthongised; unstressed, they stayed). So instead of memorising *which cells change*, think *where does the stress fall* — stem in four forms, ending in *nosotros/vosotros* — and the boot follows. **What the rule does not tell you** is *whether* a given verb diphthongises at all: *contar* does (*cuento*) but *comer* does not; *pensar* does but *entrar* does not. That is a **lexical** property you learn per verb (dictionaries mark it "(ie)/(ue)/(i)"). So: *which* verbs change is idiosyncratic; *how* they change is fully regular.

## 1.4 Irregular *yo*-forms

Many verbs are regular everywhere **except** the first-person singular. They fall into tidy families:

- **-go group:** *poner → pongo*, *salir → salgo*, *hacer → hago*, *valer → valgo*. Several combine *-go* **with** a stem change in the other boot cells — drill these hardest: *tener* → **tengo**, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen; *venir* → **vengo**, vienes…; *decir* → **digo**, dices…; *oír* → **oigo**, oyes, oye, oímos, oís, oyen.
- **-zco group** (vowel + *-cer/-cir*): *conocer → conozco*, *conducir → conduzco*, *parecer → parezco*, *traducir → traduzco*. (Note *hacer* and *decir* are *-go*, not *-zco*, despite ending in *-cer/-cir*.)
- **-oy group:** *dar → doy*, *estar → estoy*, *ser → soy*, *ir → voy*.
- **Isolated:** *saber → sé* (otherwise regular), *ver → veo*, *caber → quepo*.

**Where this feeds:** the present subjunctive (Week 17) is built by taking this *yo*-form and flipping the vowel (*tengo → tenga*, *conozco → conozca*), and the *usted*/negative commands of Module 8 come from that subjunctive. Get the *yo*-form exactly right now and you pre-build both.

## 1.5 Fully irregular high-frequency verbs

A handful are irregular across the paradigm — and are among the most frequent words in the language, so know them cold:

| | ser | estar | ir | haber |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| yo | soy | estoy | voy | he |
| tú | eres | estás | vas | has |
| él | es | está | va | ha *(hay)* |
| nosotros | somos | estamos | vamos | hemos |
| vosotros | sois | estáis | vais | habéis |
| ellos | son | están | van | han |

*Ser* and *estar* both mean "to be" but divide identity/essence from state/location (Week 38). *Estar* carries accents on four forms (*estás, está, estáis, están*). *Ir* is **suppletive** — *voy* has no *ir* in it, like *go/went*. **Haber** is primarily the **auxiliary** that builds compound tenses (Module 2), *not* possession (that's *tener*); its one independent present form is impersonal **hay** ("there is/are"), which serves singular and plural alike.

## 1.6 Spelling-change (orthographic) verbs

A small group changes **spelling** in the *yo*-form to keep the **sound** constant: **-ger/-gir → -jo** (*coger → cojo*, *dirigir → dirijo*); **-guir → -go** (*seguir → sigo* — also an e→i stem-changer; *distinguir → distingo*); **-uir → -uyo** (*construir → construyo*, *huir → huyo*); **consonant + -cer → -zo** (*vencer → venzo*). A reading note on *coger*: in Peninsular Spanish it is an ordinary high-frequency verb — *to take, catch, pick up* (*cojo el metro*) — neutral and ubiquitous; read it without flinching.

## 1.7 What the present is used for

**Habitual/repeated action** (default): *Trabajo en un banco. Todos los días leo el periódico.* Signposts: *siempre, normalmente, todos los días, a menudo*. — **Action in progress now** (where English uses the progressive): *—¿Qué haces? —Veo la tele.* — **General/timeless truths:** *El agua hierve a cien grados. La Tierra gira alrededor del Sol.* — **The historical (narrative) present**, crucial for reading: past events narrated in the present for vividness — *En 1492, Colón llega a América* — so a passage plainly about the past may run in the present; recognise the device. — **The near future:** *Mañana voy al médico. Esta noche cenamos fuera.* — **Time elapsed:** *Vivo aquí desde hace tres años* / *Hace tres años que vivo aquí* — present, not perfect, for ongoing duration. — **Real conditions** (preview of Week 28): *Si tienes tiempo, hablamos luego* — *si* + present, never present subjunctive. — **Instructions/directions:** *Sigues recto y giras a la derecha.*

## 1.8 Reading skills for this module

Read person off the **ending**, not a pronoun (pro-drop). Expect ***vosotros*** in Peninsular texts. Watch the ***usted/ustedes* third-person trick** in dialogue. Recognise the **historical present** in narration. Register **pronominal/reflexive** clitics (*me llamo, se levanta, nos vemos*) as part of the verb (full treatment Week 37).

## 1.9 Calibration

**Settled:** all the morphology above (RAE *Nueva gramática*). **Regional, not yours:** *voseo* (*vos tenés, vos sos*) is standard in much of Latin America and **absent** from Peninsular Spanish — you'll see it only in e.g. Argentine authors; and the *vosotros/ustedes* split is live in Spain, gone in Latin America. **Overstated:** filing stem-changers under "irregular verbs" — the diphthongisation is a **regular, stress-conditioned** alternation; only *which* verbs undergo it is unpredictable.

**Sources & work (Week 1).** Study the *presente de indicativo* units in **GdU** (regular → irregular-*yo* → stem-changing) with the *solucionario*; second pass in **Castro-I**; consult **B&B** (index "present tense") for the *why*. Drill the priority verbs in **501/PMP**. **Own by Friday:** any regular verb + the top ~20 irregulars (*ser, estar, ir, haber, tener, venir, decir, hacer, poner, oír, poder, querer, pedir, conocer, seguir, ver, saber, dar, salir, volver*). **Self-test:** conjugate *tener, hacer, ir, seguir, conocer, querer* cold; then read a page and name the person of every present-tense verb at sight.

# Module 2 — El pretérito perfecto compuesto
### *(Week 2)*

## 2.1 The first idea: a present tense about the past

Hold the perfect not as "a kind of past" but as **a present tense that looks back**. Its morphology says so — it is built on the *present* of *haber*. Its meaning follows — it describes a past event **in so far as it connects to the present**, either because it happened inside a still-open stretch of time ("today," "this week") or because its result persists *now*. Andrés Bello named it the **antepresente**: an event placed before a reference point that *is* the present moment. That is your mental model, and it explains both the morphology (present auxiliary) and every use below.

## 2.2 The compound architecture: *haber* + participle

A compound tense has a **conjugated auxiliary** (person/number/tense/mood) plus an **invariable non-finite form** (the meaning). Here: present of *haber* + past participle.

| | *haber* | + participle | (hablar) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | he | + hablado | he hablado |
| tú | has | | has hablado |
| él | ha | | ha hablado |
| nosotros | hemos | | hemos hablado |
| vosotros | habéis | | habéis hablado |
| ellos | han | | han hablado |

Three structural facts that matter for reading. **The two pieces are inseparable** — nothing comes between *haber* and the participle; adverbs go outside (*nunca he visto*, *ya he comido*). **Clitics attach to the front of the whole unit** (*lo he visto, me ha dicho, se han ido*), never inside (*\*he lo visto*). And this *haber* is **not possession** (that's *tener*); its one independent form is impersonal **ha habido** ("there has/have been").

## 2.3 Past participle formation

**Regular:** *-ar → -ado* (hablado); *-er/-ir → -ido* (comido, vivido) — the two classes collapse into one. **Accent sub-rule:** an *-er/-ir* stem ending in a strong vowel takes an accent on the *í* to mark the hiatus — *leído, traído, oído, caído, creído* (but *construido, huido* — *ui* is a diphthong, no accent). **Irregular participles** (learn as a block — the main cause of "what verb is that?"):

| hacer→**hecho** | decir→**dicho** | ver→**visto** | poner→**puesto** | volver→**vuelto** | resolver→**resuelto** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **abrir→abierto** | **cubrir→cubierto** | **escribir→escrito** | **romper→roto** | **morir→muerto** | **satisfacer→satisfecho** |

Compounds inherit it: *componer→compuesto, devolver→devuelto, describir→descrito, deshacer→deshecho, descubrir→descubierto*. **Two participles:** a few verbs keep an irregular form **only as an adjective** while the compound tense takes the regular one — *he despertado* (verb) vs *estoy despierto* (adj); *ha soltado* vs *está suelto*. A small set admits both in the compound tense: *imprimir* (imprimido/impreso), *freír* (freído/frito), *proveer* (proveído/provisto).

## 2.4 With *haber*, the participle never agrees

A clean diagnostic you'll use constantly. Governed by *haber*, the participle is **invariable** — always *-o*: *ella ha llegado, ellas han llegado, nosotras hemos comido*. As an **adjective** (with *ser/estar* or modifying a noun) it **agrees**: *la puerta está abierta, los libros están escritos*. So: **participle in *-o* right after *haber* = verb; participle agreeing in gender/number = adjective or passive.** (*Han abierto la tienda* vs *la tienda está abierta*.) This rule returns in Weeks 38 and 41.

## 2.5 What the perfect is used for

**(a) A past event inside a time-frame that includes "now"** — the signature **Peninsular** use. If the period hasn't closed (*hoy, esta mañana, esta semana, este mes/año, este verano*), the perfect is default: *Esta mañana **he ido** al médico. Este año **hemos viajado** mucho.* This is exactly where English uses the simple past ("I went this morning"), which is why English speakers under-use it. — **(b) Life experience up to now** ("ever/never/already/yet"), **universal** across dialects: *¿**Has estado** alguna vez en Japón? Nunca **he probado** las ostras. Ya **he terminado**. Todavía no **han llegado**.* Signposts: *alguna vez, nunca, ya, todavía no, aún no, una/dos veces, siempre*. — **(c) The very recent past** (Peninsular-leaning): *Te **ha llamado** tu hermana.* For "have just done," Spanish often prefers **acabar de** + infinitive (*Acabo de comer*). — **(d) A past action whose result persists now**, even with no time marker: *He perdido las llaves. Ha habido un accidente.*

## 2.6 Where the perfect stops and the preterite starts (a preview of Module 3/4)

Standard Peninsular draws the line by whether the time marker **includes the moment of speaking**:

| Includes now → **perfecto** | Closed/detached → **indefinido** |
|---|---|
| hoy → *Hoy he ido.* | ayer → *Ayer fui.* |
| esta semana → *Esta semana he trabajado.* | la semana pasada → *La semana pasada trabajé.* |
| este año, alguna vez, ya | el año pasado, en 2010, hace dos años |

Mechanical test: *hoy/esta semana/este año/alguna vez/ya* → open → perfect; *ayer/anoche/la semana pasada/en + year/hace + time* → closed → preterite.

## 2.7 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** read *haber* + participle as one verb; the irregular participles (*hecho, dicho, visto, puesto, escrito, vuelto, roto, abierto*) are the trap — drill them; clitics sit before the unit; use the agreement diagnostic; *ha habido* = "there has/have been." **Settled & pan-Hispanic:** the morphology and the *experiential* use. **Regional (the defining issue):** use (a) is the **central/northern-Peninsular** norm and the prestige/textbook norm — but **not universal even in Spain** (Galicia, Asturias, León, the Canaries lean to the preterite even for today), and across most of **Latin America** the preterite dominates that territory (*hoy fui, ya terminé*). So "perfect for today" is a **dialectal norm of central Peninsular**, which is your target — but knowing it's regional keeps an Argentine novel's *hoy comí* from reading as an error. **Overstated:** "it's just the English present perfect." They agree in rejecting definite-past-time (*\*Lo he visto ayer* / *\*I have seen him yesterday*), but diverge on "this morning/today" (Peninsular perfect vs American English simple past; British English tolerates the perfect more) — so the **time-frame test**, not translation from English, is the guide. **Note:** PMP is American-leaning and will *under*-drill use (a) — trust GdU/Castro for it.

**Sources & work (Week 2).** Study the *pretérito perfecto* and participle units in **GdU** with the *solucionario*; the *perfecto* unit in **Castro-I** for the time-marker contrasts; **B&B** (index "perfect tense," "past participle") is unusually good on the Peninsular-vs-American difference — read that. **Own by Friday:** the present of *haber* + the irregular participles. **Self-test:** form the perfect of *hacer, ver, escribir, volver, decir* cold; then, given *hoy / ayer / esta semana / la semana pasada / alguna vez / en 2015* + *(ir al cine)*, produce *he ido / fui / he ido / fui / he ido / fui*.

# Module 3 — El pretérito indefinido (regular e irregular)
### *(Weeks 3–4)*

## 3.1 The first idea: a completed whole, seen from the outside

The preterite presents a past event as a **complete, bounded whole**, viewed from outside as finished — **perfective aspect**. The Spanish contrast that matters is not *when* but *how the event is viewed*: the preterite sees a closed package; the imperfect (Module 4) sees it from inside, ongoing, endpoints irrelevant. Both are past; the difference is aspect. Three consequences: the preterite **moves a narrative forward** (event after completed event); it is selected by **completeness, not duration** (a twenty-year action can be preterite if viewed as finished — killing the myth that "long actions take the imperfect"); and, being detached from the present, it is the tense of **closed past time**, which is the boundary with the perfect.

## 3.2 The regular endings *(Week 3)*

| | -ar (hablar) | -er (comer) | -ir (vivir) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablé | comí | viví |
| tú | hablaste | comiste | viviste |
| él | habló | comió | vivió |
| nosotros | hablamos | comimos | vivimos |
| vosotros | hablasteis | comisteis | vivisteis |
| ellos | hablaron | comieron | vivieron |

Four things that recur when reading. The *-er/-ir* classes are **completely identical**. The **1sg and 3sg are stressed on the ending and accented** — *hablé/habló*, *comí/comió* — and this accent does lexical work (§3.6). There is **no *-o* first person**; here *-ó/-ió* is the *third* person (*habló* = "he spoke," not "I speak") — a reversal from the present. And the **1st-person plural is ambiguous for *-ar/-ir*** verbs: *hablamos/vivimos* are present *or* preterite (context decides), while *-er* escapes it (*comemos* vs *comimos*).

## 3.3 Regular verbs with spelling changes *(Week 3)*

Regular endings, predictable spelling shifts to keep the sound. **-car/-gar/-zar (yo only):** *buscar → busqué*, *llegar → llegué*, *empezar → empecé* (also *saqué, toqué, pagué, jugué, almorcé, crucé*). **i → y (3rd persons)**, with accents elsewhere for hiatus: *leer → leí, leíste, **leyó**, leímos, leísteis, **leyeron***; likewise *creyó, oyó, cayó, construyó*. Read *leyó/oyó/cayó* as ordinary preterites, not exotic irregulars.

## 3.4 The irregular "strong" preterites *(Week 4)*

Here is the new material, and it is high-leverage. A set of very common verbs has an **irregular stem** plus a distinctive ending set that is **unstressed — and therefore unaccented — in the 1sg and 3sg**: **-e, -iste, -o, -imos, -isteis, -ieron**. That root-stress is what "strong" means: *tener → **tuve, tuvo*** (no accent), against regular *hablé, habló*. The stems group neatly:

| Group | Stem change | Verbs |
|---|---|---|
| **u-stems** | → *u* | estar→estuv-, tener→tuv-, poder→pud-, poner→pus-, saber→sup-, andar→anduv-, caber→cup-, haber→hub- |
| **i-stems** | → *i* | hacer→hic- *(hizo, c→z before o)*, querer→quis-, venir→vin- |
| **j-stems** | → *j*, and **-eron** (no *i*) in 3pl | decir→dij- *(dijeron)*, traer→traj- *(trajeron)*, conducir→conduj-, producir→produj-, and all *-ducir* verbs |

Model paradigm (*tener*): **tuve, tuviste, tuvo, tuvimos, tuvisteis, tuvieron.** Watch **hizo** (*hacer*, spelling) and the **j-group 3pl** (*dijeron, trajeron, condujeron* — not *\*dijieron*).

**Three verbs stand apart.** *Ser* and *ir* share **one** preterite — *fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fuisteis, fueron* — identical for both; only context tells them apart (*Fui médico* "I was a doctor" vs *Fui al médico* "I went to the doctor"). *Dar* takes *-er/-ir* endings with no accents: **di, diste, dio, dimos, disteis, dieron.** *Ver* likewise: **vi, viste, vio, vimos, visteis, vieron.**

**The *-ir* stem-changers** (flagged in Week 3): otherwise-regular endings, but the stem vowel raises **e→i / o→u in the 3rd person**: *pedir → pidió, pidieron*; *dormir → durmió, durmieron*; *sentir → sintió, sintieron*; *morir → murió, murieron*; *seguir → siguió, siguieron*.

**Where this feeds — the payoff promised in Module 3.** The **entire imperfect subjunctive** (Week 29) is formed from the **third-person-plural preterite**: drop *-ron*, add *-ra/-se*. So *tuvieron → tuviera*, *dijeron → dijera*, *hicieron → hiciera*, *fueron → fuera*, *pudieron → pudiera*, *pidieron → pidiera*, *leyeron → leyera*. Every strong stem you drill now is an imperfect-subjunctive stem pre-built — which is why Week 4 is worth over-investing in.

## 3.5 What the preterite is used for

**Single completed events in closed time:** *Ayer comí paella. Nació en 1985. La reunión terminó a las cinco.* Markers: *ayer, anoche, el otro día, la semana/el año pasado, en 1999, hace dos años, entonces*. — **Sequences — the narrative engine:** *Abrió la puerta, entró y encendió la luz.* (Note *abrió*: irregular *participle* **abierto** but regular *preterite* — independent.) A run of preterites is the plot moving forward. — **Bounded durations, now over:** *Viví en Madrid tres años. La guerra duró seis años.* Length is irrelevant; boundedness selects. — **Change of state:** with state verbs the preterite marks the *moment the state began*: *Conocí a María en 2015* ("I **met** her") vs imperfect *conocía* ("I **knew** her"). The same shift, using the strong preterites of Week 4: *supe* "found out" / *sabía* "knew"; *pude* "managed to" (*no pude* "failed to") / *podía* "was able to"; *quise* "tried" (*no quise* "refused") / *quería* "wanted"; *tuve* "got/received" / *tenía* "had." (Full contrast in Module 4.)

## 3.6 Reading skills

**Read the accent** — *hablo* ("I speak") vs *habló* ("he spoke") differ only by it, in both person and time; train it as information-bearing. Remember the **person reversal** (*-ó/-ió* = third person). Watch the ***nosotros* ambiguity** (*hablamos/vivimos*). Read *leyó/cayó/oyó* and the **strong stems** (*tuvo, dijo, hizo, fue*) at sight — the strong preterites are short and easy to misparse. **Simple, not compound:** one word, no *haber*.

## 3.7 The two boundaries & calibration

**vs the imperfect** (Module 4): perfective foreground vs imperfective background — *Cuando salí de casa, llovía*. **vs the perfect** (Module 2): closed vs open time — *ayer fui* / *hoy he ido* (standard Peninsular). **Terminology (map it once):** this one tense is *pretérito perfecto simple* (RAE current) = *pretérito indefinido* (Castro, GdU, all ELE, and this curriculum) = *pretérito* (Bello) = *preterite / simple past* (English). **Regional:** most of Latin America and NW Spain/the Canaries use the preterite **more** than central Spain (even for today), so its territory is larger across the language than in central Peninsular; *vosotros* is *-asteis/-isteis* (vernacular *-astes/-istes* appears in dialogue but is non-standard). **Overstated:** "long/durative actions take the imperfect" (false — completeness selects); "preterite = English simple past" (English simple past also covers habitual past, which is the *imperfect*, and sometimes maps to the Spanish *perfect*).

**Sources & work (Weeks 3–4).** Week 3: the *indefinido* regular unit in **GdU** (incl. spelling changes) + **Castro-I**. Week 4: the *indefinido* irregular unit(s) — drill the strong stems daily; **B&B** (index "preterite") is strong on aspect and the state-verb shifts. **Own by end of Week 4:** both regular ending-sets; the *-car/-gar/-zar* and *i→y* forms; the u-/i-/j-stem strong preterites; *fui, di, vi*; the *-ir* stem-changers. **Self-test:** conjugate *trabajar, beber, escribir, buscar, llegar, leer* (Wk3) and *estar, tener, hacer, decir, poder, poner, venir, ir* (Wk4) cold; then read a page and identify every preterite at sight, never misreading *habló* as *hablo*.

# Module 4 — El pretérito imperfecto y el gran contraste
### *(Weeks 5–7)*

This is the conceptual core of Phase 1. The imperfect itself is easy morphologically; the **contrast** with the preterite is the single most important thing for reading narrative, and it is where you'll spend real effort.

## 4.1 The imperfect: the imperfective past *(Week 5)*

Where the preterite packages an event as a completed whole, the **imperfect** views it **from inside** — ongoing, habitual, or descriptive, without regard to endpoints. It is the past's **imperfective** aspect: the tense of *what was going on* and *what things were like*, the **background** against which preterite events happen.

**Morphology — the most regular tense in Spanish:**

| | -ar (hablar) | -er (comer) | -ir (vivir) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablaba | comía | vivía |
| tú | hablabas | comías | vivías |
| él | hablaba | comía | vivía |
| nosotros | hablábamos | comíamos | vivíamos |
| vosotros | hablabais | comíais | vivíais |
| ellos | hablaban | comían | vivían |

Accents on *-ábamos* and on **every** *-ía* form. And there are only **three irregular verbs in the entire language**: *ser* → **era, eras, era, éramos, erais, eran**; *ir* → **iba, ibas, iba, íbamos, ibais, iban**; *ver* → **veía, veías, veía, veíamos, veíais, veían**. That's all — nothing else is irregular. **Crucial reading point:** the **1sg and 3sg are identical** (*hablaba* = "I/he spoke/was speaking"; *comía* = "I/he ate/was eating"), so unlike the present and preterite, here you **must** use context or a pronoun to fix the person.

**Uses of the imperfect.** **(a) Ongoing/in-progress past:** *Llovía. Los niños jugaban en el parque.* — **(b) Habitual/repeated past** ("used to / would"): *De niño iba al colegio andando. Todos los veranos íbamos a la playa.* Markers: *siempre, todos los días, normalmente, a menudo, cada…, de niño/joven*. — **(c) Description** — scenery, states, physical/mental/emotional condition, time, age, weather: *Era de noche. Hacía frío. Tenía veinte años. Estaba cansado. La casa era grande.* — **(d) Simultaneous ongoing actions:** *Mientras cocinaba, escuchaba la radio.* — **(e) Background to a preterite event** (the contrast use): *Cuando llegué, todos dormían.* — Plus **softening/politeness** (*¿Qué deseaba?*) and, in speech, the *imperfecto lúdico* of children's games (*—Yo era el rey y tú venías…*).

**Where this feeds:** the imperfect of *haber* (*había…*) builds the pluperfect (Module 5); and these *-ía* endings are, letter for letter, the **conditional** endings (Module 6).

## 4.2 The great contrast: perfective vs imperfective *(Weeks 6–7)*

Everything now turns on **how the event is viewed**, not when it happened. The preterite is **completed foreground** (what happened, the plot); the imperfect is **ongoing/habitual/descriptive background** (the scenery, the conditions). The canonical patterns:

- **Interruption** — ongoing imperfect + interrupting preterite: *Dormía cuando sonó el teléfono.* / *Cuando salí, llovía.*
- **Two simultaneous ongoing actions** — both imperfect: *Mientras yo leía, él veía la tele.*
- **A sequence of completed events** — all preterite: *Se levantó, se duchó y salió.*
- **Scene + event** — descriptive imperfect sets the stage, preterite delivers the event: *Era de noche y llovía cuando llamaron a la puerta.*
- **Habitual vs single occurrence** — *Antes fumaba* (habitual, imperfect) vs *Ayer fumé un cigarro* (once, preterite).

**Change-of-state verbs — the aspectual contrast made lexical.** Certain state verbs shift meaning between the two aspects, the preterite marking the **change into/out of** the state:

| Verb | Imperfect (ongoing state) | Preterite (change / onset) |
|---|---|---|
| saber | sabía — knew | supe — found out |
| conocer | conocía — knew (a person) | conocí — met |
| poder | podía — was able (capacity) | pude — managed to *(no pude — failed to)* |
| querer | quería — wanted | quise — tried *(no quise — refused)* |
| tener | tenía — had | tuve — got, received |

**The three-tense past picture (folding in Week 7's perfecto vs indefinido).** Standard Peninsular now has a clean three-way split by *how the event relates to the present and how it is viewed*:

- **Pretérito perfecto** — completed, but in a time-frame **connected to now** (*hoy, esta semana, alguna vez, ya*): *Hoy he ido.*
- **Pretérito indefinido** — completed and **detached** from now (*ayer, en 2010*): *Ayer fui.*
- **Pretérito imperfecto** — **ongoing/habitual/descriptive** background, regardless of connection to now: *Iba todos los días. / Llovía.*

## 4.3 Reading skills & calibration

**The reading skill for all of literature:** in a narrative, read the **imperfects as scenery** (setting, weather, states, ongoing background, habits) and the **preterites as plot** (the sequence of completed events). Training this single distinction is what turns opaque narrative into transparent narrative. Remember the **1sg/3sg imperfect ambiguity** — fix the person from context. **Settled:** the morphology and the aspectual analysis (this is textbook consensus, Comrie's *Aspect* and B&B alike). **Overstated (again, because it's the killer error):** "duration/length decides" — it does not; *aspect* (how the event is viewed) decides, which is why *viví allí veinte años* is preterite and *de niño vivía allí* is imperfect. **Regional:** the perfecto/indefinido line within the three-way split is the central-Peninsular norm (Module 2 §2.7).

**Sources & work (Weeks 5–7).** Week 5: the *imperfecto* unit in **GdU** + **Castro-I**. Weeks 6–7: the *contraste indefinido/imperfecto* unit(s) and the *perfecto/indefinido* contrast; **B&B** (index "imperfect," "preterite vs imperfect") for the aspect discussion and the state-verb shifts; ★ The Spanish Experiment for a plain-English recap. **Own:** the imperfect endings + the three irregulars (*era, iba, veía*); the change-of-state pairs. **Self-test:** re-narrate a graded-reader paragraph aloud, justifying every preterite vs imperfect choice; take a mixed exercise on *saber/conocer/poder/querer* and explain each meaning; given *hoy/ayer/de pequeño* + a verb, select perfecto/indefinido/imperfecto correctly. **This is the block to repeat if anything feels shaky — it gates all narrative reading.**

# Module 5 — El pretérito pluscuamperfecto
### *(Week 9)*

## 5.1 The idea, and why it's short

The pluperfect ("had done") marks a past action **completed before another past reference point** — the "past before the past." Conceptually it's simple, and morphologically it's almost free, because it **reuses the compound architecture and the very participle you learned in Module 2**. Only the tense of *haber* changes: from present (perfect) to **imperfect** (pluperfect).

## 5.2 Form and use

**Imperfect of *haber* + participle:**

| | *haber* (imperfect) | + participle |
|---|---|---|
| yo | había | + hablado |
| tú | habías | |
| él | había | |
| nosotros | habíamos | |
| vosotros | habíais | |
| ellos | habían | |

*había hablado, habías comido, habían vuelto*. Everything from Module 2 carries over unchanged: the two halves are **inseparable**, **clitics precede** *haber* (*ya se habían ido*, *no lo había visto*), the **participle is invariable** (*-o* after *haber*), and the same **irregular participles** apply (*había hecho, dicho, puesto, escrito, roto, abierto*).

**Use:** an event prior to another past moment. *Cuando llegué, ya se habían ido* (they left *before* I arrived). *Nunca había comido paella hasta aquel día.* *Me dijo que había perdido las llaves.* In narrative it steps **back** from the main timeline to fill in what had happened earlier — a flashback marker.

## 5.3 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** *había* + participle is one verb (don't read *había* as merely "there was" — that impersonal sense is *había* used alone, as in *había mucha gente*; with a participle it's the pluperfect auxiliary). Use the agreement diagnostic to tell pluperfect verb (*habían abierto la tienda*) from *estar/ser* + adjective/passive. **Settled and pan-Hispanic** — the pluperfect is uniform across dialects with none of the regional wrinkles of the perfect. *(One literary footnote for recognition only: an older narrative "past anterior," the* pretérito anterior *—* hubo hablado *— survives in formal/archaic prose after* cuando/apenas/en cuanto*; you'll meet it rarely and passively.)*

**Sources & work (Week 9).** The *pluscuamperfecto* unit in **GdU**; **B&B** (index "pluperfect"); drill in **PMP**. **Own:** the imperfect of *haber* (trivial once you know the imperfect) + the same participles. **Self-test:** convert several sentences to show "the past before the past" (*Llegué / ellos ya + salir* → *Cuando llegué, ya habían salido*); read a page and flag each pluperfect as a step back in time.

# Module 6 — El futuro simple y el condicional simple
### *(Weeks 10 & 12)*

These two tenses are twins: they are built on the **same base** (the full infinitive) and share **one set of irregular stems**. Learn the machinery once and you get both.

## 6.1 Morphology — built on the full infinitive

Unusually, the endings attach to the **whole infinitive**, not the stem.

**Future** = infinitive + **-é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án** (all conjugations identical; accents on all but *nosotros*):

| | hablar | comer | vivir |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | hablaré | comeré | viviré |
| tú | hablarás | comerás | vivirás |
| él | hablará | comerá | vivirá |
| nosotros | hablaremos | comeremos | viviremos |
| vosotros | hablaréis | comeréis | viviréis |
| ellos | hablarán | comerán | vivirán |

**Conditional** = infinitive + **-ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían** — i.e. the **imperfect *-ía* endings from Module 4**, now on the full infinitive (*hablaría, comería, viviría*). All three conjugations identical.

**The twelve shared irregular stems** (same stem for **both** tenses — this is the high-leverage fact):

| Type | Stems | Future / Conditional |
|---|---|---|
| Drop the vowel | caber→cabr-, haber→habr-, poder→podr-, querer→querr-, saber→sabr- | cabré/cabría, habré/habría, podré/podría, querré/querría, sabré/sabría |
| Vowel → *d* | poner→pondr-, salir→saldr-, tener→tendr-, valer→valdr-, venir→vendr- | pondré/pondría, saldré/saldría, tendré/tendría, valdré/valdría, vendré/vendría |
| Fully irregular | decir→dir-, hacer→har- | diré/diría, haré/haría |

Twelve stems, two tenses. (Compounds inherit them: *mantener→mantendré*, *deshacer→desharé*.)

## 6.2 Uses

**Future.** (i) **Future events** — though present-for-future is common (Module 1): *Mañana lloverá. El año que viene cambiaré de trabajo.* (ii) **Probability/conjecture in the present** — a use that surprises English speakers and is essential for reading: a future-tense verb often expresses a **present** guess, not a future event. *¿Qué hora es? —Serán las tres* ("It must be about three"). *Estará en casa* ("He's probably home"). *Tendrá unos cuarenta años* ("She's about forty, I'd guess"). (iii) Solemn commands/promises: *No matarás.*

**Conditional.** (i) **Hypothetical "would"** — the main-clause partner of *si*-conditionals (full Type-2 in Week 31): *Yo en tu lugar, no iría. Me encantaría ir.* (ii) **Probability/conjecture in the past** — the past counterpart of the future-probability use: *Serían las tres cuando llegó* ("It must have been about three"). *Tendría veinte años entonces* ("He was probably about twenty"). (iii) **Politeness/softening:** *Me gustaría un café. ¿Podrías ayudarme? Deberías descansar* (advice). (iv) **Future-in-the-past** in reported speech (Week 35): *Dijo que vendría* ("He said he would come").

The two **probability** uses — future for present guesses, conditional for past guesses — are among the highest-value reading skills in this module; watch for a future/conditional verb where the context is plainly *not* about the future.

## 6.3 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** recognise the shared irregular stems (*pondrá, tendría, habrá, dirá, hará*) instantly; and, above all, don't reflexively read every future as "will" or every conditional as "would" — check whether the sense is **conjecture** (*serán las tres* is present, *serían las tres* is past). Distinguish the one-word **conditional** *hablaría* from the compound **conditional perfect** *habría hablado* (Week 46) — the latter has *haber*. **Settled and pan-Hispanic:** the forms and the probability uses. **Overstated/worth noting:** in everyday **speech**, the *ir a* + infinitive periphrasis (*voy a hablar*) and the plain present often replace the synthetic future for actual future time, which is why the synthetic future's **probability** value is proportionally so prominent in what you read and hear.

**Sources & work (Weeks 10 & 12).** Week 10: the *futuro simple* unit in **GdU** + the probability use; **B&B** (index "future tense"). Week 12: the *condicional simple* unit; **B&B** (index "conditional tense"). Drill the twelve stems in **501/PMP** — **once**, for both tenses. **Own:** both ending-sets + the twelve shared stems. **Self-test:** conjugate *tener, hacer, poder, decir, salir, haber* in **both** future and conditional from the shared stem; then translate the four probability sentences (*Serán/Serían las tres; Tendrá/Tendría cuarenta años*) and read a page flagging any future/conditional that is really a guess.

# Module 7 — Las formas progresivas (estar + gerundio)
### *(Week 11)*

## 7.1 The idea

The continuous, *estar* + **gerundio** ("to be doing"), foregrounds an action **actually in progress at a given moment**. Coming after Module 1, the key calibration is restraint: Spanish uses the **simple present** for ongoing action far more than English does, and reserves the continuous for genuine emphasis on *in-progress-right-now*. *Leo un libro* is the neutral "I'm reading a book"; *estoy leyendo* stresses that I'm doing it **at this very moment**.

## 7.2 Gerund formation

**-ar → -ando** (*hablando*); **-er/-ir → -iendo** (*comiendo, viviendo*). Two adjustments, both familiar: **i→y** when the stem ends in a vowel — *leyendo, oyendo, cayendo, trayendo, construyendo*, and *ir → yendo*; and the **-ir stem-changers raise the vowel** e→i / o→u — *pidiendo, durmiendo, sintiendo, muriendo, diciendo, viniendo* (**the same raising as the preterite 3rd person and the imperfect subjunctive** — one pattern, three places).

## 7.3 Uses and placement

**In progress now:** *Estoy escribiendo un correo* (right now). **In the past** — *estaba* + gerundio (was …ing), vividly in-progress and often interchangeable with the plain imperfect: *Estaba duchándome cuando llamaron*. **Across other tenses:** *estuve leyendo* (spent time reading), *he estado trabajando*, *estaré esperando*. **Clitic placement** is flexible: **before *estar*** or **attached to the gerundio** (with an added accent) — *me estoy duchando* = *estoy duchándome*; *lo está haciendo* = *está haciéndolo*.

**Two things Spanish does *not* do with the gerund** (common English-interference errors, useful for reading calibration): it is **not** used for the **future** — English "I'm leaving tomorrow" is *Salgo/Saldré mañana*, never *\*estoy saliendo mañana*; and it is **not** used as a **noun** — English "-ing" nouns become the **infinitive**: *Ver es creer* ("Seeing is believing"), *Me gusta nadar* ("I like swimming").

**Where this feeds:** the aspectual periphrases of Week 43 (*seguir/llevar/ir/venir* + gerundio) build directly on the gerund — *seguir leyendo* ("to keep reading"), *llevar dos años estudiando* ("to have been studying for two years").

## 7.4 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** read *estar* + gerundio as one unit; don't over-translate simple presents into progressives; recognise gerundio-with-attached-clitic (*haciéndolo, duchándome*) as ordinary forms. **Settled and pan-Hispanic**; the main subtlety is the **frequency** difference from English, not the forms.

**Sources & work (Week 11).** The *estar + gerundio* / *perífrasis con gerundio* unit in **GdU**; **B&B** (index "continuous forms," "gerund"). **Own:** gerund formation including *i→y* and the vowel-raising verbs. **Self-test:** form the gerund of *leer, oír, pedir, dormir, decir, construir*; then read a page, mark each *estar* + gerundio, and for each simple present ask whether English would (wrongly) want a progressive.

# Module 8 — El imperativo
### *(Weeks 13–14)*

## 8.1 The idea, and why it's the bridge to Phase 2

Commands. Affirmative and negative, across *tú / vosotros / usted(es) / nosotros*. The reason this module closes Phase 1 is that **most of the imperative already *is* the subjunctive**: every **negative** command, and **all** the *usted*, *ustedes*, and *nosotros* commands, use present-subjunctive forms. So without yet calling it "the subjunctive," you get your **first working exposure** to those forms — a running start into Week 17.

## 8.2 Affirmative commands *(Week 13)*

- **tú** = the **3rd-person singular present indicative**: *habla, come, vive*. Eight irregulars to memorise: **di** (decir), **haz** (hacer), **ve** (ir), **pon** (poner), **sal** (salir), **sé** (ser), **ten** (tener), **ven** (venir).
- **vosotros** = infinitive with final **-r → -d**: *hablad, comed, vivid* (no exceptions; with a reflexive the *-d* drops before *os*: *sentaos*, and irregularly *idos*).
- **usted / ustedes** = **present subjunctive**: *hable / hablen, coma / coman, viva / vivan*.
- **nosotros** ("let's …") = **present subjunctive**: *hablemos, comamos, vivamos* (for "let's go," *vamos*).

## 8.3 Negative commands *(Week 14)*

**All** negative commands use the **present subjunctive**, in every person:

| | affirmative | negative (= subjunctive) |
|---|---|---|
| tú | habla | **no hables** |
| vosotros | hablad | **no habléis** |
| usted | hable | **no hable** |
| ustedes | hablen | **no hablen** |
| nosotros | hablemos | **no hablemos** |

Note that *tú* and *vosotros* are exactly where affirmative and negative **diverge** (*come* → *no comas*; *comed* → *no comáis*), because the affirmative *tú/vosotros* forms are *not* subjunctive but the negatives are.

## 8.4 Clitic placement — the mirror image

Pronouns flip position with polarity, a rule you'll reuse for compound verbs and beyond:

- **Affirmative → attached to the end**, with an added written accent to keep the stress: *dímelo, háblame, siéntate, levantaos, dígamelo*.
- **Negative → before the verb**, separate: *no me lo digas, no te sientes, no me hables*.

## 8.5 Reading & calibration

**Reading:** recognise the **eight short irregular *tú* forms** (*di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven*) — they're tiny and easy to misread (*ve* is both "go!" and "see!"/3sg of *ver*; context decides); and read clitic-laden affirmatives (*dímelo, tráemelo*) as ordinary commands rather than unfamiliar words. **Settled**, with the usual **regional** note: the *vosotros* command (*hablad*) is Peninsular; Latin America uses the *ustedes* form for plural commands. **Where this feeds:** you have now met the present subjunctive in four of its forms — Week 17 will name it, systematise it, and extend it far beyond commands.

**Sources & work (Weeks 13–14).** Week 13: the *imperativo afirmativo* unit in **GdU** + clitic attachment. Week 14: the *imperativo negativo* unit + pronoun position; **B&B** (index "imperative"). **Own:** the eight irregular *tú* affirmatives; the *-d* *vosotros* rule; the subjunctive-based negative/*usted*/*nosotros* forms; the affirmative-vs-negative clitic flip. **Self-test:** give all four affirmative + negative commands of *hacer, poner, decir* with a clitic (*hazlo / no lo hagas*, etc.); read a page and identify every command, including the short irregulars.
