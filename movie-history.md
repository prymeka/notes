# A History of Cinema — Curriculum & Viewing Canon
### From the origins (1890s) to the present, blending three threads: film as an **art form**, as an **industry & technology**, and as a **cultural/social force**. Reading paired with an essential film canon per era. Global in reach, weighted to the US and Europe.

*The films are the primary texts. Everything else — the histories, the theory, the criticism — exists to help you see them better. So this is built as ten chronological units; in each, you **watch** a tight canon and **read** the texts keyed to that era, while a short introduction weaves the three threads together. Meta-sections at the end give the reading spine, a theory throughline, a compressed fast-track, a calibration section, and a free-resources appendix.*

---

## How to use this

Go chronologically — film history is genuinely cumulative (the French New Wave is unintelligible without the studio system it rebelled against; New Hollywood without the New Wave). For each unit: read the relevant chapter of your **spine history** first for orientation, watch the canon, then read the **keyed texts** (the theory or criticism that era produced or provoked). Budget roughly one unit every 2–4 weeks for a serious pace; the fast-track at the end compresses it.

Two habits do most of the work: **watch actively** (what's the shot doing? why cut here? — this is where the earlier composition/framing material pays off), and **read the theory beside the film it responds to**, not in the abstract (Eisenstein while watching *Potemkin*; Bazin while watching *Bicycle Thieves*).

A note on editions and currency: standard bibliographic details are given, but confirm the latest edition of the textbooks (they're revised periodically), and treat everything past roughly 2020 as provisional — the contemporary canon and its scholarship are still forming, and my information thins out toward the present.

## The spine (read across the whole curriculum)

**Kristin Thompson & David Bordwell, *Film History: An Introduction*** — McGraw-Hill (4th ed., 2018/2019). **The primary spine.** The standard global survey, and the one that best fits this brief because it deliberately integrates aesthetics, technology, and industry. Read its chapters alongside each unit below.

**Geoffrey Nowell-Smith (ed.), *The Oxford History of World Cinema*** — Oxford University Press, 1996. **Reference.** A comprehensive, essay-based global history — the book to consult when you want depth on a national cinema or period the spine treats briefly.

**Leo Braudy & Marshall Cohen (eds.), *Film Theory and Criticism: Introductory Readings*** — Oxford University Press (8th ed., 2016; check for a newer one). **The primary-source theory anthology.** Collects the actual foundational essays (Münsterberg, Arnheim, Eisenstein, Bazin, Sarris, Kael, Mulvey, and on) in one volume. This is where you read the theory as primary text rather than paraphrase.

**Mark Cousins, *The Story of Film*** — Pavilion Books, 2004 (updated editions since). **The accessible narrative + viewing companion.** An idiosyncratic, global, auteur-loving narrative; its 15-part documentary version, ***The Story of Film: An Odyssey*** (2011), is the single best way to *see* the connective tissue of world film history and doubles as a curated viewing guide. ● Documentary is a superb spine on its own.

**Robert Sklar, *Movie-Made America: A Cultural History of American Movies*** — Random House, 1975 (revised 1994). **The US cultural thread.** The keystone for the "film and society" dimension of American cinema; read across the US-focused units.

*(Alternatives/supplements: David A. Cook, *A History of Narrative Film* (Norton) and Gerald Mast & Bruce Kawin, *A Short History of the Movies* are both solid single-volume surveys if you want a second voice.)*

## Unit 1 — Origins & Early Cinema (c. 1878–1905)

**What changed.** Cinema is invented more or less simultaneously as a *technology* (Edison's Kinetoscope, 1891; the Lumières' Cinématographe and first paid public projection, 1895), an *attraction* (the "cinema of attractions" — spectacle and novelty before storytelling), and a *business* (Edison's patent wars; the nickelodeon boom that made moviegoing a mass habit). Méliès turns the camera toward fantasy and trick effects; Porter toward narrative editing.

**Watch.**
- Lumière brothers, *actualités* — *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*, *Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat* (1895–96)
- Georges Méliès, *A Trip to the Moon / Le Voyage dans la Lune* (1902, France) — fantasy, effects, the first "movie magic"
- Edwin S. Porter, *The Great Train Robbery* (1903, US) — narrative and cross-cutting
- *(Context)* Eadweard Muybridge, *The Horse in Motion* (1878) — the proto-cinema motion study

**Read.**
- Spine: Thompson & Bordwell, early chapters.
- Charles Musser, *The Emergence of Cinema: The American Screen to 1907* (Scribner's, 1990) — the authoritative account of the medium's first years.
- Tom Gunning, "The Cinema of Attractions: Early Film, Its Spectator and the Avant-Garde" (*Wide Angle*, 1986) — the essay that reframed how we understand early cinema. **⚠** and note: the famous story that audiences *fled* the Lumière train is largely myth — good early lesson in distrusting cinema's tidy legends.

---

## Unit 2 — The Silent Era: Narrative Consolidation & the Rise of Hollywood (c. 1905–1927)

**What changed.** Film grows from shorts into features; the "grammar" of continuity editing is codified (largely credited to Griffith, though the reality is more diffuse); Hollywood becomes the industrial center as production moves west; the **star system** and the first vertically integrated studios emerge. Silent comedy reaches an artistic peak that has never been surpassed.

**Watch.**
- D.W. Griffith, *The Birth of a Nation* (1915) **⚠ Calibration** — technically monumental *and* virulently racist; it helped revive the Ku Klux Klan. Study it as a historical object, with that inseparable from any account of its "importance." *Intolerance* (1916) is the ambitious, non-toxic companion.
- Buster Keaton, *The General* (1926) and *Sherlock Jr.* (1924) — the summit of visual/physical invention
- Charlie Chaplin, *The Gold Rush* (1925) *(and, just after, *City Lights*, 1931)*
- Harold Lloyd, *Safety Last!* (1923)

**Read.**
- Spine: Thompson & Bordwell.
- Kevin Brownlow, *The Parade's Gone By...* (Knopf, 1968) — the great, humane oral history of the silent era; makes the period live.

---

## Unit 3 — European Silent Avant-Gardes: Film Becomes an Art (c. 1919–1930)

**What changed.** In Germany, the USSR, and France, cinema is claimed as a serious *art form* with its own theory. This is the richest aesthetic period of the silent age and — per your Europe weighting — the heart of the early curriculum. Two rival theories of what cinema *is* are born here and still structure the whole field: **montage** (meaning is created by the collision of shots) versus, later, **realism** (meaning is discovered in the unbroken image).

**Watch.**
- *German Expressionism:* Robert Wiene, *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* (1920); F.W. Murnau, *Nosferatu* (1922) and *The Last Laugh* (1924); Fritz Lang, *Metropolis* (1927) *(and *M*, 1931, at the sound threshold)*
- *Soviet Montage:* Sergei Eisenstein, *Battleship Potemkin* (1925); Dziga Vertov, *Man with a Movie Camera* (1929) — the dazzling self-reflexive city-symphony documentary
- *French/Surrealist avant-garde:* Luis Buñuel & Salvador Dalí, *Un Chien Andalou* (1929); Abel Gance, *Napoléon* (1927)
- *Nordic:* Carl Th. Dreyer, *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928) — a peak of the silent art film

**Read.**
- Sergei Eisenstein, *Film Form* (1949) and *The Film Sense* (1942), ed./trans. Jay Leyda — the montage theory, from its author. Connects directly to the editing thread (Murch et al.) in the filmmaking material.
- Lotte H. Eisner, *The Haunted Screen: Expressionism in the German Cinema* (UC Press, 1969) — the standard on German Expressionism.
- Siegfried Kracauer, *From Caligari to Hitler: A Psychological History of the German Film* (Princeton UP, 1947) **⚠ Calibration** — the landmark cultural reading (Weimar cinema as premonition of Nazism); brilliant and methodologically contested. A perfect specimen of the "film as social symptom" approach and its limits.

---

## Unit 4 — Sound & the Classical Hollywood Studio System (c. 1927–1945)

**What changed.** *The Jazz Singer* (1927) triggers the wrenching, expensive conversion to sound (later dramatized in *Singin' in the Rain*). The **studio system** reaches its height: vertical integration, the "genius of the system," genre factories, the star system — and the **Production (Hays) Code** (enforced from 1934), which shapes what American films could say for two decades (the culture/industry thread at its clearest). Meanwhile Europe produces its own sound-era masterpieces.

**Watch.**
- *Hollywood:* Orson Welles, *Citizen Kane* (1941) — perennial "greatest film," a compendium of technique; John Ford, *Stagecoach* (1939); Howard Hawks, *Bringing Up Baby* (1938); Michael Curtiz, *Casablanca* (1942); *(genre)* James Whale, *Frankenstein* (1931); Billy Wilder, *Double Indemnity* (1944) — film noir crystallizing
- *France (Poetic Realism):* Jean Renoir, *The Rules of the Game / La Règle du jeu* (1939) and *Grand Illusion* (1937); Marcel Carné, *Children of Paradise* (1945)
- *Japan (pre-war):* Yasujirō Ozu, *I Was Born, But…* (1932)

**Read.**
- Thomas Schatz, *The Genius of the System: Hollywood Filmmaking in the Studio Era* (Pantheon, 1988) — the industry-thread keystone: how the studios actually worked.
- David Bordwell, Janet Staiger & Kristin Thompson, *The Classical Hollywood Cinema: Film Style and Mode of Production to 1960* (Columbia UP, 1985) — the definitive scholarly account of how Hollywood's *style* and *industrial organization* were two sides of one system. Dense but exactly on your "blend" brief.
- Sklar, *Movie-Made America* (the Code, the Depression-era audience).

---

## Unit 5 — Neorealism & the Postwar Break (c. 1945–1952)

**What changed.** Out of the rubble of war, **Italian Neorealism** rejects studio artifice for real locations, non-professional actors, and social reality — and becomes the most influential movement of the century, seeding new cinemas in France, India, Latin America, and beyond. In the US, the postwar years bring the **HUAC blacklist** (the industry's political trauma) and the **1948 *Paramount* antitrust ruling** that broke up vertical integration and began the studio system's long decline — while **television** emerges as the existential threat.

**Watch.**
- *Italian Neorealism:* Roberto Rossellini, *Rome, Open City* (1945); Vittorio De Sica, *Bicycle Thieves* (1948) and *Umberto D.* (1952)
- *US:* the film noir peak — e.g., Jacques Tourneur, *Out of the Past* (1947); Carol Reed (UK), *The Third Man* (1949)

**Read.**
- **André Bazin, *What Is Cinema?* Vols. I & II** (trans. Hugh Gray, UC Press, 1967/1971; newer trans. by Timothy Barnard) — the great critic of realism, the long take, and deep focus; the theoretical *counterweight* to Eisenstein and the intellectual father of the coming New Wave. Essential.
- Spine chapters on the blacklist and the *Paramount* decree (Thompson & Bordwell; Sklar).

---

## Unit 6 — The 1950s: Widescreen, Color & Global Golden Ages (c. 1950–1959)

**What changed.** Hollywood answers television with *spectacle* — CinemaScope, Technicolor, epics, 3-D — and with **Method acting** (Brando) bringing a new psychological realism. Simultaneously, the two greatest non-Western art cinemas arrive on the world stage: **Japan's golden age** and **India's Satyajit Ray**. And in Paris, the *Cahiers du cinéma* critics formulate **auteur theory**, setting the fuse for the New Wave.

**Watch.**
- *Japan:* Akira Kurosawa, *Rashomon* (1950 — the film that opened Western eyes to Japanese cinema) and *Seven Samurai* (1954); Yasujirō Ozu, *Tokyo Story* (1953); Kenji Mizoguchi, *Ugetsu* (1953)
- *India:* Satyajit Ray, the Apu Trilogy — *Pather Panchali* (1955), *Aparajito* (1956), *The World of Apu* (1959)
- *Sweden:* Ingmar Bergman, *The Seventh Seal* (1957)
- *US:* Alfred Hitchcock, *Vertigo* (1958 — often now ranked the greatest film); John Ford, *The Searchers* (1956); Douglas Sirk, *All That Heaven Allows* (1955)

**Read.**
- Andrew Sarris, *The American Cinema: Directors and Directions 1929–1968* (Dutton, 1968) and his essay "Notes on the Auteur Theory in 1962" — auteur theory's American formulation.
- Pauline Kael, "Circles and Squares" (1963, in *I Lost It at the Movies*) **⚠** — the great rebuttal. Read the two together: the **Sarris–Kael auteur debate** is the field's foundational argument about whether the director is "the author," and it directly complicates the collaborative reality stressed in the filmmaking material.

---

## Unit 7 — The New Waves & International Art Cinema (c. 1958–1968)

**What changed.** A worldwide explosion of young, self-conscious, cinephile filmmaking — jump cuts, location shooting, personal voice, and rule-breaking as a program. This is the densest aesthetic period after the 1920s and, again, Europe-heavy. In parallel, the **documentary revolution** (Direct Cinema / cinéma vérité) and **American independent cinema** (Cassavetes) are born.

**Watch.**
- *French New Wave:* Jean-Luc Godard, *Breathless* (1960); François Truffaut, *The 400 Blows* (1959) and *Jules and Jim* (1962); Agnès Varda, *Cléo from 5 to 7* (1962); *(Left Bank)* Alain Resnais, *Hiroshima mon amour* (1959); Chris Marker, *La Jetée* (1962)
- *Italian modernism:* Federico Fellini, *8½* (1963 — the great film about filmmaking); Michelangelo Antonioni, *L'Avventura* (1960)
- *Elsewhere in Europe:* Andrzej Wajda (Poland), *Ashes and Diamonds* (1958); Miloš Forman (Czech New Wave), *Loves of a Blonde* (1965); Věra Chytilová, *Daisies* (1966); Andrei Tarkovsky (USSR), *Andrei Rublev* (1966)
- *US independent / documentary:* John Cassavetes, *Shadows* (1959); D.A. Pennebaker, *Dont Look Back* (1967)
- *Japan:* Masaki Kobayashi, *Harakiri* (1962)

**Read.**
- Spine chapters on the New Waves (Thompson & Bordwell; Nowell-Smith on the national cinemas).
- David Bordwell, "The Art Cinema as a Mode of Film Practice" (essay, 1979) — the best short account of what "art cinema" *is* as a distinct storytelling mode.

---

## Unit 8 — New Hollywood: The American Renaissance (c. 1967–1980)

**What changed.** The studio system finally collapses; the Production Code is replaced by the **MPAA ratings system** (1968); and a film-school-educated generation ("the movie brats"), high on the European New Waves, briefly seizes creative control of Hollywood. All three threads converge: adventurous *art*, an industry *in crisis and transition*, and films saturated with the *culture* of Vietnam and Watergate. Then *Jaws* (1975) and *Star Wars* (1977) invent the summer blockbuster and end the party.

**Watch.**
- Francis Ford Coppola, *The Godfather* (1972) and *Part II* (1974); *The Conversation* (1974)
- Martin Scorsese, *Taxi Driver* (1976) *(and *Raging Bull*, 1980)*
- Stanley Kubrick, *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968); Robert Altman, *Nashville* (1975); Roman Polanski, *Chinatown* (1974); Sidney Lumet, *Network* (1976); Terrence Malick, *Days of Heaven* (1978)
- *The turn:* Steven Spielberg, *Jaws* (1975); George Lucas, *Star Wars* (1977)

**Read.**
- Peter Biskind, *Easy Riders, Raging Bulls* (Simon & Schuster, 1998) **⚠ Calibration** — the definitive, gossipy narrative of the era; hugely readable, journalistically loose, so enjoy it as story more than gospel.
- Robin Wood, *Hollywood from Vietnam to Reagan* (Columbia UP, 1986) — the serious political/critical reading of the same period.
- **Laura Mulvey, "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (*Screen*, 1975)** — the landmark feminist/psychoanalytic essay ("the male gaze") that reshaped film theory for a generation. Read it here (it's *of* this moment) and let it recolor everything you've watched so far.

## Unit 9 — Blockbusters, New Independents & Global New Waves (c. 1980–1999)

**What changed.** Industrially: the **high-concept blockbuster**, franchises, home video (VHS) upending revenue, media conglomeration, and the multiplex. Aesthetically, two counter-currents thrive — a booming **American independent** scene (Sundance/Miramax "indiewood") and a wave of major **global new cinemas**. And a quiet revolution begins: **CGI** (*Jurassic Park*, 1993) and the first fully computer-animated feature (*Toy Story*, 1995) start the digital transition.

**Watch.**
- *US independents:* Spike Lee, *Do the Right Thing* (1989 — landmark of Black American cinema); Joel & Ethan Coen, *Fargo* (1996); Quentin Tarantino, *Pulp Fiction* (1994); Steven Soderbergh, *sex, lies, and videotape* (1989)
- *New German Cinema* (its tail): Wim Wenders, *Wings of Desire* (1987); Werner Herzog, *Aguirre, the Wrath of God* (1972) and *Fitzcarraldo* (1982)
- *Global new waves:* Wong Kar-wai (Hong Kong), *Chungking Express* (1994); Abbas Kiarostami (Iran), *Close-Up* (1990); Zhang Yimou (China, Fifth Generation), *Raise the Red Lantern* (1991); Edward Yang (Taiwan), *Yi Yi* (2000); Lars von Trier & the **Dogme 95** movement, *Breaking the Waves* (1996)

**Read.**
- Peter Biskind, *Down and Dirty Pictures: Miramax, Sundance, and the Rise of Independent Film* (Simon & Schuster, 2004) — the indie-boom industry story.
- Nowell-Smith / spine chapters on the global new waves and the digital turn.

---

## Unit 10 — The Digital Age & Contemporary Cinema (c. 2000–present)

**What changed.** The medium goes fully **digital** — capture (Arri Alexa, RED), projection (DCP), and effects — and film stock nearly dies. Industrially, the **franchise/IP era** (the Marvel Cinematic Universe from 2008) dominates theaters while the mid-budget adult drama migrates elsewhere, and the **streaming revolution** (Netflix originals, collapsing theatrical windows, accelerated by the 2020 pandemic) rewrites the whole business — the same upheaval discussed in the feature-film study map. Culturally: globalization of the audience, reckonings over representation (#OscarsSoWhite) and abuse (#MeToo/Weinstein, 2017), and the end of monoculture. Art cinema, meanwhile, is arguably in a golden age of global auteurs.

**Watch** *(a starting canon — the recent end is provisional and personal):*
- Paul Thomas Anderson, *There Will Be Blood* (2007); the Coens, *No Country for Old Men* (2007)
- Wong Kar-wai, *In the Mood for Love* (2000); Michael Haneke, *Amour* (2012); Asghar Farhadi (Iran), *A Separation* (2011)
- Bong Joon-ho (Korea), *Parasite* (2019 — first non-English Best Picture); Park Chan-wook, *Oldboy* (2003); Hirokazu Kore-eda (Japan), *Shoplifters* (2018)
- Barry Jenkins, *Moonlight* (2016); Jordan Peele, *Get Out* (2017); Céline Sciamma, *Portrait of a Lady on Fire* (2019); Alfonso Cuarón, *Roma* (2018); Jonathan Glazer, *The Zone of Interest* (2023); Christopher Nolan, *Oppenheimer* (2023)

**Read.**
- The updated editions of Thompson & Bordwell (their later chapters) and Bordwell/Thompson's blog for the digital transition.
- Current criticism and trade press for the streaming era (and cross-reference the market analysis in the feature-film document). **⚠** No stable canon exists yet for this period — hold your judgments loosely; much of what looks major now will be reassessed.

## The theory throughline (in one glance)

If you read only the primary-source spine (Braudy & Cohen), track this argument as it develops: **Eisenstein** (meaning from montage) ⟷ **Bazin** (meaning from the unbroken real) → **Sarris vs. Kael** (is the director the author?) → **Mulvey** and 1970s *Screen* theory (the politics of the gaze; the apparatus) → later **feminist, queer, and postcolonial** critiques (e.g., bell hooks's "the oppositional gaze") that widen the canon. That arc *is* the intellectual history of taking film seriously.

## The minimum viable fast-track

If you want the shape of the whole history quickly:
1. **One spine, watched not just read:** Mark Cousins's *The Story of Film: An Odyssey* (documentary) — it narrates the connective tissue and shows you the clips. Pair with the relevant chapters of Thompson & Bordwell for rigor.
2. **A ~25-film essential canon** hitting each unit's landmark: *A Trip to the Moon* · *The General* · *Metropolis* · *Battleship Potemkin* · *The Passion of Joan of Arc* · *Citizen Kane* · *The Rules of the Game* · *Bicycle Thieves* · *Tokyo Story* · *Seven Samurai* · *Pather Panchali* · *The Seventh Seal* · *Vertigo* · *Breathless* · *8½* · *The 400 Blows* · *2001: A Space Odyssey* · *The Godfather Part II* · *Taxi Driver* · *Do the Right Thing* · *Pulp Fiction* · *In the Mood for Love* · *A Separation* · *Parasite*.
3. **Four theory essays** (all in Braudy & Cohen): Eisenstein on montage · Bazin on realism · Sarris + Kael on the auteur · Mulvey on the gaze.
4. **A viewing backbone:** the BFI *Sight and Sound* "Greatest Films of All Time" poll (2022) as a checklist to fill in over years. ● Free.

## Calibration — what's settled, what's contested, what's myth

**Genuinely settled (broad consensus):** the periodization and the identification of the major *movements* and their landmark works. That German Expressionism, Soviet Montage, Neorealism, the New Waves, and New Hollywood happened, roughly when and where the standard account says, and that their canonical films are canonical, is stable knowledge you can rely on.

**Contested (know the debate):**
- **The auteur framing.** The standard history is organized around great directors; this overstates the director-as-sole-author and undervalues the collaborative, industrial, below-the-line reality — the same point the filmmaking material makes. Sarris vs. Kael is where this fight lives.
- **The Euro-American centering.** Even a US/Europe-weighted history (like this one) should be held with the awareness that it marginalizes vast popular and national cinemas (Indian popular film, Nigerian "Nollywood," Egyptian, Hong Kong genre film) and the contributions of women, whose erasure from the standard canon is itself a historiographical problem — visible in the 2022 *Sight and Sound* poll crowning Chantal Akerman's *Jeanne Dielman* (1975), a deliberate corrective.
- **"Decline" and "death of cinema" narratives** (e.g., "*Star Wars* killed New Hollywood," "streaming killed the movies") — real turning points, but the tidy causal stories oversimplify.

**Myth (don't repeat it):**
- Audiences did **not** stampede from the Lumière train (1896) — the story is embroidered.
- **"Firsts" are almost always contestable** — first film, first narrative, first close-up, first sound film. The record is messier than the textbook shorthand; be skeptical of any clean "the first ___."
- Kracauer's *Caligari*-to-Hitler thesis reads Weimar cinema backward from the Nazi outcome — influential, but treat it as an interpretive argument, not established fact.
- **The "greatness" of *The Birth of a Nation* cannot be cleanly separated from its racism and real-world harm.** Any account that celebrates its technique without that is telling half the story.

## Free & public-domain appendix

**Watch for free (public domain / library access):**
- Much of the **silent and early canon is public domain** — Lumière and Méliès films, early Chaplin, *Nosferatu*, *Battleship Potemkin*, *Metropolis* (restoration-dependent), and more — freely available via the **Internet Archive** and the **Library of Congress** (whose **National Film Registry** is itself a curated viewing list). ●
- **Kanopy** and **Hoopla** — free with many public/university library cards; strong on classics and world cinema. ●
- *(Paid but the gold standard for this curriculum: the **Criterion Channel** for curated classics and world cinema; **Mubi** for art house.)*

**Read / listen for free:**
- The **BFI *Sight and Sound* "Greatest Films" poll** (decennial; 2022 latest) — the canonical viewing backbone. ●
- **David Bordwell & Kristin Thompson's blog, "Observations on Film Art"** (and Bordwell's free online books) — rigorous, generous, free. ●
- **Karina Longworth, *You Must Remember This*** (podcast) — superb narrative Hollywood cultural history; ideal for the "film and society" thread. ●
- Video essayists — **Every Frame a Painting**, **The Nerdwriter**, **CinemaTyler** — for close analysis of the canonical films. ●

## How this connects to your other film work

This curriculum is the **context and taste-building** leg that the earlier feature-film study map flagged as essential; watching the canon actively is also the best possible training ground for the **composition/framing** reading list (analyze how *Citizen Kane*, *The Rules of the Game*, or *In the Mood for Love* build a frame). Read together: the study map is *how films are made*, the composition list is *how the image is built*, and this is *where it all came from and why it matters*.

## Caveats
- **Confirm current editions** of the textbooks (Thompson & Bordwell, Braudy & Cohen especially are revised periodically).
- **The contemporary unit is provisional.** The canon and scholarship for roughly the last decade aren't settled, and my information thins toward the present — treat Unit 10's film list as a starting point to be updated, not a verdict.
- **This is a map; the films are the territory.** Reading history is no substitute for watching — one carefully watched film per unit teaches more than a chapter about it. Watch first, read second, and let the two argue.
